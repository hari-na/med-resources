Short case 19 · General Surgery

Undescended Testis

The whole case turns on one differential, the retractile testis, and one piece of modern evidence that contradicts what most students are taught: orchidopexy does not appear to undo the cancer risk.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1Definitions, which must be exact

Incomplete descent
B&L: "Incomplete descent of the testis, also known as cryptorchidism, occurs when one or both testes are arrested at some point in the normal path to the scrotum. An ectopic testis is a testis that is abnormally placed outside this path."B&L 86 · p.1558
The distinctionUndescended is on the path but short of the scrotum. Ectopic is off the path.
SRB's vocabulary, which is finer and often examined
Undescended testis: arrest of descent somewhere along its pathway to the scrotum. Cryptorchidism: SRB reserves this for bilateral undescended testis, meaning hidden testis. Anorchism: complete agenesis of the testis, and the two can be differentiated by an HCG test.SRB 26 · p.1065
And the umbrella term"Maldescended testis is one that cannot be made to touch the bottom of the scrotum. It can be undescended testis, cryptorchidism, ectopic testis or anorchism."SRB 26 · p.1065 That is the cleanest definition in any of the three books: it cannot be brought to the bottom of the scrotum.
One moreVanishing testis: the testis develops but disappears before birth, probably from prenatal torsion.SRB 26 · p.1065
Where an ectopic testis goes
B&L: "an ectopic testis is one that has ended up away from the normal path of descent. The commonest site is the superficial inguinal pouch, just inferior and medial to the superficial inguinal ring."B&L 86 · p.1559 The other sites are femoral, perineal, at the root of the penis and crossed to the opposite scrotum.
The incidence figures, which track the natural history B&L: "About 3% of full-term and 30% of premature male infants are born with one or both testes undescended. About two-thirds of these reach the scrotum during the first 3 months of life, but full descent after that is uncommon. The incidence of testicular maldescent at the age of 1 year is around 1%."B&L 86 · p.1558

Three per cent at term, thirty per cent if premature, one per cent at a year. Those three numbers tell you when to stop waiting for nature: most that are going to descend do so in the first three months, and almost none after a year. They do not tell you when to operate, which is a separate question the two books answer differently; see Timing below.

And B&L notes the practical problem: "The condition is sometimes missed in the neonatal period and only discovered later."B&L 86 · p.1558

2The differential that decides everything: retractile testis

Examine the boy relaxed, in a warm room, supine. Can you coax the testis into the scrotum, and does it stay? Why that setupB&L: "When assessing a child with suspected testicular maldescent, it is helpful to have the boy as relaxed as possible in a warm room, usually in a supine position."B&L 86 · p.1559 A cold room and a nervous child manufacture the diagnosis.
The differential"The important differential diagnosis is the so-called 'retractile testis'. During childhood the testes are mobile and the cremasteric reflex is active, so that, in some boys, any stimulation of the skin of the scrotum or thigh caus"es the testis to retract to the groin.B&L 86 · p.1559
The sign that separates themA retractile testis can be coaxed into the scrotum and will stay there, at least briefly. A truly undescended testis cannot be brought to the bottom of the scrotum.SRB 26 · p.1065
And the second sign, which is visualB&L: in bilateral undescended testes there is an underdeveloped scrotum, whereas "in cases of retractile testis, the scrotum is relatively well developed".B&L 86 · p.1559 The scrotum tells you whether a testis has ever lived in it.
TechniqueStart at the anterior superior iliac spine and sweep downwards and medially along the inguinal canal with a flat hand, milking the testis towards the scrotum, and hold it there with the other hand. Warm hands, and no soap.

3Why it matters: four consequences

1. Infertility, with the figures
B&L: "Men with undescended testes may have reduced fertility, even after orchidopexy." "The infertility rate for unilateral cases is not believed to be very different from the general population." "The fertility reduction after orchidopexy for bilateral cryptorchidism is about 38%." "Patients with bilateral undescended testes who receive orchidopexies as adults are almost always infertile and azoospermic; but there are reports of pregnancies achieved through sperm retrieval and assisted reproduction in this group."B&L 86 · p.1559
SoUnilateral: fertility much as the general population. Bilateral, operated in childhood: about 38% reduction. Bilateral, operated as an adult: almost always azoospermic. That gradient is the argument for early surgery, and B&L says so: "The recommendation for early surgery is due" to the histological change.B&L 86 · p.1559
2. Malignancy, and here the evidence surprises people
See the conflict box below.
3. Hernia
B&L: "Around 90% of boys with an undescended testis have a patent processus vaginalis although the incidence of a clinically apparent hernia is much lower."B&L 86 · p.1559 So the processus is almost always patent and must be dealt with at orchidopexy.
4. Torsion
B&L: "The undescended testis is more prone to testicular torsion, largely as a consequence of a devel"opmental abnormality.B&L 86 · p.1559 So an undescended testis that becomes acutely painful is a torsion until proved otherwise, and an impalpable testis with groin pain is a surgical emergency.
And the associations to screen for
SRB: cryptorchidism may be associated with Klinefelter's syndrome, hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism, Prune-Belly syndrome, renal agenesis, bladder exstrophy, gastroschisis, horseshoe kidney and anorectal malformations.SRB 26 · p.1065 Bilateral impalpable testes in a newborn is a reason to think about disorders of sex development and to check the karyotype, not simply to book an operation.
Does orchidopexy reduce the risk of testicular cancer? Both books say it probably does not, which contradicts what is commonly taught; B&L 86 p.1559 adds the figures that qualify it
SRB
Plainly: "Orchidopexy may not alter the chances or risk of developing carcinoma of testis. But it makes easy observation and evaluation by ultrasound to detect the possible changes early."SRB 26 · p.1067 So the benefit it claims is surveillance, not prevention.
Bailey & Love 28e, with the numbers
Agrees, and is more precise about what timing does and does not change: "Overall, the risk of testicular cancer if orchidopexy is done before puberty is around two to three times that of the general population. It is five to six times higher when orchidopexy is done after puberty. The risk of cancer does not seem to be different when orchidopexy is done early in infancy compared with later in childhood."B&L 86 · p.1559
And the histology changes with it: "The most common type of testicular cancer in untreated undescended testes is seminoma", with a peak age range of 15 to 45 years; whereas "after orchidopexy, seminomas represent only 30% of testicular tumours in previously undescended testes". Its conclusion is about surveillance, not cure: "It is treatable if caught early, so boys who had an orchidopexy as infants should be taught testicular self-examination."B&L 86 · p.1559
The two books agree, and together they contradict the common teaching that early orchidopexy prevents cancer. Say it carefully, because the nuance is the whole point.

Answer: "Orchidopexy does not appear to remove the cancer risk. The risk is about two to three times the general population if the operation is done before puberty, and five to six times if after puberty, so operating before puberty matters. But within childhood, the timing does not seem to change the cancer risk: doing it in infancy is no better than later in childhood for that particular outcome. What early surgery does improve is fertility, and what orchidopexy always achieves is a testis in a palpable position, so that a tumour can be detected early. For that reason boys who have had an orchidopexy should be taught testicular self-examination."B&L 86 · p.1559SRB 26 · p.1067

Why this is the better answer: it separates the two reasons for operating early. Fertility is time-sensitive within childhood; cancer risk is sensitive to puberty but not to the year within childhood. A candidate who says "we operate early to prevent cancer" is giving the answer both books have abandoned.

4Management

Timing, and the two questions students run together
When you stop waiting, and when you operate, are different ages.
When you stop waiting: three monthsB&L: "two-thirds of these reach the scrotum during the first 3 months of life, but full descent after that is uncommon."B&L 86 · p.1558 Its paediatric chapter turns that into a follow-up plan: boys "should be examined at birth and at 6 weeks; if a UDT is found, they should be seen at 3 months since a testis is unlikely to descend after this time."B&L 17 · p.257 Three months is the age at which the diagnosis is settled, not the age at which the child goes to theatre.
When you operate: the books differ, and by two years B&L: "orchidopexy is usually performed between 6 and 18 months of age in an attempt to prevent the consequences described earlier. For premature babies, corrected age is used to determine" the timing.B&L 86 · p.1560 SRB: "treatment: always surgery. Surgery is done between two and four years of age."SRB 26 · p.1067
The argument for not delayingHistological, and about fertility: the higher the testis, the greater the degree of histological change,B&L 86 · p.1559 and B&L completes the reasoning it opens: "the recommendation for early surgery is due to degeneration of spermatogenic tissue and reduced spermatogonia counts after the second year of life."B&L 86 · p.1559 That last clause is why the age matters at all, and it is also why B&L's window is the earlier one.
Orchidopexy for a palpable testis
Mobilise the cord, divide the patent processus vaginalis, which is present in about 90%, and place the testis in a subdartos pouch. SRB: the undescended testis is separated and placed in the scrotal pouch, and the laparoscopic approach has become a popular and accepted method.SRB 26 · p.1067
For an impalpable testis
B&L: "For non-palpable testes under anaesthesia, diagnostic laparoscopy is recommended." If a testis is found, the options are:
1. Laparoscopic orchidopexy preserving the vessels, in which "the testis is dissected off a triangular pedicle containing the testicular vessels and the vas".
2. Laparoscopic one-stage Fowler-Stephens orchidopexy, dividing the testicular vessels and relying on the collateral supply through the vasal and cremasteric vessels.B&L 86 · p.1560
And the two-stage versionThe vessels are clipped at the first operation and the testis brought down at a second, six months later, which is B&L's stated interval, to allow the collateral supply to develop fully.B&L 86 · p.1560 Bringing it down sooner risks atrophy.
When to remove it instead
B&L: orchidectomy "if the incompletely descended testis is atrophic and/or there is a suspicion of malignancy, particularly in the postpubertal boy if the other testis is normal".B&L 86 · p.1560 SRB: orchidectomy is considered if there is atrophy, torsion, or inability to bring down an abdominal testis.SRB 26 · p.1067
The logicIn a postpubertal boy with a normal contralateral testis, an atrophic undescended testis contributes nothing to fertility and carries a cancer risk five to six times normal, so removing it is the rational choice.B&L 86 · p.1559, p.1560
And afterwards
Teach testicular self-examination, because the point of putting the testis in the scrotum is that a tumour in it can be found.B&L 86 · p.1559 SRB's framing is the same: it makes easy observation and evaluation by ultrasound to detect possible changes early.SRB 26 · p.1067
At what age is an orchidopexy done? B&L 86 p.1560 says 6 to 18 months; B&L 17 p.257 says 6 to 12 months; SRB 26 p.1067 says two to four years

Bailey & Love does not agree with itself. Its testis chapter says orchidopexy "is usually performed between 6 and 18 months of age",B&L 86 · p.1560 while its paediatric surgery chapter says a testis found at the three-month review has "an orchidopexy... then scheduled for between 6 and 12 months".B&L 17 · p.257 The two windows start together and end six months apart.

SRB is a different answer altogether: "surgery is done between two and four years of age."SRB 26 · p.1067 That is not a refinement of B&L's window, it begins after B&L's has closed.

How to answerGive SRB's two to four years first, because an Indian examiner marking from SRB is listening for it, and then add in one sentence that Bailey & Love 28e brings it forward to between 6 and 18 months, because spermatogenic tissue degenerates and spermatogonia counts fall after the second year of life.B&L 86 · p.1559 That reason is in the book and it is the strongest thing you can say here, since it explains why the modern window is early rather than simply asserting a different number. If you are asked to choose, say that current practice follows the earlier window.

5Rapid-fire viva

Define undescended, ectopic, cryptorchid and maldescended testis.

Incomplete descent, or cryptorchidism, is arrest of one or both testes at some point on the normal path to the scrotum; an ectopic testis is abnormally placed outside that path.B&L 86 · p.1558 SRB reserves cryptorchidism for bilateral undescended testis, uses anorchism for complete agenesis, differentiated by an HCG test, and defines maldescended testis as one that cannot be made to touch the bottom of the scrotum, which may be undescended, cryptorchid, ectopic or anorchic.SRB 26 · p.1065 A vanishing testis develops but disappears before birth, probably from prenatal torsion.

Where is the commonest ectopic site?

The superficial inguinal pouch, just inferior and medial to the superficial inguinal ring.B&L 86 · p.1559 Others are femoral, perineal, at the root of the penis, and crossed to the opposite scrotum.

Give the incidence figures.

About 3% of full-term and 30% of premature male infants are born with one or both testes undescended; about two-thirds reach the scrotum in the first three months of life, but full descent after that is uncommon; and the incidence at one year is around 1%.B&L 86 · p.1558 Those numbers justify observing for three months and operating thereafter.

How do you distinguish a retractile from an undescended testis?

Examine the boy relaxed in a warm room, supine, because the cremasteric reflex is active in childhood and any stimulation of scrotal or thigh skin makes the testis retract.B&L 86 · p.1559 A retractile testis can be coaxed into the scrotum and will stay there; a truly undescended one cannot be brought to the bottom of the scrotum.SRB 26 · p.1065 And look at the scrotum: it is underdeveloped in true undescent but relatively well developed in a retractile testis, because a retractile testis has lived in it.B&L 86 · p.1559

What are the consequences of an undescended testis?

Four: infertility, malignancy, hernia and torsion. Around 90% have a patent processus vaginalis, though clinically apparent hernia is much less common; and the undescended testis is more prone to torsion.B&L 86 · p.1559

What happens to fertility?

Reduced, even after orchidopexy. Unilateral cases have an infertility rate not believed to differ much from the general population. After orchidopexy for bilateral cryptorchidism the fertility reduction is about 38%. Patients with bilateral undescended testes operated as adults are almost always infertile and azoospermic, though pregnancies have been reported through sperm retrieval and assisted reproduction.B&L 86 · p.1559 B&L's wording is the higher the testis, the greater the histological change, which is about position rather than delay. Delay still matters, but for the separate reason that a testis left undescended goes on being damaged.

Does orchidopexy prevent testicular cancer?

Apparently not. SRB says orchidopexy may not alter the risk of developing carcinoma of the testis, but makes observation and ultrasound surveillance easier.SRB 26 · p.1067 Bailey and Love gives the figures: the risk is two to three times the general population if orchidopexy is done before puberty and five to six times if after, but the risk does not seem different when it is done early in infancy compared with later in childhood.B&L 86 · p.1559 So doing it before puberty matters, the year within childhood does not, and the real gains of early surgery are fertility and the ability to examine the testis.

Which tumour, and at what age?

In untreated undescended testes the commonest is seminoma, with a peak age range of 15 to 45 years. After orchidopexy, seminomas represent only 30% of testicular tumours in previously undescended testes.B&L 86 · p.1559 It is treatable if caught early, which is why boys who had an orchidopexy as infants should be taught testicular self-examination.

The testis is impalpable under anaesthesia. What now?

Diagnostic laparoscopy, which Bailey and Love recommends for non-palpable testes. If a testis is found, the options are laparoscopic orchidopexy preserving the vessels, dissecting the testis off a triangular pedicle containing the testicular vessels and the vas, or a laparoscopic one-stage Fowler-Stephens orchidopexy, dividing the testicular vessels and relying on the collateral vasal and cremasteric supply.B&L 86 · p.1560 A two-stage Fowler-Stephens clips the vessels first and brings the testis down six months later, once the collateral supply has developed.B&L 86 · p.1560

When would you remove rather than reposition the testis?

If the incompletely descended testis is atrophic or there is suspicion of malignancy, particularly in a postpubertal boy with a normal contralateral testis.B&L 86 · p.1560 SRB adds atrophy, torsion, and inability to bring down an abdominal testis.SRB 26 · p.1067 The logic is that such a testis contributes nothing to fertility and carries five to six times the normal cancer risk.

What else would you look for in a boy with bilateral impalpable testes?

A disorder of sex development, and the associations SRB lists: Klinefelter's syndrome, hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism, Prune-Belly syndrome, renal agenesis, bladder exstrophy, gastroschisis, horseshoe kidney and anorectal malformations.SRB 26 · p.1065 An HCG test distinguishes undescended testes from anorchism. So the first step is endocrine and genetic assessment, not an operation.
