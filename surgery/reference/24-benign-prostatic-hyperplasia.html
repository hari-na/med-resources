Short case 24 · General Surgery

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia

A man over sixty with hesitancy, a poor stream and nocturia, and a smooth elastic enlarged prostate on rectal examination. The one idea that organises the whole case is that the size of the gland is almost irrelevant: what you treat is the symptoms, the flow rate and the residual urine. Say that early and the rest of the answer follows from it.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework
The sentence to open with, and all three books support it S Das: "It must be remembered that the clinical importance of prostatic hyperplasia is measured by the severity of symptoms and the amount of residual urine, and not by the size of the gland."Das 37 · p.564

B&L says the same thing in modern language: "it is important to realise that the relationship between anatomical prostatic enlargement, LUTS and urodynamic evidence of BOO is complex", and then enumerates the possible combinations, which is the best way to see it:B&L 84 · p.1522
No symptoms, no bladder outflow obstruction. No symptoms, but urodynamic evidence of obstruction. Symptoms, no evidence of obstruction. Symptoms and obstruction. Plus the complications: acute or chronic retention, haematuria, infection and stones.B&L 84 · p.1522

Four separate things can be present or absent independently: a big prostate, symptoms, obstruction, and complications. A candidate who treats them as one thing will get the management wrong.

1Anatomy, because every part of the case depends on it

The lobes, and which does what
SRB: five lobes, anterior, posterior, two lateral and one middle or median.SRB 26 · p.1032
Median lobe: "produces an elevation in the lower part of the trigone of bladder (uvula vesicae). Adenoma is more common here due to more amount of glandular tissue."SRB 26 · p.1032 B&L: when BPH affects the central zone glands, "a 'middle' lobe develops that projects up into the bladder within the internal sphincter."B&L 84 · p.1522
Lateral lobes: SRB, "lateral lobes narrow the urethra causing obstruction."SRB 26 · p.1033
Posterior lobe: SRB, "primary carcinoma is said to begin in posterior lobe."SRB 26 · p.1032
So the one-line distinctionBenign hyperplasia starts centrally and medially; carcinoma starts posteriorly and peripherally. That is why a cancer is felt first on rectal examination and a benign median lobe may not be felt at all.
McNeal's zones, which is how the books now describe it
SRB: "McNeal divided prostate into three zones. 1. Peripheral zone, prone for carcinoma. 2. Periurethral transition zone where BPH arises. 3. Central zone."SRB 26 · p.1032 And the note that follows is the crux: "prostate carcinoma develops in prostatic gland proper. BPH develops in submucosal glands."SRB 26 · p.1032
B&L agrees in the same terms: "BPH typically affects the submucous group of glands in the transitional zone, forming a nodular enlargement. Eventually, this overgrowth compresses the peripheral zone glands into a false capsule and causes the appearance of the typical 'lateral' lobes."B&L 84 · p.1522
Two things follow from that one sentence, and both are examined 1. The false capsule exists because BPH compresses normal gland. It is not a true capsule but flattened peripheral-zone tissue, and it is the plane in which an adenoma is enucleated. B&L describes HOLEP as cutting "all the attachments of the adenoma to the false capsule" in "the relatively avascular plane between the peripheral and transitional zones".B&L 84 · p.1529
2. A TURP or an enucleation does not remove the peripheral zone, which is where cancer arises. So a man who has had a prostatectomy can still get prostate cancer.
The two capsules, which SRB wants named
True capsule: "formed by the condensation of the peripheral part of the gland; continues with the stroma of the gland." False capsule: "derived from the pelvic fascia. On each side prostatic venous plexus are embedded in it."SRB 26 · p.1032
Why the venous plexus mattersBecause it is the source of the haemorrhage that is the main risk of every prostatectomy, and because SRB uses it to explain haematuria in BPH: "enlarged prostate compresses the prostatic venous plexus causing congestion, called as vesical piles leading to haematuria."SRB 26 · p.1033
And the surgical landmark that protects continence
The verumontanum. Resection stops there. B&L: "strips of tissue are cut from the bladder neck down to the level of the verumontanum",B&L 84 · p.1529 and "damage to the external sphincter is avoided provided the verumontanum is used as a guide to the most distal point of the resection, vaporisation or enucleation."B&L 84 · p.1529 SRB says the same: resection is done "above the level of verumontanum".SRB 26 · p.1034
Say the landmark and the reason togetherResect from the bladder neck down to the verumontanum, and no further, because the external sphincter lies distal to it. That is the answer to "how do you avoid incontinence?"

2Aetiology

The hormonal theory, which both books give identically
B&L: "serum testosterone levels slowly but significantly decrease with advancing age; however, levels of oestrogenic steroids are not decreased equally. According to this theory, the prostate enlarges because of increased oestrogenic effects. It is likely that the secretion of intermediate peptide growth factors plays a part in the development of BPH."B&L 84 · p.1522 SRB puts it in the same words: "with age TS level drops slowly. But fall of oestrogen level is not equal. So prostate enlarges through intermediate peptide growth factor."SRB 26 · p.1033
And SRB's hormonal axis, which is worth reciting because it explains the drugs
"Hypothalamus, pulsatile release of LHRH, release of luteinising hormone from anterior pituitary, stimulates Leydig cells of testes, releases testosterone, reaches prostate, releases 5-alpha reductase type II of prostate, converts testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT) for its effects." With the two facts that make it clinically useful: "DHT is five times more potent than TS. 90% TS is from testes. Remaining 10% TS is from adrenal cortex."SRB 26 · p.1033
That axis is the drug chart5-alpha reductase inhibitors block the conversion of testosterone to DHT, which is exactly the step SRB names. B&L describes them as inhibiting "the conversion of testosterone to 1,5-dihydrotestosterone (DHT), the most active form of androgen."B&L 84 · p.1528 And the 10% adrenal contribution is why androgen deprivation for prostate cancer is never complete with orchidectomy alone.
The other associations
B&L: "metabolic syndrome and hereditary factors have also been implicated in its development."B&L 84 · p.1522 SRB adds the historical neoplastic theory and then withdraws it: "BPH is a benign neoplasm, also called as fibromyoadenoma. The neoplastic theory is old one, not universally accepted now, under debate."SRB 26 · p.1033
Age and frequency
B&L: "occurs in men over 50 years of age; by the age of 60 years, 50% of men have histological evidence of BPH", and it is "the most common cause of bladder outflow obstruction in men over 70 years of age".B&L 84 · p.1522 SRB: "occurs after 50 years, usually between 60 and 70 years."SRB 26 · p.1033

3History: the symptoms, named properly

Use the modern vocabulary
The term is lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), divided into voiding and storage. SRB's table is the one to learn:SRB 26 · p.1034
Voiding symptoms: "hesitancy; poor flow not improving by straining; dribbling even after micturition; intermittent stream, stops and starts; poor bladder emptying; episodes of near retention."
Storage symptoms: "frequency; nocturia; urgency; urge incontinence; nocturnal incontinence."
Why the division matters more than the listBecause it predicts the result of surgery. B&L: men "who are unobstructed and have detrusor instability do not respond well to TURP; in fact, their storage symptoms could accentuate postoperatively."B&L 84 · p.1528 Voiding symptoms with a low flow rate get better after TURP; pure storage symptoms may get worse. That one sentence is the most useful thing in the case.
The older term, which examiners still use
SRB: "prostatism is a combination of symptoms like frequency both at day and night, poor stream, delay in starting and difficulty in micturition."SRB 26 · p.1034 Recognise it, then use LUTS.
Two historical details from S Das that discriminate
Does straining help or hinder? "Straining improves the stream (e.g. urethral stricture) or retards the stream (e.g. enlarged prostate, the median lobe pressing on the internal urethral meatus)."Das 37 · p.558 A patient whose stream gets worse when he strains has a ball-valve median lobe; a stricture improves with straining. This is a question almost nobody asks and it separates the two commonest causes of a poor stream.
Does posture help? S Das notes that with a diverticulum of the bladder, "the micturition may be started again by changing posture."Das 37 · p.558
Chronic retention is painless, and that is the trap
S Das: "in chronic retention of urine the patient experiences little or no suprapubic discomfort even though the bladder" is grossly distended.Das 37 · p.557 B&L describes how such men present: "such men may present with overflow incontinence, enuresis and renal insufficiency. These symptoms should alert the doctor to the presence of this condition."B&L 84 · p.1525
So bedwetting in an old man is a surgical emergency Nocturnal enuresis in an elderly man is overflow incontinence from high-pressure chronic retention, and it may be the only symptom of impending renal failure. B&L lists the consequence: back pressure on the upper tract "with the development of bilateral hydronephrosis. As a result, upper tract infection and renal impairment may develop."B&L 84 · p.1525
The International Prostate Symptom Score
B&L: "symptom score sheets such as the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) assign a score that gives information regarding the severity of symptoms at the outset and changes over time and following intervention. The IPSS assessment should include an assessment of quality of life, which is a reflection of the degree of 'bother' caused by a patient's symptoms."B&L 84 · p.1525 SRB notes simply that "international prostate symptom score is available now."SRB 26 · p.1034
And the thing to add that is not the scoreB&L: "in addition to the IPSS, a frequency-volume diary completed by the patient before attending the clinic is invaluable in revealing fluid intake habits, diurnal variation in outputs and low-volume, frequent voiding."B&L 84 · p.1525 A man drinking four litres of tea does not have an obstructed prostate, and the diary is what tells you that.
Ask about complications, because they are indications for surgery
Haematuria; recurrent infection; stones; and retention. SRB's list of consequences is haematuria, renal failure, hydroureter and hydronephrosis from back pressure.SRB 26 · pp.1033, 1034 B&L adds the caution on haematuria: "this may be a complication of BPH. Other causes must be excluded by carrying out urine culture, cytology, computed tomography urography and cystoscopy."B&L 84 · p.1525
And SRB has a name for the error"Incrimination of BPH as the source of haematuria before excluding other causes is termed as 'decoy prostate'."SRB 26 · p.1033 A memorable term, and it is exactly B&L's point. Never attribute haematuria to a big prostate until you have excluded a bladder tumour.

4Examination

1. Examine the abdomen before you examine the rectum Because a full bladder makes the rectal examination unreliable. S Das is explicit: "it is again emphasised that before doing rectal examination palpate the abdomen particularly the suprapubic region to know if the bladder is totally empty or not. Even after micturition if the bladder is not empty, this finding is important and predicts that the rectal examination will not be very reliable in providing necessary informations regarding the prostate."Das 37 · p.563 B&L gives the mechanism: "if there is a considerable amount of residual urine present, it pushes the prostate downwards, making it appear larger than it is."B&L 84 · p.1526 So the sequence isWatch him void, palpate and percuss the suprapubic region, then do the rectal examination. SRB agrees that the rectal examination "should be done when bladder is empty".SRB 26 · p.1034 The volume needed to feel a bladderS Das: "at least 150 ml of urine must be collected in the bladder to make it palpable above symphysis pubis. The swelling is elastic in feel and dull on percussion. Pressure induces a desire for micturition."Das 37 · p.563 B&L adds the inspection sign: in chronic retention, a distended bladder is found "on palpation, on percussion and sometimes on inspection with loss of the transverse suprapubic skin crease."B&L 84 · p.1525
2. Look for the signs of renal failure Because they change the urgency of everything. B&L: "general physical examination may demonstrate signs of chronic renal impairment with anaemia, pedal oedema and dehydration."B&L 84 · p.1525 SRB: "tenderness in suprapubic region, with palpable enlarged bladder due to chronic retention. Hydronephrotic kidney may be palpable."SRB 26 · p.1034 And what else B&L examines"The external urinary meatus should be examined to exclude stenosis and the epididymides are palpated for signs of inflammation."B&L 84 · p.1525 Meatal stenosis is a cause of a poor stream that is missed because nobody looks.
3. Rectal examination: the six things to note S Das's list, and each has a reason:Das 37 · p.564
(i) Size, with the warning that the clinical importance is measured by symptoms and residual urine, not by size.
(ii) Consistency. "Normally the consistency of the prostate is like a rubber. It becomes mushy when congested, becomes indurated in chronic infection with or without calculi and becomes stony hard in carcinoma." B&L: in benign enlargement, "the posterior surface of the prostate is smooth, convex and typically elastic, but the fibrous element may give the prostate a firm consistency."B&L 84 · p.1526
(iii) Surface, smooth or irregular.
(iv) The median groove and the lateral sulci, and whether they are preserved.
(v) Mobility of the rectal mucosa over the prostate, which S Das calls "an important point to differentiate benign enlargement from malignant diseases of the prostate." B&L: "the rectal mucosa can be made to move over the prostate."B&L 84 · p.1526
(vi) Prostatic massage, for asymptomatic prostatitis, and S Das names when not to do it: "prostate should not be massaged in acute prostatitis, acute urethritis and obvious cancer of prostate." The two findings that say cancerS Das, writing about carcinoma: "the median sulcus will be obliterated (very important sign) and the rectal mucosa is tethered" to the gland.Das 37 · p.589 So the contrast is clean. Benign: smooth, convex, elastic, median groove preserved, mucosa mobile. Malignant: hard, nodular, irregular, median groove obliterated, mucosa tethered. Learn it as five paired findings.
4. Two things the rectal examination cannot tell you The median lobe. S Das: "it must be remembered that enlargement of median lobe may not be detected by rectal examination. But one can palpate this lobe per rectum with introduction of a urethral bougie."Das 37 · p.564 The median lobe grows upwards into the bladder, away from your finger, which is why a man can be in retention with a prostate that feels normal.
The top of a very large gland. B&L: "it is not always possible to examine the cranial extreme of the very large prostate per rectum. An inability to get to the prostate base implies a volume of at least 50 mL."B&L 84 · p.1526 That is a usable rule: if you cannot reach the upper border, the gland is at least 50 mL. And what you can feel that students missS Das: "residual urine in the post-prostatic pouch can be felt as a soft swelling above the prostate."Das 37 · p.563 Also the intravesical lobe of the prostate on bimanual examination, and the seminal vesicles.Das 37 · p.563
5. Examine the nervous system, and say why Because a neurological bladder mimics prostatic obstruction exactly, and operating on it makes the patient worse. B&L: "the nervous system is examined to eliminate a neurological lesion. Diabetes mellitus, tabes dorsalis, disseminated sclerosis, cervical spondylosis, Parkinson's disease and other neurological states may mimic prostatic obstruction. If these are suspected, then a pressure-flow urodynamic study should be carried out to diagnose BOO. Examination of perianal sensation and anal tone is useful in detection of an S2 to S4 cauda equina lesion."B&L 84 · p.1526 Two bedside tests, thirty secondsPerianal sensation and anal tone. S Das makes the same point from the other end: "rectal examination should be started by noting the tone of the external sphincter. Laxity of the sphincter suggests similar change in the urinary sphincter."Das 37 · p.563 Offering this unprompted is one of the most reliable marks in the case, because almost nobody does it. SRB's differential, which is the same listStricture urethra; bladder tumour; carcinoma prostate; neurological causes of retention like diabetes, tabes, disseminated sclerosis, Parkinson's disease; idiopathic detrusor activity; bladder neck stenosis and bladder neck hypertrophy.SRB 26 · p.1034

5Investigations

The essentials, in B&L's own list
"Urine analysis by dipstick for blood, leukocyte esterase, glucose and protein; urine culture for infection; serum creatinine; urinary flow rate and residual volume measurement." And as additional investigations, "PSA if indicated" and "pressure-flow studies".B&L 84 · p.1525
Why glucose on a dipstickBecause diabetes causes both polyuria and a neuropathic bladder, and it is on the differential list. Two of the four dipstick items, glucose and leukocyte esterase, are there to find a mimic rather than to confirm BPH.
Flow rate, and the numbers
SRB: "urine flow rate over 15 mL/sec is normal. 10 to 15 mL is equivocal; under 10 mL is low", with "normal peak urine flow rate is 20 mL/sec. In obstruction, it is less than 10 mL/sec."SRB 26 · p.1034 B&L: "a typical history and a flow rate under 10 mL/s (for a voided volume of over 200 mL) will be sufficient for most urologists to recommend treatment."B&L 84 · p.1526
And the conditions on the measurement, which are where marks are B&L: "for this to be meaningful, two or three voids should be recorded... the voided volume should be in excess of 150 to 200 mL." And the pitfalls: "the machine must be accurately calibrated. The patient must void volumes in excess of 150 mL and two or three recordings are needed to obtain a representative measurement."B&L 84 · p.1526
The three causes of a low flow rate, which is the real question B&L: "bladder outflow obstruction; low voided volumes (characteristically in men with detrusor instability); men with weak bladder contractions (low pressure-flow voiding), also known as underactive detrusor."B&L 84 · p.1526 A low flow rate is not the same as obstruction. An underactive detrusor gives the same number and gets worse after TURP.
Voiding pressure, which SRB gives and B&L does not quantify
SRB: "voiding pressure under 60 cm of water is normal; 60 to 80 is equivocal; over 80 is high."SRB 26 · p.1034 Combine the two numbers and you have the definition of obstruction: high pressure with low flow. Low pressure with low flow is an underactive detrusor.
Pressure-flow urodynamics: who actually needs them
This is B&L's list and it is worth knowing, because it is the answer to "would you do urodynamics on everyone?" No. Only these:B&L 84 · p.1526
"Men with suspected neuropathy (Parkinson's disease, dementia, longstanding diabetes, previous strokes, multiple sclerosis); men with a dominant history of irritative symptoms and men with lifelong urgency and frequency; men with a doubtful history and those with flow rates in the near normal range (around or over 15 mL/s); men with invalid flow rate measurements (because of low voided volumes); high residual or chronic retention; men with recurrence of LUTS after previous BPH surgery; young men under 50 and older men over 80 with LUTS."
The pattern behind the listUrodynamics are for the patients in whom you are not sure there is obstruction: the neuropaths, the storage-symptom dominant, the near-normal flows, the very young and the very old. If the history is typical and the flow is under 10, you do not need them.
Ultrasound, and residual urine
SRB: "ultrasound abdomen, look for presence of residual urine."SRB 26 · p.1034 B&L: "usually, a flow rate measurement will be coupled with ultrasound measurement of postvoid residual urine."B&L 84 · p.1526
Upper tract imaging: not routinely, and that is the point
B&L: "most urologists no longer carry out imaging of the upper tract in men with straightforward symptoms. Obviously, if infection or haematuria is present, then the upper tract should be imaged by means of intravenous urogram, CT urography or ultrasound scan."B&L 84 · p.1526 SRB still lists IVU, to see kidney function,SRB 26 · p.1034 and illustrates an IVU showing the enlarged median lobe indenting the bladder.SRB 26 · p.1034
A safe answer covering both"I would not image the upper tract routinely in a man with straightforward symptoms and normal renal function, but I would if there is haematuria, infection, a raised creatinine or a large residual volume."
Cystourethroscopy: when, and what it is not for
B&L is precise about both: "inspection of the urethra, the prostate and the urothelium of the bladder should be done immediately prior to prostatectomy to exclude a urethral stricture, a bladder carcinoma and the occasional non-opaque vesical calculus... direct inspection of the prostate is not used as an indicator to establish the presence of BOO and the need for surgery."B&L 84 · p.1526
So the key sentenceCystoscopy is done to exclude other pathology before operating, not to decide whether to operate. How big the prostate looks down a telescope does not establish obstruction.
PSA, with the figures from both books
B&L is careful about who should be offered it: "after suitable counselling, measurement of serum PSA may be helpful. Men in whom a diagnosis of early prostate cancer might influence treatment option (such as those under 70 years or those with a positive family history who might be offered radical treatment) should be offered a PSA measurement. If the PSA range is 4 to 10 ng/L, a free-to-total PSA ratio of less than 15% should be suspicious of malignancy."B&L 84 · p.1526 Read that as ng/mL. The quotation is exact, and the units in it are a misprint: the same chapter writes "PSA >10 ng/mL", and ng/L would be a thousandfold error. Say ng/mL in the exam. Then multiparametric MRI, and if there is a suspicious index lesion, TRUS-guided or transperineal biopsy.B&L 84 · p.1526
SRB's figures, which an Indian paper will use: normal 4 ng/mL; over 10 significant and suggestive of carcinoma; over 35 almost diagnostic of advanced carcinoma; 25% of men with PSA 4 to 10 have carcinoma, and 20% of men with a normal PSA of 1 to 4 will also have carcinoma.SRB 26 · pp.1032, 1033 SRB also gives the refinements: PSA density over 0.5 ng/mL/g; PSA velocity over 2 ng/mL/year; PSA doubling time under 12 months; percentage free PSA under 25.SRB 26 · pp.1032, 1033
The sentence to say about PSA in a BPH caseSRB: "PSA elevation occurs not only in carcinoma but also in prostatic hyperplasia and prostatitis. But the increase is much more in carcinoma than in benign conditions."SRB 26 · p.1032 And the direction of the free fraction: "major, bound form increases in carcinoma. Minor, free form is increased in benign conditions."SRB 26 · p.1032 That is why the free-to-total ratio discriminates, and why a low free fraction is the worrying one, which is exactly B&L's under-15% rule. On acid phosphatase, see the amber boxSRB gives it a full section; it does not appear in B&L 28e at all.
Serum acid phosphatase: still a test, or gone? An edition note, not a live disagreement. SRB 26 p.1032 gives it a full section with King-Armstrong units; the words "acid phosphatase" appear nowhere in B&L 28e ch.84
SRB
Devotes a section to it, ahead of PSA, and lists it among the investigations for BPH: "it is the enzyme that splits organic phosphates... more concentrated in prostate. It is active at pH 5... serum acid phosphatase estimation should be done on empty stomach because heavy meals alter the level... normal value is 0 to 5 King Armstrong units per 100 ml of serum. It is raised significantly in carcinoma prostate with metastases... it does not increase in BPH. Slight increase occurs in acute prostatitis, Paget's disease of bone and hepatic cirrhosis."SRB 26 · p.1032 It also appears in SRB's own investigation list for BPH.SRB 26 · p.1034
Bailey & Love 28e
The term is absent from the prostate chapter. Every tumour-marker sentence is about PSA and its refinements, the free-to-total ratio, and then multiparametric MRI and targeted biopsy.B&L 84 · p.1526
Why it went
PSA superseded it on every count. Acid phosphatase rises only once a prostate cancer has metastasised, which SRB itself says, so it cannot detect the disease when detection matters; it is not prostate-specific, since SRB lists Paget's disease of bone and cirrhosis as other causes; and it needs a fasting sample. PSA is organ-specific, rises earlier, and can be tracked quantitatively by velocity, density and doubling time. There is no disagreement about the biochemistry; the test has simply been replaced.
How to answer: if asked to list investigations in a BPH or prostate case, lead with PSA and do not volunteer acid phosphatase. If an examiner asks about it directly, answer it fully, since it is in SRB and may be in the marking scheme: "serum acid phosphatase, normal 0 to 5 King-Armstrong units per 100 mL, taken fasting because a heavy meal alters the level. It is raised in carcinoma of the prostate with metastases and is not raised in benign hyperplasia, but it is also raised in acute prostatitis, Paget's disease of bone and hepatic cirrhosis." Then add, in one line, that it has been superseded by PSA because it only rises once the disease has metastasised and is not prostate-specific. For an Indian written paper include it where asked; in a viva about current practice, PSA is the answer.

6Indications for treatment

B&L's strong indications, with the proportions they account for
This is an unusually useful list because it tells you how often each actually happens:B&L 84 · pp.1526, 1527
Acute retention in fit men with no other cause such as drugs, constipation or a recent operation: 25% of prostatectomies.
Chronic retention and renal impairment: "a residual urine of 200 mL or more, hydroureter or hydronephrosis demonstrated on ultrasound, uraemic manifestations and abnormal renal function": 15% of prostatectomies.
Complications of obstruction: "stone, infection and diverticulum formation."
Haemorrhage: "these patients present with recurrent haematuria with no obvious cause and a very vascular prostate can be seen on cystoscopy."
Elective prostatectomy for severe symptoms: about 60% of prostatectomies.
And the three qualifications B&L attaches to the elective indication
"Frequency alone is not a strong indication for prostatectomy. The natural progression of outflow obstruction is variable and rarely gets worse after 10 years. Severe symptoms not responding to drug therapy, a low maximum flow rate (under 10 mL/s) and an increased residual volume of urine (100 to 250 mL) are relatively strong indications for operative treatment."B&L 84 · p.1527
Read the middle sentence again"The natural progression of outflow obstruction is variable and rarely gets worse after 10 years." That is the justification for watchful waiting, and it is the sentence that distinguishes a thoughtful answer from a reflexive one.
SRB's indications for surgery
"Prostatism (frequency, dysuria, urgency); acute retention of urine; chronic retention of urine with residual urine more than 200 mL; complications like hydroureter, hydronephrosis, stone formation, recurrent infection, bladder changes; haematuria."SRB 26 · p.1034 Essentially B&L's list, with the 200 mL threshold the same.
And the three questions B&L asks before offering an operation
"Have they failed a preliminary trial of medical therapy?" and "is BOO present?", where "the findings of significant symptoms (assessed by symptom scoring) and a benign enlarged prostate supplemented by the finding of a low maximum flow rate (under 10 to 12 mL/s for a good voided volume, over 150 to 200 mL)" will usually suffice.B&L 84 · p.1527

7Treatment

Conservative treatment, and who it is for
B&L: "it is in men with relatively mild symptoms, reasonable flow rates (over 10 to 15 mL/s) and good bladder emptying (residual urine under 100 mL) that careful discussion over the merits and side effects of operative treatment is warranted. Waiting for a period of 6 months after careful discussion of the diagnosis is indicated. After this, a repeat assessment of symptoms and flow rates and an ultrasound scan are helpful; many men with stable symptoms will elect to leave matters be."B&L 84 · p.1528
Plus the fluid advice, which B&L puts in its summary box: "watchful waiting in conjunction with fluid manipulation (avoid fluid binge and late night intake) and a reduction in caffeinated and alcoholic drinks."B&L 84 · p.1527
Say the fluid adviceIt costs nothing, it is in the book, and it treats the nocturia that is often the symptom the man actually minds.
Drugs: the two classes, and how they differ
Alpha-blockers. B&L: "alpha-adrenergic blocking agents inhibit the contraction of smooth muscle that is found in the prostate."B&L 84 · p.1528 SRB adds the mechanism in bladder terms, "they reduce the bladder neck resistance so as to improve the urine flow", and gives the agents: short acting, prazosin and indoramin; long acting, terazosin and doxazosin; selective alpha-1A, tamsulosin 0.2 to 0.4 mg once daily for 12 weeks.SRB 26 · p.1036
5-alpha reductase inhibitors. B&L: they "inhibit the conversion of testosterone to 1,5-dihydrotestosterone (DHT), the most active form of androgen. These drugs, when taken for a year, result in a 25% reduction in the size of the prostate gland."B&L 84 · p.1528 SRB: finasteride 5 mg daily for 6 to 8 months.SRB 26 · p.1036
The comparison to quoteB&L: "both groups of drugs are effective; however, alpha-blockers work more quickly and although the 5-alpha reductase inhibitors have fewer side effects they need to be taken for at least 6 months and their effect is greatest in patients with large (over 40 g) glands."B&L 84 · p.1528 So: alpha-blocker for quick symptomatic relief, 5ARI for a big gland and for slowing progression, both together for a gland over 35 g.B&L 84 · p.1527
A third class. B&L: "another drug class that has improved patients' symptom scores but not their maximum flow rate are the phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors, which reduce smooth muscle tone and possibly the inflammation in the prostate gland. These drugs are particularly useful if patients have concomitant erectile dysfunction."B&L 84 · p.1528
And B&L's honest verdict on drugs, which is worth having ready "Drug therapy results in improvements in maximum flow rates by about 2 mL/s more than placebo and results in a mild (20%) improvement in symptom scores... TURP, however, results in improvements in maximum flow rates from 9 to 18 mL/s and a 75% improvement in symptom scores. These drugs are expensive in comparison with their effectiveness, and a significant proportion of men who try these drugs will subsequently undergo surgical treatment."B&L 84 · p.1528
So the honest framingDrugs: +2 mL/s and a 20% symptom improvement. TURP: 9 to 18 mL/s and a 75% symptom improvement. Drugs are for the man who wants to avoid the side effects of surgery, chiefly retrograde ejaculation, which is why B&L introduces them with "in men who are very concerned about the development of sexual dysfunction after TURP, the use of drugs may be helpful."B&L 84 · p.1528
Managing acute retention
Catheterise first. SRB: "patient with acute retention of urine requires urethral catheterisation. If urethral catheterisation fails, then suprapubic cystostomy is done."SRB 26 · p.1034 B&L: "once the bladder has been drained by means of a catheter, the patient's fitness for treatment is determined. If retention was not caused by drugs or constipation, then prostatectomy would usually be the correct management. Unfit men or those with dementia may be treated by means of an indwelling urethral or suprapubic catheter."B&L 84 · p.1527
Trial without catheter. B&L: "the role of alpha-adrenergic drugs followed by a trial of a catheter has been tested and found to be successful in certain groups with a short history and a low residual volume of urine, but the recurrence rate becomes cumulatively high."B&L 84 · p.1527

Chronic retention, and the two traps in it
Trap one: who needs catheterising urgently. B&L: "men with chronic retention who have relatively low volumes of residual urine and who do not have symptoms suggestive of coexisting infection and with good renal function do not necessarily require catheterisation before proceeding to prostatectomy on the next available list. For those who are uraemic, urgent catheterisation is mandatory to allow renal function to recover and stabilise."B&L 84 · p.1527
Trap two: post-obstructive diuresis, which is the examined complication. B&L: "uraemic patients with chronic retention are often dehydrated at the time of admission. Owing to the chronic back pressure on the distal tubules within the kidney, there is loss of the ability to reabsorb salts and water. The result, following release of this pressure, may be an enormous outflow of salts and water, which is known as postobstructive diuresis. It is for this reason that a careful fluid chart, daily measurements of the patient's weight and serial estimations of creatinine and electrolytes are essential."B&L 84 · p.1527 Intravenous fluid is given if the patient cannot keep up, and blood when the haemoglobin is under 9 g/dL once fluid balance is stable.B&L 84 · p.1527 The page prints "g/L", which is a misprint; see below.
That haemoglobin threshold is a misprint in the book B&L 84 p.1527 prints g/L where it means g/dL

The page really does read "if haemoglobin is <9 g/L". Taken literally that is a haemoglobin of 0.9 g/dL, which is not compatible with life. The intended figure is 9 g/dL, that is 90 g/L.

How to answer: say 9 g/dL, which is B&L's figure with its units read correctly. If you are quoting the book aloud, say "around 9 g per decilitre" rather than reciting the units as printed.
Outside the three books, flagged as such: restrictive transfusion thresholds in current hospital practice are lower than this, and transfusion is judged on symptoms and ongoing loss rather than a number alone. And the other thing that happens on catheterising a chronic retention B&L: "haematuria often occurs following catheterisation owing to collapse of the distended bladder and upper tract, but settles within a couple of days."B&L 84 · p.1527 Decompression haematuria. TURP: the operation
B&L: "strips of tissue are cut from the bladder neck down to the level of the verumontanum. Cutting is performed by a high-frequency diathermy current, which is applied across a loop mounted on the hand-held trigger of the resectoscope... the 'chips' of prostate are then removed from the bladder using an Ellik evacuator. Resection proceeds at 1 g per minute in experienced hands."B&L 84 · p.1529
The time limit, and why bipolar changed it. "The duration of resection for monopolar TURP is limited to 1 hour due to the risk of resorption of water if 1% glycine is used as an irrigant. The advent of bipolar TURP where normal saline is used as an irrigant permits resection of larger prostates."B&L 84 · p.1529
That is the whole of TUR syndrome in two sentencesMonopolar diathermy cannot work in saline, so a non-conducting hypo-osmolar irrigant, 1.5% glycine, is used; absorption of it through open venous sinuses causes dilutional hyponatraemia and water intoxication. Bipolar diathermy works in saline, so the irrigant is isotonic and the syndrome essentially disappears, which is why larger glands can now be resected.
Antibiotics: "men with indwelling catheters, those with recent urinary infection, those with chronic retention or those with prosthetic material or heart valves benefit from prophylactic antibiotics."B&L 84 · p.1529
Afterwards: a three-way self-retaining catheter with irrigation "continued until the outflow is pale pink, and the catheter is usually removed on the second or third postoperative day."B&L 84 · p.1529 SRB: continuous bladder irrigation through a three-way Foley catheter, "catheter is removed within 72 hours".SRB 26 · p.1034
On which fluid you irrigate with afterwards, see the amber box The two books do not say the same thing, and it matters.

Controlling bleeding, which is the commonest problem
B&L: "haemorrhage is a major risk following prostatectomy whatever the surgical approach." Arterial points are diathermied, and "some of the venous bleeding due to deep resection can only be stopped by gentle traction via a Foley catheter balloon inflated to 30 to 40 mL and kept in the bladder. Sustained traction is applied by taping the catheter to the anterior abdominal wall or thigh for 12 to 24 hours. This causes compression of the prostatic tissue and veins and thus stops the bleeding."B&L 84 · p.1529
And if the catheter stops draining: "this may indicate that a clot is blocking the eye of the catheter. The bladder should be promptly washed out using a strict aseptic technique."B&L 84 · p.1529
Secondary haemorrhage: "tends to occur several days after the patient has been discharged. All men should be warned about this possibility and given appropriate advice to rest and to have a high fluid intake."B&L 84 · p.1529
Why traction works, in one lineThe balloon pulled down onto the prostatic cavity tamponades the venous plexus in the false capsule. It is pressure, not cautery.

What do you irrigate the bladder with after a TURP: glycine or saline? A live and clinically important disagreement. SRB 26 p.1035 says glycine for 72 hours; B&L 84 p.1529 says isotonic saline
SRB
Intra-operatively, "using cystoscope with fluid like glycine irrigating continuously"; post-operatively, "after surgery, continuous bladder irrigation using normal saline is done using three way Foley's catheter".SRB 26 · p.1034 But in its own comparison box on the next page, among the features of TURP, SRB lists: "continuous postoperative irrigation using glycine solution is needed for 72 hours."SRB 26 · p.1035 So SRB says saline on one page and glycine on the next.
Bailey & Love 28e
Unambiguous, and gives both the fluid and the reason for stopping: "following TURP, careful haemostasis is performed, and a three-way, self-retaining catheter irrigated with isotonic saline is introduced into the bladder to prevent any further bleeding from forming blood clots. Irrigation is continued until the outflow is pale pink, and the catheter is usually removed on the second or third postoperative day."B&L 84 · p.1529 Glycine appears in B&L only as the intra-operative irrigant for monopolar resection, and only as the reason the resection is limited to an hour: "the duration of resection for monopolar TURP is limited to 1 hour due to the risk of resorption of water if 1% glycine is used as an irrigant."B&L 84 · p.1529
Which is right, and why it is not a matter of taste
B&L is right, and SRB's own text agrees with it on p.1034. The logic is in B&L's own sentence. Glycine is used intra-operatively only because monopolar diathermy cannot work in a conducting fluid; it is hypo-osmolar, and absorbing it through open prostatic venous sinuses causes dilutional hyponatraemia and water intoxication, which is TUR syndrome, and is the reason the resection time is capped at an hour. Once the resecting loop is out, there is no reason to keep using it, and continuing to irrigate with glycine for 72 hours would be running the risk of TUR syndrome for three days for no benefit at all. Post-operative irrigation exists only to wash clots out, and isotonic saline does that without any metabolic risk. The modern version of the same logic is bipolar TURP, which uses normal saline as the operating irrigant and therefore "permits resection of larger prostates".B&L 84 · p.1529
How to answer: say saline, and give the reason, which also answers the TUR syndrome question before it is asked. "After the resection I would irrigate through a three-way catheter with isotonic saline, continued until the outflow is pale pink, usually removing the catheter on the second or third day. Glycine is used only as the intra-operative irrigant in monopolar resection, because monopolar diathermy will not work in a conducting medium; it is hypo-osmolar, and absorption through open venous sinuses causes dilutional hyponatraemia and water intoxication, which is why monopolar resection is limited to about an hour. Bipolar resection uses normal saline throughout and so allows larger glands to be resected." If an examiner states that post-operative irrigation is with glycine, do not argue; note that SRB's own text on the preceding page specifies normal saline post-operatively, and move on. Never write that you would irrigate with glycine for 72 hours.
After catheterising a man in retention, how long before you operate: one to two weeks, or six? A live disagreement about the interval. SRB 26 p.1035 says TURP after 7 to 14 days; B&L 84 p.1527 says not less than 6 weeks
SRB
"If patient presents with acute retention of urine, initial urethral catheterization is done. If not possible then suprapubic trocar cystostomy ideally or formal open SPC is done. Once patient's obstructive uropathy is under control, TURP is done after 7 to 14 days after evaluation."SRB 26 · p.1035
Bailey & Love 28e
Gives a much longer interval, but note the patient it is talking about: "patients who develop renal impairment and/or hydronephrosis after urinary retention will need to keep the catheter until definitive surgical treatment is provided, usually not less than 6 weeks afterwards to allow renal function recovery."B&L 84 · p.1527 For the uncomplicated case B&L gives no interval at all and implies it can be prompt: "if retention was not caused by drugs or constipation, then prostatectomy would usually be the correct management",B&L 84 · p.1527 and men with chronic retention, low residuals and good renal function may proceed "to prostatectomy on the next available list".B&L 84 · p.1527
They are describing different patients
SRB's 7 to 14 days is for the straightforward man in acute retention whose "obstructive uropathy is under control". B&L's 6 weeks is specifically for the man who developed renal impairment or hydronephrosis, where the point of waiting is to let the kidneys recover before an anaesthetic and a resection. So there is no contradiction about principle, only an apparent one because B&L names the complicated case and SRB names the simple one. The thing that decides the interval is renal function, not the calendar.
How to answer: make the condition explicit and both figures become one answer. "I would catheterise him and reassess. If he developed renal impairment or hydronephrosis, I would leave the catheter until renal function has recovered and stabilised, usually not less than six weeks, monitoring for post-obstructive diuresis with a fluid chart, daily weights and serial creatinine and electrolytes." Mentioning post-obstructive diuresis in the same breath as the six weeks shows you know why you are waiting, which is the actual question.

What actually happened
The open operations were not disproved; they were made unnecessary, and then their own principle was carried out endoscopically. B&L's description of HOLEP makes this explicit: it does with a laser exactly what Millin's finger did, enucleating the adenoma in the relatively avascular plane between the transitional and peripheral zones, and then morcellating it out through the cystoscope rather than through an abdominal incision.B&L 84 · p.1529 So the eponyms describe an operation whose logic is alive even where the incision is not. Open surgery remains for the very large gland, and B&L now adds robotic to that option.
How to answer: give the four approaches, name the eponyms with their approaches, and then the current ladder. "The prostate can be approached transurethrally, retropubically, transvesically or perineally. Transurethral resection remains the gold standard and is by far the commonest. The open operations are Freyer's suprapubic transvesical prostatectomy, Millin's retropubic prostatectomy, done without opening the bladder, and Young's perineal prostatectomy; open surgery, now sometimes robotically assisted, is reserved for very large glands. Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate does endoscopically what Millin's finger did, enucleating the adenoma in the plane between the transitional and peripheral zones and morcellating it out. For small glands or bladder neck dyssynergia, a transurethral incision of the prostate is preferred to a resection. Newer minimally invasive options include prostate artery embolisation, water vapour therapy, prostatic urethral lift and aquablation." For an Indian written paper, give SRB's eponyms and their complications, since that is the marked answer; the newer options are worth one closing sentence.
When do you give a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor, and is it contraindicated in obstruction? A live disagreement about the indication. B&L 84 pp.1527, 1528 ties it to gland size in millilitres; SRB 26 p.1036 ties it to a palpably enlarged gland and calls it contraindicated in obstructive uropathy
Bailey & Love 28e
Quantifies the indication by gland size and treats it as a drug for progression as well as symptoms. "5-alpha reductase is given to prevent progression of symptoms in men with large (over 35 to 40 mL) prostates. Combination therapy (alpha-blocker and 5-alpha reductase) is better for the larger gland."B&L 84 · p.1527 Its summary box: "drug therapy is with alpha-blockers or, in men with a large prostate, a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor, or both; combination therapy has a better outcome in glands bigger than 35 g."B&L 84 · p.1527 And the effect size: a 25% reduction in prostate size over a year, with "their effect is greatest in patients with large (over 40 g) glands" and a need to be taken "for at least 6 months".B&L 84 · p.1528 It gives these drugs after acute retention precisely to prevent progression.B&L 84 · p.1527
SRB
No size in millilitres, and a prohibition B&L does not have: "it is effective in palpably enlarged prostate. Drug used is finasteride 5 mg daily. Duration of treatment is about 6 to 8 months. It is contraindicated in obstructive uropathy or carcinoma prostate."SRB 26 · p.1036
How to read SRB's "contraindicated in obstructive uropathy"
Read literally it is self-contradictory, since bladder outflow obstruction from a large prostate is the condition the drug is for, and B&L gives it to exactly those men. The sensible reading is established obstructive uropathy, meaning a man with hydronephrosis, a large residual volume or renal impairment. That man needs drainage and then an operation, and a drug that takes six months to shrink the gland by a quarter will let his kidneys fail while he waits. The carcinoma caution is different and is real: finasteride lowers the serum PSA, roughly halving it, so it can mask a cancer and confuse the interpretation of the PSA. The two books agree about everything else: the mechanism, the slow onset, the need for months of treatment, and the greater effect in bigger glands.
How to answer: state the indication by gland size and then the two real cautions. "An alpha-blocker is first-line for symptomatic relief because it works within days. A 5-alpha reductase inhibitor, finasteride 5 mg daily, is added for a large gland, over about 35 to 40 mL, where it reduces prostate volume by about 25% over a year and prevents progression; it needs at least six months to work, and combination therapy gives the best outcome in glands over 35 g. Drugs are not a substitute for surgery in a man with established obstructive uropathy, hydronephrosis, a large residual volume or renal impairment: he needs drainage and a resection. And finasteride lowers the serum PSA, so a baseline PSA should be taken before starting it and the result interpreted with that in mind." That answer contains both books' positions and resolves SRB's phrasing without contradicting it.

8Rapid-fire viva

Which zone of the prostate does BPH arise in, and which does carcinoma arise in?

BPH arises in the submucosal glands of the periurethral transition zone; carcinoma arises in the peripheral zone, and classically in the posterior lobe.SRB 26 · p.1032B&L 84 · p.1522 That is why a cancer is felt first on rectal examination and why a man who has had a prostatectomy can still develop prostate cancer, since the resection removes the transition zone and leaves the peripheral zone behind.

What is the false capsule, and why does it matter?

It is peripheral-zone prostatic tissue compressed and flattened by the expanding adenoma, not a true capsule.B&L 84 · p.1522 It matters because the plane between the adenoma and the false capsule is relatively avascular and is the plane in which the adenoma is enucleated, whether by a finger in Millin's operation or by a laser in HOLEP.B&L 84 · p.1529

Does the size of the prostate determine the treatment?

No. S Das: the clinical importance of prostatic hyperplasia is measured by the severity of symptoms and the amount of residual urine, not by the size of the gland.Das 37 · p.564 Bailey and Love says the same, that the relationship between anatomical enlargement, lower urinary tract symptoms and urodynamic obstruction is complex, and lists the combinations: no symptoms and no obstruction; no symptoms but urodynamic obstruction; symptoms without obstruction; and symptoms with obstruction.B&L 84 · p.1522 Size matters for one thing only: choosing the drug and the operation.

Classify the symptoms, and which respond to surgery?

Voiding symptoms: hesitancy, poor flow not improved by straining, intermittency, terminal dribbling, incomplete emptying. Storage symptoms: frequency, nocturia, urgency, urge and nocturnal incontinence.SRB 26 · p.1034 Voiding symptoms with a proven low flow rate respond well to TURP. Men who are unobstructed and have detrusor instability do not, and their storage symptoms may be worse afterwards.B&L 84 · p.1528

A patient says straining makes his stream worse. What does that suggest?

An enlarged median lobe acting as a ball valve on the internal urethral meatus. S Das: straining improves the stream in a urethral stricture but retards it when the median lobe presses on the internal urethral meatus.Das 37 · p.558

An elderly man has started wetting the bed. Why does that worry you?

Because it is likely to be overflow incontinence from high-pressure chronic retention, which is painless, and which causes bilateral hydronephrosis, upper tract infection and renal impairment. Bailey and Love: such men present with overflow incontinence, enuresis and renal insufficiency, and these symptoms should alert the doctor to the condition.B&L 84 · p.1525 He needs his abdomen palpated, his creatinine measured and his bladder drained.

What would you do before the rectal examination, and why?

Watch him void if possible, then palpate and percuss the suprapubic region to establish whether the bladder is empty. S Das: if the bladder is not empty after micturition, the rectal examination will not be reliable.Das 37 · p.563 Bailey and Love gives the reason: residual urine pushes the prostate downwards and makes it feel larger than it is.B&L 84 · p.1526

Benign or malignant on rectal examination?

Benign: smooth, convex, elastic, median groove preserved, lateral sulci palpable, and the rectal mucosa moves freely over the gland.B&L 84 · p.1526Das 37 · p.564 Malignant: hard, nodular, irregular, median sulcus obliterated, and the mucosa tethered to the gland.Das 37 · p.589 Mobility of the rectal mucosa is the single most useful discriminator.

Can a normal-feeling prostate cause retention?

Yes. The median lobe grows upwards into the bladder, away from the examining finger, so S Das notes that enlargement of the median lobe may not be detected on rectal examination at all, though it can be felt per rectum with a urethral bougie introduced.Das 37 · p.564

You cannot reach the upper border of the prostate. What does that tell you?

That the gland is at least 50 mL. Bailey and Love: an inability to get to the prostate base implies a volume of at least 50 mL.B&L 84 · p.1526

What two neurological signs would you check, and why?

Perianal sensation and anal tone, to detect an S2 to S4 cauda equina lesion.B&L 84 · p.1526 Diabetes, tabes dorsalis, multiple sclerosis, cervical spondylosis and Parkinson's disease all mimic prostatic obstruction, and operating on a neuropathic bladder makes the patient worse. S Das adds that laxity of the anal sphincter suggests similar change in the urinary sphincter.Das 37 · p.563

Essential investigations in a man with LUTS?

Urine dipstick for blood, leukocyte esterase, glucose and protein; urine culture; serum creatinine; and measurement of urinary flow rate and post-void residual volume. PSA if indicated and pressure-flow studies are additional.B&L 84 · p.1525

What flow rate indicates obstruction, and what are the pitfalls?

Under 10 mL/s is low, 10 to 15 equivocal, over 15 normal, with a normal peak around 20 mL/s.SRB 26 · p.1034 The pitfalls: the voided volume must exceed 150 to 200 mL, two or three recordings are needed, and the machine must be calibrated. And a low flow rate has three causes, not one: outflow obstruction, a low voided volume, and an underactive detrusor with weak bladder contractions.B&L 84 · p.1526

How do you prove bladder outflow obstruction?

A pressure-flow urodynamic study, which shows high voiding pressure with low flow. SRB: voiding pressure under 60 cm of water is normal, 60 to 80 equivocal, over 80 high.SRB 26 · p.1034 Low pressure with low flow is an underactive detrusor, not obstruction.

Would you do urodynamics on everyone?

No. They are for the patients in whom obstruction is in doubt: suspected neuropathy; a dominant history of irritative or storage symptoms; near-normal flow rates around 15 mL/s or above; invalid flow measurements because of low voided volumes; high residual or chronic retention; recurrent symptoms after previous BPH surgery; and men under 50 or over 80.B&L 84 · p.1526 With a typical history and a flow rate under 10 they are not needed.

What is cystoscopy for in this case?

To exclude other pathology immediately before prostatectomy: a urethral stricture, a bladder carcinoma and a non-opaque vesical calculus. Bailey and Love is explicit that direct inspection of the prostate is not used to establish the presence of obstruction or the need for surgery.B&L 84 · p.1526

Haematuria in a man with a big prostate. What do you do?

Do not blame the prostate. SRB calls that error the "decoy prostate".SRB 26 · p.1033 Other causes must be excluded by urine culture, cytology, CT urography and cystoscopy.B&L 84 · p.1525 BPH can bleed, from congestion of the prostatic venous plexus, which SRB calls vesical piles,SRB 26 · p.1033 but that is a diagnosis of exclusion.

Indications for surgery in BPH?

Acute retention in a fit man with no other cause, about 25% of prostatectomies; chronic retention with renal impairment, a residual of 200 mL or more, hydroureter or hydronephrosis, about 15%; complications of obstruction, meaning stone, infection or diverticulum; recurrent haematuria from a vascular prostate with no other cause; and severe symptoms not responding to drugs, with a flow rate under 10 mL/s and a residual of 100 to 250 mL, which is about 60%.B&L 84 · pp.1526, 1527 Frequency alone is not an indication.

Compare drugs with TURP.

Drugs improve the maximum flow rate by about 2 mL/s more than placebo and give about a 20% improvement in symptom score. TURP improves the flow rate from about 9 to 18 mL/s and gives about a 75% improvement in symptom scores.B&L 84 · p.1528 Drugs are chiefly for the man who wants to avoid the sexual side effects of surgery, and a significant proportion who try them go on to have surgery anyway.

What is TUR syndrome, and how is it avoided?

Water intoxication with dilutional hyponatraemia, from absorption of the hypo-osmolar glycine irrigant through open prostatic venous sinuses during monopolar resection; it can cause congestive cardiac failure and confusion.SRB 26 · p.1034 It is avoided by limiting monopolar resection to about an hour, and now largely avoided altogether by bipolar resection, which works in normal saline and therefore allows larger glands to be resected.B&L 84 · p.1529

How far do you resect, and why?

From the bladder neck down to the verumontanum and no further.B&L 84 · p.1529SRB 26 · p.1034 The verumontanum is the landmark for the most distal point of resection because the external sphincter lies distal to it, and damage to it causes incontinence.B&L 84 · p.1529

Bleeding after a TURP that will not stop with diathermy. What next?

Catheter traction. The Foley balloon is inflated to 30 to 40 mL, kept in the bladder and taped under sustained traction to the anterior abdominal wall or thigh for 12 to 24 hours, which compresses the prostatic tissue and veins and stops venous bleeding.B&L 84 · p.1529 If the catheter stops draining, suspect a clot blocking the eye and wash the bladder out aseptically.

What would you tell a man before a TURP?

That retrograde ejaculation occurs in about 65 to 85%; erectile dysfunction in about 5 to 10%, usually where potency is already waning; incontinence in about 1%; that about 15% need a second operation within eight to ten years; mortality is under 0.5%; severe sepsis about 6%; and bleeding needing transfusion of more than two units about 3%.B&L 84 · p.1528SRB 26 · p.1035 And that the results depend on selection: about 90% do well if the obstruction was proven, but only about 65% if symptoms were mild or the detrusor was weak.

What is post-obstructive diuresis, and how do you manage it?

After relief of chronic high-pressure retention, chronic back pressure on the distal tubules has destroyed the ability to reabsorb salt and water, so there is an enormous outflow of both. It is managed with a careful fluid chart, daily weights, serial creatinine and electrolytes, and intravenous replacement if the patient cannot keep up; blood transfusion if the haemoglobin is under 9 g/dL once fluid balance is stable. B&L prints this as "9 g/L", which is a misprint: 9 g/L would be lethal.B&L 84 · p.1527

Name the open operations for BPH.

Freyer's suprapubic transvesical prostatectomy, which was the procedure of choice before TURP; Millin's retropubic prostatectomy, done without opening the bladder; and Young's perineal prostatectomy.SRB 26 · p.1035 Bailey and Love names the same four anatomical approaches and reserves open, now sometimes robotic, simple prostatectomy for very large glands.B&L 84 · pp.1527, 1528

What is HOLEP?

Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate. The laser cuts all the attachments of the adenoma to the false capsule and coagulates the small vessels crossing the relatively avascular plane between the peripheral and transitional zones, while the tip of the cystoscope enucleates the adenoma much as the surgeon's finger does in Millin's prostatectomy; the adenoma is pushed into the bladder, morcellated and extracted.B&L 84 · p.1529

Which drug would you start, and why that one first?

An alpha-blocker, such as tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, because it relaxes prostatic and bladder neck smooth muscle and works within days.SRB 26 · p.1036B&L 84 · p.1528 A 5-alpha reductase inhibitor, finasteride 5 mg daily, is added for a gland over about 35 to 40 mL; it reduces prostate volume by about 25% over a year but needs at least six months to work. One thing to remember about finasteride and PSA.

It lowers the serum PSA, so take a baseline PSA before starting it and interpret later values with that in mind. SRB lists carcinoma of the prostate as a contraindication to the drug for related reasons.SRB 26 · p.1036

Why is PSA not specific, and what makes it more useful?

Because it rises in benign hyperplasia and prostatitis as well as in carcinoma, although the rise is much greater in carcinoma. The bound form rises in carcinoma and the free form in benign disease, so the free-to-total ratio discriminates: a ratio under 15% in a PSA of 4 to 10 ng/mL is suspicious of malignancy.
