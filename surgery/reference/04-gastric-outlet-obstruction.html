Long case 04 · General Surgery

Gastric Outlet Obstruction

The case where the metabolic answer matters more than the anatomical one. Anyone can say "pyloric stenosis". The marks are in explaining why the urine turns acid while the blood turns alkaline, and in knowing that Bailey & Love now tells you to assume cancer until you have a biopsy that says otherwise.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1The case in one breath

The two causes, and the order you should hold them in
B&L: "The two common causes of gastric outlet obstruction are gastric cancer and pyloric stenosis secondary to peptic ulceration."B&L 67 · p.1168 Note which it names first, and see the disagreement below.
SRB's fuller differential, which is the list to reproduce
Congenital · chronic duodenal ulcer, fibrosed or cicatrised · carcinoma pylorus · adult pyloric stenosis, which is treated by pyloroplasty and not by pyloromyotomy · pyloric mucosal diaphragm, which must be excised surgically or endoscopically.SRB 20 · p.819
The mechanism in benign disease
A chronic duodenal ulcer, after many years, undergoes scarring and cicatrisation causing total obstruction of the pylorus and enormous dilatation of the stomach.SRB 20 · p.818 B&L puts the same thing histologically: the ulcer penetrates the muscle coat, leading to fibrosis, and the resulting scarring may cause a deformity such as pyloric stenosis.B&L 67 · p.1157 S Das: cicatrisation of a duodenal or juxta-pyloric ulcer.Das 34 · p.508
The triad that makes the diagnosis at the bedside
SRB states it as a set: visible gastric peristalsis positive, auscultopercussion test positive, succussion splash positive.SRB 20 · p.840 And its mnemonic for the three: "stomach we see, stomach we feel, stomach we hear".SRB 20 · p.818
Benign until proven otherwise, or malignant until proven otherwise? S Das 34 p.508 and SRB 20 p.818 build the case around chronic ulcer; B&L 67 p.1168 says assume cancer
S Das
Defines the entity itself as benign: "Pyloric stenosis. This is due to cicatrisation of a duodenal or a juxta-pyloric ulcer."Das 34 · p.508 Carcinoma of the stomach is described separately, as an obstructive type of gastric cancer.Das 34 · p.508
SRB
Gives "Pyloric Stenosis due to Chronic Duodenal Ulcer" its own chapter heading and its own clinical features, investigations and treatment,SRB 20 · p.818, p.819, p.820 and lists carcinoma pylorus as the differential diagnosis, distinguished because there a mass may be palpable, whereas in benign pyloric stenosis "mass is never palpable".SRB 20 · p.819
Bailey & Love 28e
Reverses the presumption outright: "With the decreasing incidence of peptic ulceration gastric outlet obstruction should be considered malignant until proven otherwise."B&L 67 · p.1168 And it makes the consequence an instruction rather than a suggestion: "Endoscopic biopsy is essential to exclude malignancy", and "biopsy of the area around the pylorus is essential".B&L 67 · p.1169
This is the most important disagreement in the case, and the two positions are both right about different populations. B&L's premise is a falling incidence of peptic ulcer disease in high-income countries, which makes cancer the likelier cause by elimination. In India peptic ulcer disease remains common, so the benign cause is still frequent, which is why SRB gives it a chapter.

How to answer: give the classical cause, then take B&L's position on management explicitly. Say: "In our population a cicatrised chronic duodenal ulcer is still a common cause, but I would manage every gastric outlet obstruction as malignant until endoscopic biopsy of the pyloric region proves otherwise, because the current Bailey and Love position is that falling ulcer incidence makes cancer the default, and because a missed cancer costs the patient far more than an unnecessary biopsy." That answer takes a position, cites why, and is safe. The one finding that should shift you strongly towards cancer before the biopsy is a palpable mass, which SRB says is never present in benign stenosis.SRB 20 · p.819

2Why this patient obstructed, and why he is so ill

The metabolic cascade, which is the heart of this case Learn it as a chain of consequences, because that is how it will be asked.B&L 67 · p.1169

1. Vomiting hydrochloric acid produces a hypochloraemic alkalosis.
2. Initially the sodium and potassium may be relatively normal. At this stage the urine has a low chloride and high bicarbonate content, reflecting the primary abnormality.
3. As dehydration progresses, more profound abnormalities appear, partly related to renal dysfunction, and the patient becomes progressively hyponatraemic.
4. Because of the dehydration, sodium is retained and potassium and hydrogen ions are excreted in its place.
5. So the urine becomes paradoxically acidic and hypokalaemia ensues.
6. Alkalosis lowers the circulating ionised calcium, and tetany can occur.

SRB names that last step "gastric tetany" and gives the renal logic in its own words: in alkalosis without hyponatraemia the kidney excretes bicarbonate along with sodium, but here, because the body must conserve sodium, bicarbonate is excreted with hydrogen ion instead, so the urine turns acid.SRB 20 · p.820 That sentence is the cleanest explanation of paradoxical aciduria in any of the three books: quote it.
Which electrolytes are abnormal: a list, or a sequence? SRB 20 p.820 gives a flat list of six; B&L 67 p.1169 gives a staged account
SRB
Lists them together, under the heading "Electrolyte Changes in Pyloric Stenosis": hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypochloraemia, metabolic alkalosis, paradoxical aciduria,SRB 20 · p.820 and elsewhere a six-part version adding hypocalcaemia: hypochloraemic, hyponatraemic, hypokalaemic, hypocalcaemic, hypomagnesaemic alkalosis.SRB 20 · p.818
Bailey & Love 28e
Insists on the order. The primary abnormality is hypochloraemic alkalosis alone; "initially the sodium and potassium may be relatively normal; however, as dehydration progresses, more profound metabolic abnormalities arise", and the hyponatraemia and then the hypokalaemia are consequences of the renal response to dehydration.B&L 67 · p.1169
Not a contradiction but a difference in resolution, and the graded answer is the better one because it tells the examiner you know which abnormality causes which. Answer: "The primary disturbance is a hypochloraemic alkalosis from vomiting acid. Sodium and potassium may be normal early. As dehydration progresses the kidney conserves sodium and excretes potassium and hydrogen instead, so hypokalaemia and a paradoxically acidic urine develop, and hypomagnesaemia follows the losses. The alkalosis lowers ionised calcium and can cause tetany." Then give the full list as the complete answer. If you are asked for a single early biochemical abnormality, say hypochloraemia with alkalosis, not hypokalaemia, because hypokalaemia is second-order.
Why the cancer patient is less alkalotic, which is a favourite question Because the stomach is not making as much acid to lose. B&L: in cancer "the metabolic consequences may be somewhat different from those of benign pyloric stenosis because of the relative hypochlorhydria found in patients with gastric cancer",B&L 67 · p.1168 and the alkalosis is "usually less pronounced than when duodenal ulceration leads to obstruction".B&L 67 · p.1170 Its summary box goes further: the hypochloraemic alkalosis "is usually only seen with peptic ulcer disease".B&L 67 · p.1169 SRB agrees in the same terms: metabolic changes are not severe in carcinoma pylorus because there is often hypochlorhydria or achlorhydria.SRB 20 · p.820

So a florid hypochloraemic alkalosis points to a benign cause, and its absence does not reassure you: it points towards cancer. That inversion is the mark.

Risk factors, if the cause turns out to be cancer

Helicobacter pylori, and the limit of what follows from it
Epidemiology points to a role for H. pylori, principally in carcinoma of the body and distal stomach rather than the proximal stomach. But "there is insufficient evidence to support eradication programmes in asymptomatic patients".B&L 67 · p.1170 Know both halves: the association is real, the population screening conclusion does not follow.
Previous ulcer surgery
Patients who have had peptic ulcer surgery, particularly drainage procedures such as Billroth II or Pólya gastrectomy, gastroenterostomy or pyloroplasty, are at approximately four times the average risk, thought to be from bile reflux and intestinal metaplasia.B&L 67 · p.1170 This is the one risk factor the surgeon creates, so it is worth volunteering when you are asked to justify an operation.
Pernicious anaemia, gastric atrophy, adenomatous polyps
All increase risk.B&L 67 · p.1170 On polyps, the rule is: metaplastic polyps are the commonest type, associated with H. pylori and regress after eradication; fundic gland polyps are associated with PPI use and with familial adenomatous polyposis; neither has proven malignant potential, but true adenomas do and should be removed. Biopsy is essential in all of them, because a polyp may actually be an early gastric cancer.B&L 67 · p.1169
Diet, smoking and environment
Excessive salt intake, deficiency of antioxidants, exposure to N-nitroso compounds; cigarette smoking; dust ingestion in industrial processes. The evidence that diet matters is the decline in gastric cancer among Japanese families living in the USA.B&L 67 · p.1170
And the split that changes the whole picture
Distal and body cancer is falling at about 1% per year in the West and is commonest in low socioeconomic groups; proximal gastric cancer, particularly at the gastro-oesophageal junction, is rising and affects higher socioeconomic groups, is not associated with H. pylori, and is associated with obesity. Its aetiology remains an enigma. This shift has not been observed in Japan, where distal cancer still predominates.B&L 67 · p.1170

3History, and why each question earns its place

Establish that there was an ulcer first

How long have you had pain? Where exactly? Point to it. DistinguishesS Das localises both ulcers precisely. Duodenal ulcer: on the transpyloric plane about 1½ inches to the right of the midline, the duodenal point, which is about a hand's breadth below the xiphisternal joint.Das 34 · p.483, p.507 Gastric ulcer: deep tenderness in the mid-epigastric region, elicitable "with remarkable constancy".Das 34 · p.507
Why it matters hereBenign gastric outlet obstruction requires a long history of peptic ulcer disease,B&L 67 · p.1168 so establishing years of typical ulcer pain is what makes the benign diagnosis plausible. Its absence pushes you towards cancer.
Does food make the pain better or worse? How long after eating? DistinguishesDuodenal ulcer: pain appears 2½ to 4 hours after a meal, when the stomach becomes empty, so it is "hunger pain", typically in the early morning or late afternoon, and relieved by taking food.Das 34 · p.507 Gastric ulcer: pain immediately after food, with "clock-like" regularity, relieved when the stomach empties, or by alkali, or by vomiting which is often self-induced.Das 34 · p.507
SoThe food relation separates the two ulcers, and S Das adds the body-habitus consequence: the duodenal ulcer patient has an exceptionally good appetite and eats frequently to relieve pain, so most are well built, whereas the gastric ulcer patient restricts food for fear of pain and is often thin, though appetite remains good.Das 34 · p.507
And the heavier the meal"The heavier the meal, the longer is the interval but the worse is the pain."Das 34 · p.507
Has the pain lost its old pattern? Does it still come and go in spells? Rules inLoss of periodicity. In peptic ulcer there is definite periodicity lasting several weeks followed by an interval of freedom, with attacks in spring and autumn.Das 34 · p.482, p.507 SRB lists loss of periodicity as a clinical feature of pyloric stenosis.SRB 20 · p.818 S Das's version in the history is "certain loss of its periodicity with enhancement of pain and fullness towards evening".Das 34 · p.508
And the other thing loss of periodicity meansS Das's warning about gastric ulcer: "if [the patient] loses his periodicity of pain one may suspect superimposition of carcinoma or penetration into the pancreas or pyloric obstruction".Das 34 · p.483 Three possibilities from one answer, and you should name all three.
Rules outIn appendicular dyspepsia and gallbladder disease this definite periodicity is not found; instead a mild pain continues even in the quiet periods.Das 34 · p.482

Now characterise the vomiting, which is the diagnostic symptom

How much do you vomit, at what time of day, and what is in it? Rules inAll three books converge. B&L: "The vomitus is characteristically of undigested food and is totally lacking in bile."B&L 67 · p.1168 SRB: large quantity, foul smelling and frothy, containing food consumed on the previous day, partially digested or undigested.SRB 20 · p.818 S Das: large in amount, characteristically in the evening, often foul and frothy, containing undigested food eaten even 2 or 3 days earlier.Das 34 · p.508
The three features to nameLarge volume; no bile; and old food. The absence of bile localises the obstruction at or proximal to the pylorus, which is the whole point of asking.
ContrastIn superior mesenteric artery syndrome, the cast syndrome, the vomiting is bilious, with upper abdominal fullness, visible peristalsis and dehydration, and SRB lists pyloric stenosis with gastric outlet obstruction as its differential.SRB 20 · p.832 Bile in the vomit moves the obstruction distal to the second part of the duodenum.
And one more characterS Das: "coffee ground" vomiting indicates slow haemorrhage into the stomach.Das 34 · p.483
Does vomiting relieve the pain? Rules inS Das: "Vomiting always relieves pain of peptic ulcer."Das 34 · p.483
Rules in something worseIn carcinoma of the stomach the late symptom is vomiting which gives no relief to the pain, with constant epigastric pain neither caused by nor relieved by food.Das 34 · p.508 So "does it help?" is a one-question screen for malignancy, and almost nobody asks it.
Have you lost weight? How is your appetite now? WhyWeight loss is a feature of benign gastric outlet obstruction too, and the patient appears unwell and dehydrated,B&L 67 · p.1168 so weight loss alone does not mean cancer. SRB lists loss of appetite and weight.SRB 20 · p.818
DistinguishesWhat does discriminate is the sequence. In benign disease there are years of typical ulcer pain and then obstruction and then weight loss. In cancer, early satiety, bloating, distension and vomiting arrive on a short history, with profound weight loss,B&L 67 · p.1170 and S Das notes of the insidious type that "the history is usually short".Das 34 · p.508
Careful about ageS Das: carcinoma of the stomach is a disease of the elderly, "but if suspected in the young, should not be turned down on the point of age only".Das 34 · p.508

Then go looking for cancer

Any black stools, or vomiting of blood? Any tiredness or breathlessness? Rules inBoth causes bleed. S Das: haematemesis and melaena accompany chronic gastric ulcer in about a quarter of cases, and in duodenal ulcer they are more frequent than in gastric ulcer, with a 5% chance of massive haemorrhage and a 2% chance of perforation.Das 34 · p.507 B&L on cancer: the tumour frequently bleeds, resulting in iron deficiency anaemia.B&L 67 · p.1170 SRB gives haematemesis in 15% of gastric cancers.SRB 20 · p.841
SoAnaemia in a patient with gastric outlet obstruction is expected either way, but iron deficiency anaemia with a short history and no ulcer history is a cancer story.
Any swelling in the neck? Any abdominal distension? Any leg swelling or painful veins? Rules inMetastatic disease, and each has a name. Troisier's sign: tumour appearing in the left supraclavicular node, Virchow's node.B&L 67 · p.1171SRB 20 · p.839 Ascites from transcoelomic spread. Trousseau's sign: migrating thrombophlebitis and deep venous thrombosis, a non-metastatic effect of malignancy arising from the tumour's effect on thrombotic and haemostatic mechanisms.B&L 67 · p.1170SRB 20 · p.841
The footnote worth knowingTrousseau described the sign in himself and suspected he had gastric cancer; at post-mortem it proved to be pancreatic cancer.B&L 67 · p.1170 SRB also lists migrating thrombophlebitis in carcinoma of the pancreas.SRB 20 · p.841
And the nodal point that surprises peopleB&L: "Unlike malignancies such as breast cancer, nodal involvement does not" carry the same implication.B&L 67 · p.1171 Gastric cancer nodal spread may be extensive and still resectable, which is why D2 lymphadenectomy exists.
Any previous stomach operation? Rules inTwo separate things. First, cancer risk: previous drainage surgery raises it fourfold through bile reflux and intestinal metaplasia.B&L 67 · p.1170 Second, gastro-jejunal ulcer, which S Das describes in detail: it follows a Pólya gastrectomy or a gastro-jejunal anastomosis, the incidence being very high when gastro-jejunostomy is not combined with vagotomy, recurring in about 3% after Pólya gastrectomy and 5% after vagotomy and gastro-jejunostomy, usually within 2 years, with pain within half an hour of food radiating from above and left of the umbilicus to the left iliac fossa.Das 34 · p.508
And its dreadful end pointGastro-jejunocolic fistula: anastomotic ulcer symptoms disappear and are replaced by severe diarrhoea, eructation of foul gas, and vomiting of fragments of formed faeces, from fouling of the jejunum by colonic contents. And the examinable technical point: it is better diagnosed by a barium enema, since in more than half of patients the barium meal fails to reveal the fistula.Das 34 · p.508

4Examination, and what each finding settles

Before you touch the patient The examiner is marking this before you have found anything. Bailey & Love gives it as a sequence: "ensure you have followed appropriate hand hygiene guidance. Introduce yourself and check the patient's name and date of birth. Request presence of a chaperone as appropriate. Explain what you are going to do, obtain verbal consent and ensure that the patient is comfortable."B&L 35 · p.472 And the standard it is held to: "patients should be treated with respect and dignity, receive a clear explanation of the examination undertaken and be kept as comfortable as possible. A chaperone should be present, especially for intimate examinations."B&L 21 · p.285

Do it, and be heard doing itWash your hands. Introduce yourself. Say what you want to examine and why, and ask permission. Ask for a chaperone where the examination is intimate. Expose what you need and cover the rest. Warm your hands. Tell the patient what each step will feel like and that they can ask you to stop, examine the tender part last, and watch their face rather than only your hands. Cover them and thank them before you turn to the examiner.

For the abdomen, privacy is part of the examination B&L asks for permission to begin the consultation in ensured privacy, and notes that "if a patient is still uncomfortable or reticent, the presence of a close family member as a chaperone can help. A tense patient without a relaxed abdominal wall will substantively affect the accuracy of the clinical examination."B&L 63 · p.1051 So this is not only manners: an embarrassed patient guards, and a guarded abdomen hides the sign you are after.
Look for the three signs in the order SRB names them Stomach we see, stomach we feel, stomach we hear.SRB 20 · p.818 Visible gastric peristalsis; the dilated stomach outlined by auscultopercussion; and the succussion splash. Then B&L's much shorter version, which is all it offers: "On examination the distended stomach may be visible and a succussion splash may be audible."B&L 67 · p.1168

General examination first

Dehydration
B&L: the patient appears unwell and dehydrated.B&L 67 · p.1168 Assess it properly, because the fluid deficit is what you will be treating first.
Confusion, irritability, convulsions
SRB lists confused status because of alkalosis and electrolyte changes,SRB 20 · p.818 and in its account of the metabolic syndrome: irritability, confused status, dehydration, often convulsions, and features of alkalosis such as rapid Cheyne-Stokes breathing and tetany.SRB 20 · p.820 A confused patient with vomiting is not septic until proved otherwise: check the calcium and the gases.
Signs of tetany
Look for them actively. Alkalosis lowers ionised calcium and tetany can occur,B&L 67 · p.1169 which SRB calls gastric tetany.SRB 20 · p.820
Anaemia and cachexia
SRB lists both among the features of gastric cancer,SRB 20 · p.841 and iron deficiency anaemia follows from a tumour that bleeds.B&L 67 · p.1170

Inspection of the abdomen

Look across the epigastrium in tangential light for several minutes. Then give the patient a cup of water and look again. Rules inVisible gastric peristalsis. S Das is explicit about its value: "visible peristalsis passing from left to right is pathognomonic".Das 34 · p.508 And he tells you when to go looking: "If there is any history suggesting pyloric stenosis, one should look for visible peristalsis."Das 34 · p.489
How to provoke itSRB: visible gastric peristalsis may be elicited by asking the patient to drink a cup of water.SRB 20 · p.818 Say that you would do this, and then actually wait: peristalsis comes in waves and candidates look for three seconds and report it absent.
Direction mattersLeft to right, following the line of the stomach towards the obstructed pylorus.Das 34 · p.508
Does Bailey & Love teach visible gastric peristalsis? S Das 34 p.508 calls it pathognomonic and SRB 20 p.818 tells you how to provoke it; the word does not appear in B&L 67
S Das
"On examination, visible peristalsis passing from left to right is pathognomonic."Das 34 · p.508 It is also one of the two things he instructs you to look for whenever the history suggests pyloric stenosis.Das 34 · p.489
SRB
Lists visible gastric peristalsis as a clinical feature, gives the manoeuvre to bring it out, and makes it the first of the three signs in its triad.SRB 20 · p.818, p.840
Bailey & Love 28e
The word "peristalsis" does not occur anywhere in chapter 67. Its entire physical examination of gastric outlet obstruction is one sentence: the distended stomach may be visible and a succussion splash may be audible.B&L 67 · p.1168 Elsewhere it says of peptic ulcer disease generally that examination "may reveal epigastric tenderness but, except in gastric outlet obstruction, there is unlikely to be much else to find".B&L 67 · p.1159
Not a disagreement about the sign, but about whose job it is: B&L has largely handed the bedside to endoscopy, and the clinical signs in this case live in the Indian texts. Elicit and report visible gastric peristalsis, because your examiner is marking from S Das and SRB and because it is a genuinely useful sign. Just do not say "Bailey and Love says" when you mean S Das: the only two physical findings B&L offers here are a visible distended stomach and a succussion splash. If an examiner trained on the British text seems unimpressed by the sign, the honest line is that it is a classical sign retained in the Indian clinical tradition and that the diagnosis is confirmed endoscopically either way.

Palpation and percussion

Feel for a mass, and let the answer change your diagnosis
SRB: in pyloric stenosis due to chronic duodenal ulcer, "mass is never palpable"; in carcinoma pylorus, "here mass may be palpable".SRB 20 · p.819 This is the single most useful discriminating sign at the bedside, and it is the reason to palpate carefully with the stomach emptied.
The auscultopercussion test, described properly
Place a stethoscope over the epigastrium. Scratch the skin from the left side downwards at several points away from the epigastrium, towards the left, with a finger, and join the points at which the sound changes. Normally the greater curvature lies above the level of the umbilicus, midway between umbilicus and epigastrium. In gastric outlet obstruction it lies below the umbilicus.SRB 20 · p.818 On barium, SRB's equivalent threshold is more extreme: the greater curvature below the level of the iliac crest.SRB 20 · p.819
Examine for ascites and for secondaries
SRB's list for advanced gastric cancer: a liver with secondaries, hard and nodular in 50%, with umbilication; ascites; Troisier's sign; rectovesical secondaries, the Blumer shelf, on rectal examination; and Trousseau's sign.SRB 20 · p.841 B&L adds the two named transcoelomic deposits: the ovaries as the sole site of spread, Krukenberg's tumours, and spread to the umbilicus, Sister Joseph's nodule.B&L 67 · p.1174
So do a rectal examinationBecause the rectovesical shelf is free information and candidates skip it.

Auscultation: the succussion splash

Place the stethoscope over the epigastrium and shake the patient by the shoulders or the hips. Rules inA splash of fluid in a stomach that should be empty, so the stomach is not emptying. All three books carry the sign.Das 34 · p.508SRB 20 · p.818B&L 67 · p.1168
The precondition is the whole testThe stomach must have been empty for some hours, or you are splashing the breakfast of a normal person. SRB: shake the patient adequately, with a stethoscope over the epigastric region.SRB 20 · p.818
And it is not specific to obstructionSRB lists a positive succussion splash in acute gastric dilatation too.SRB 20 · p.833 A dilated stomach splashes whatever the reason it is dilated.
How long must the stomach be empty before testing for a succussion splash? SRB says 4 hours on p.818 and 4 to 6 hours on p.840
SRB, in the pyloric stenosis section
"Positive succussion splash which is done with 4 hours empty stomach, by placing a stethoscope over the epigastric region and shaking the patient adequately."SRB 20 · p.818
SRB, in the gastric cancer section
Features of gastric outlet obstruction are VGP positive, auscultopercussion positive, and "+ve succussion splash (to be checked with 4-6 hours empty stomach)".SRB 20 · p.840
S Das and Bailey & Love
Neither states a time. S Das says only that the splash "is often heard",Das 34 · p.508 and B&L only that it "may be audible".B&L 67 · p.1168 So there is no third opinion to appeal to.
One book giving two numbers is a gift, because you cannot be wrong if you give the safer one and say why. Answer "at least four hours, and I would prefer six", and give the reasoning rather than the citation: the test asks whether the stomach still holds fluid when it should be empty, so the longer the fast, the more specific the sign. Four hours is the minimum at which a normal stomach should have emptied a light meal; at six hours a splash is hard to explain any other way. If the examiner wants a single figure, say four hours, since that is the number in SRB's pyloric stenosis chapter, which is the chapter this case comes from.

5Investigations, and why each one

What do you do before you investigate anything? AnswerEmpty the stomach, and empty it properly. B&L: "A large nasogastric tube may not be sufficiently large to deal with the contents of the stomach, and it may be necessary to pass an orogastric tube and lavage the stomach until it is completely emptied. This allows investigation with endoscopy and contrast radiology."B&L 67 · p.1169 SRB names a tube for it, Ewald's, and gives the second benefit: a stomach wash with normal saline also reduces the oedema of the stomach wall and improves gastric [emptying].SRB 20 · p.819
SoYou cannot scope a stomach full of three-day-old food, and saying this first shows you have thought about the actual patient rather than the list.

The investigations, in order of priority

1. Serum electrolytes, arterial blood gases, serum calcium and magnesium
Because the metabolic abnormality is what will kill him, and because correcting it is the first treatment. SRB's list is exactly this: serum electrolytes; arterial blood gas analysis; serum calcium level estimation.SRB 20 · p.820 Add magnesium, which SRB lists among the deficits and the replacements.
2. ECG
SRB: to check for hypokalaemia.SRB 20 · p.819 And it is needed again during potassium replacement, which SRB says must be given slowly under ECG monitoring.SRB 20 · p.820
3. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy: the decisive test
SRB: gastroscopy to rule out carcinoma stomach and to visualise the stenosed area.SRB 20 · p.819 B&L is stronger: "Endoscopic biopsy is essential to exclude malignancy", and "biopsy of the area around the pylorus is essential".B&L 67 · p.1169 Say the word essential, because that is the word the book uses.
And biopsy everything that looks like a polypGastric polyps matter because they may actually represent early gastric cancer, so biopsy is essential there too.B&L 67 · p.1169
4. Barium meal, if endoscopy is unavailable
SRB's four findings: absence of the duodenal cap; a dilated stomach with the greater curvature below the level of the iliac crest; a mottled stomach, from retained food; and barium will not pass into the duodenum.SRB 20 · p.819 S Das: "Barium meal X-ray will clinch the diagnosis."Das 34 · p.508
The complication nobody mentionsSRB warns that a barium meal may precipitate intestinal obstruction.SRB 20 · p.808
And the mechanism of the absent capWith deformity of the first part of the duodenum, barium will not stay, so the cap will not be formed.SRB 20 · p.808
5. Goldstein's saline load test
Instil 750 mL of saline; aspirate half an hour later; more than 250 mL remaining suggests obstruction.SRB 20 · p.819 A bedside test of gastric emptying that is worth naming even though endoscopy has superseded it, because it is the kind of thing an examiner asks to see whether you have read SRB or a summary.
6. Staging, once malignancy is proven
Contrast CT, and the staging system is the UICC TNM. Two eighth-edition points worth knowing: all gastric tumours whose epicentre is within 5 cm of the gastro-oesophageal junction and which extend into the oesophagus are now staged by the oesophageal system, while those within 5 cm that do not extend into the oesophagus, and all other gastric cancers, are staged by the gastric system.B&L 67 · p.1171 Transperitoneal spread is detected most effectively by laparoscopy rather than by CT.B&L 67 · p.1174

6Treatment, and the reasoning behind each choice

The order of operations, literally B&L: "Treating the patient involves correcting the metabolic abnormalities and dealing with the mechanical obstruction." In that order.B&L 67 · p.1169 Nobody with a hypochloraemic alkalosis and a potassium of 2.5 should be anaesthetised, and saying so unprompted is worth more than any operative detail in this case.

Resuscitation, and why saline is the right fluid

Isotonic saline with potassium
B&L: "The patient should be rehydrated with intravenous isotonic saline with potassium supplementation." And the reason, which is the examinable part: "Replacing the sodium chloride and water allows the kidney to correct the acid-base abnormality."B&L 67 · p.1169 Its summary box says the same: the hypochloraemic alkalosis should be treated with isotonic saline with potassium.B&L 67 · p.1169
SoYou do not give bicarbonate, and you do not give acid. You give the kidney back the chloride and the volume it needs to excrete bicarbonate itself.
SRB's version, with the extras
Normal saline, often double strength normal saline, and slow correction is important. Intravenous potassium slowly under ECG monitoring; intravenous magnesium; intravenous calcium. Plus blood transfusion if anaemic and total parenteral nutrition support.SRB 20 · p.819, p.820 The nutritional point is a real one: these patients have not eaten properly for weeks.
Stomach wash
With normal saline through a wide tube, which also reduces the oedema of the stomach wall.SRB 20 · p.819
And treat the ulcer while you wait
B&L: "Early cases may settle with conservative treatment, as oedema around the ulcer diminishes as the ulcer is healed", and its summary box notes that aggressive medical therapy for peptic ulcer disease often suffices in early cases.B&L 67 · p.1169 So part of the obstruction is oedema, which is reversible, and part is scar, which is not. That distinction is why a few days of decompression and acid suppression can convert an operation into a discharge.
Is endoscopic balloon dilatation worth doing? B&L 67 p.1169 says it may be effective and most useful early; SRB 20 p.820 says it benefits only temporarily
Bailey & Love 28e
Positive, with a condition. Its summary box lists "Endoscopic dilatation of the gastric outlet may be effective" as a management option, and the text says "Endoscopic treatment with balloon dilatation may be most useful in early cases and may have to be repeated several times."B&L 67 · p.1169
SRB
Negative: "Endoscopic balloon dilatation of the stenosed area benefits only temporarily with high recurrence rate."SRB 20 · p.820
They are not far apart once you notice that B&L concedes the recurrence in the same sentence: "may have to be repeated several times" is the same observation SRB states as an objection. Answer it as a question of selection, not of principle: "Balloon dilatation has a role in early, benign, oedematous stenosis, and in a patient too unfit for surgery, but it commonly needs repeating and recurrence is high, so it is not a definitive treatment for an established fibrotic stricture." Then add the two places endoscopy genuinely wins: it is how you get the biopsy, which is mandatory; and in unresectable malignancy, where B&L says a duodenal stent may be considered.B&L 67 · p.1169

The operation for benign obstruction

Gastroenterostomy, not pyloroplasty, and both books agree on why B&L: "Traditionally, severe cases are treated surgically, usually with a gastroenterostomy rather than a pyloroplasty."B&L 67 · p.1169 Its summary box puts it as a drainage procedure for benign disease and appropriate resection for malignancy.B&L 67 · p.1169

SRB supplies the mechanism B&L leaves out, and it is the better answer: "No role of pyloroplasty or only HSV in a scarred duodenum, as it can cause disruption and bile leak."SRB 20 · p.820 You cannot safely cut and re-suture a thick, fibrotic, scarred duodenum, so you bypass it. SRB's operation of choice follows: "GJ with vagotomy is commonly used in pyloric stenosis due to chronic duodenal ulcer."SRB 20 · p.820
Why add a vagotomy to the gastrojejunostomy
Because a drainage procedure alone leaves the acid, and a gastrojejunostomy without vagotomy is the classical setting for a stomal ulcer. S Das gives the figures: the incidence of gastro-jejunal ulcer is very high when gastro-jejunostomy is not associated with vagotomy, and recurrence is 3% after Pólya gastrectomy and 5% after vagotomy and gastro-jejunostomy.Das 34 · p.508
What vagotomy actually does, in SRB's phrase
"No acid, no ulcer." In highly selective vagotomy, only the fibres supplying the parietal cells are divided; the nerve of Latarjet, supplying the antral pump, is retained, so no drainage procedure is required. Fibres in the last 6 cm of stomach proximal to the pylorus are preserved, the crow's foot, and distal 6 cm of oesophageal nerve clearance is essential. Described by Amdrup and Johnston in 1969; also called parietal cell or superselective vagotomy.SRB 20 · p.818
Its figuresOperative mortality 0.2%, morbidity 0.5%; very low post-vagotomy diarrhoea and dumping; but lesser curve necrosis from ischaemia and recurrent ulcer in 10 to 15% at 10 years.SRB 20 · p.818
But not hereSRB specifically excludes HSV alone in a scarred duodenum for the same reason it excludes pyloroplasty.SRB 20 · p.820
Alternatives, and where they sit
Vagotomy with antrectomy: removes the gastrin-producing antrum and the vagal pathway, with gastroduodenal anastomosis; ulcer recurrence very low but morbidity higher; SRB says it is occasionally used for intractable resistant duodenal ulcer or chronic duodenal ulcer with obstruction.SRB 20 · p.818, p.820 Gastrectomy and other procedures are usually not necessary in pyloric stenosis.SRB 20 · p.820
What to warn the patient about afterwards
SRB: gastric emptying may be delayed for 2 to 4 weeks after surgery in pyloric stenosis, though it usually recovers in 7 days.SRB 20 · p.820 A stomach dilated for months does not regain tone overnight, and a patient who still vomits on day 5 does not necessarily need re-operation.
And eradicate H. pylori anyway
SRB: after recovery, eradication of H. pylori is routinely done even though infection may not be evident in many patients with outlet obstruction.SRB 20 · p.820 The triple regime it gives: a proton pump inhibitor with clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily and metronidazole 400 mg twice daily, or amoxycillin 500 to 750 mg twice daily and tinidazole 600 mg twice daily. And a practical warning: antacids and H2 blockers should not be used along with a PPI, because they create an alkaline medium that reduces the PPI's action. Follow-up gastroscopy is a must, to confirm the ulcer has healed.SRB 20 · p.818

If the cause is cancer

The prognosis, stated honestly
"Carcinoma of the stomach is a major cause of mortality worldwide. Its prognosis tends to be poor, with cure rates little better than 5 to 10%, although better results are obtained in Japan, where the disease is common."B&L 67 · p.1169 And the sentence that explains the gap: "Gastric cancer is actually an eminently curable disease" whose poor outcome is owing to the advanced stage at presentation.B&L 67 · p.1169 Better results in Japan come from a high population incidence, screening programmes and high-quality surgery.B&L 67 · p.1169
Incidence, for scale
USA about 10 per 100 000 per year; Eastern Europe about 40; Japan approximately 70.B&L 67 · p.1169
The classification that actually matters clinically
The Laurén classification, which B&L calls the most useful clinicopathological classification. Intestinal type resembles a carcinoma elsewhere in the gut, forms polypoid tumours or ulcers, and probably arises in intestinal metaplasia. Diffuse type, often with signet ring cells, infiltrates deeply without forming an obvious mass but spreads widely in the gastric wall, and unsurprisingly has a much worse prognosis.B&L 67 · p.1170 SRB gives the intestinal type as 53% with favourable prognosis, and notes that in the diffuse type linitis plastica is common, with decreased E-cadherin and p53 and p16 inactivation.SRB 20 · p.837
Early versus advanced, which is the staging that decides cure
Early gastric cancer is cancer limited to the mucosa and submucosa, with or without node involvement, T1 any N. It is eminently curable, with 5-year survival around 90%, and is classified by the Japanese system as protruding, superficial or excavated.B&L 67 · p.1170 Advanced cancer involves the muscularis and is classified by Borrmann into four types, of which types III and IV are commonly incurable.B&L 67 · p.1170 SRB's Borrmann list: I single polypoid; II ulcerated with a clear margin; III ulcerated without a clear margin; IV diffuse, linitis plastica; V unclassified.SRB 20 · p.838
Resection, and the margin
For distal tumours a subtotal gastrectomy suffices: "it is unnecessary to remove the whole stomach", the proximal remnant surviving on the short gastric arteries.B&L 67 · p.1175 Resection margins should be well clear of the tumour, greater than 5 cm, with frozen section if either margin is in doubt. Continuity is restored with a Roux loop, whose alimentary limb should be at least 50 cm, to prevent bile reflux oesophagitis.B&L 67 · p.1175
D1 or D2
D1 removes the perigastric nodes; D2 clears the major arterial trunks. In practice most specialist centres perform a radical total gastrectomy conserving the spleen and pancreas, with D2 lymphadenectomy sparing station 10 nodes.B&L 67 · p.1175 Conserving spleen and pancreas is the modern part of that sentence and the part worth saying.
Palliation of an obstructing, unresectable tumour
Palliative resection is appropriate where there are significant symptoms of obstruction or bleeding, and a palliative gastrectomy need not be radical. Where the tumour cannot be resected, B&L is blunt about the usual bypass: "A high gastroenterostomy is a poor operation that very frequently does not allow the stomach to empty adequately and may produce the additional problem of bile reflux." A Roux loop with a wide gastrojejunal anastomosis may be better, although even this may not allow the stomach to empty well. For inoperable cardia tumours, intubation or stenting.B&L 67 · p.1175 And for unresectable malignant outlet obstruction, a duodenal stent.B&L 67 · p.1169
What proportion of gastric cancers in Japan are early gastric cancer? B&L 67 p.1170 says about one-third; SRB 20 p.837 says 50%
Bailey & Love 28e
"In Japan, approximately one-third of gastric cancers diagnosed are in this stage." It adds that early diagnosis in the UK is relatively uncommon, as dyspeptic patients are not always referred for endoscopy at an appropriate stage.B&L 67 · p.1170
SRB
"More common in Japan: 50% of gastric cancers treated are early gastric cancers. In USA, 20%." With the figures that matter more: 10% of early gastric cancers have nodal metastases, 3% in purely mucosal lesions and 20% in submucosal lesions, and nodal spread depends on tumour size over 2 cm and differentiation.SRB 20 · p.837
A small numeric disagreement, and the figures are measuring slightly different things: B&L says "diagnosed", SRB says "treated". Answer the range and attribute it: "Between a third and a half of gastric cancers in Japan are early gastric cancer, against about a fifth in the USA, and relatively few in countries without endoscopic screening." Then move to the numbers that change management, which are SRB's: early gastric cancer still has nodal metastases in about 10%, only 3% if confined to mucosa but 20% once the submucosa is involved, which is exactly why endoscopic mucosal resection is offered for mucosal lesions and not for submucosal ones.SRB 20 · p.837 Quoting a bare percentage for Japan gains nothing; explaining why submucosal invasion changes the operation gains a lot.

7Rapid-fire viva

What are the causes of gastric outlet obstruction in an adult?

The two common ones are gastric cancer and pyloric stenosis secondary to peptic ulceration.B&L 67 · p.1168 SRB's fuller list adds congenital stenosis, adult pyloric stenosis, which is treated by pyloroplasty and not pyloromyotomy, and a pyloric mucosal diaphragm, which must be excised surgically or endoscopically.SRB 20 · p.819 And remember superior mesenteric artery syndrome as a mimic, distinguished because its vomiting is bilious.SRB 20 · p.832

Benign or malignant until proven otherwise?

Bailey and Love 28e is explicit: with the decreasing incidence of peptic ulceration, gastric outlet obstruction should be considered malignant until proven otherwise, and endoscopic biopsy of the pyloric region is essential.B&L 67 · p.1168, p.1169 In India, where peptic ulcer disease is still common, a cicatrised chronic duodenal ulcer remains a frequent cause, so give the classical cause but manage it as malignant until the biopsy returns. The finding that shifts you towards cancer before the biopsy is a palpable mass: SRB says a mass is never palpable in benign pyloric stenosis and may be palpable in carcinoma pylorus.SRB 20 · p.819

Describe the vomiting.

Three features. Large volume; completely lacking bile; and containing undigested or partially digested food eaten a day or even two or three days earlier.B&L 67 · p.1168SRB 20 · p.818Das 34 · p.508 S Das adds that it is typically in the evening and often foul and frothy. The absence of bile is the important part: it places the obstruction at or proximal to the pylorus.

Explain paradoxical aciduria.

Vomiting hydrochloric acid gives a hypochloraemic alkalosis, and early the urine is appropriately alkaline with a low chloride and high bicarbonate. As dehydration progresses the kidney must conserve sodium, so instead of excreting bicarbonate with sodium it excretes bicarbonate with hydrogen ion, and potassium is lost as well. So the urine turns acidic while the blood is alkalotic, and hypokalaemia develops.B&L 67 · p.1169SRB 20 · p.820 SRB puts it crisply: in alkalosis without hyponatraemia bicarbonate is secreted along with sodium, but here the body conserves sodium, so bicarbonate goes out with hydrogen.

Why can this patient have tetany?

Alkalosis lowers the circulating ionised calcium, and tetany can occur.B&L 67 · p.1169 SRB calls it gastric tetany and groups it with the other features of alkalosis, rapid Cheyne-Stokes breathing, irritability, confusion and sometimes convulsions.SRB 20 · p.820

Why is the alkalosis less marked when the cause is cancer?

Because of the relative hypochlorhydria, or achlorhydria, found in gastric cancer: there is less acid to lose in the vomit.B&L 67 · p.1168, p.1170SRB 20 · p.820 Bailey and Love's summary box goes as far as saying the hypochloraemic alkalosis is usually only seen with peptic ulcer disease.B&L 67 · p.1169 So a florid alkalosis suggests a benign cause, and its absence does not reassure you.

What fluid would you give, and why that one?

Intravenous isotonic saline with potassium supplementation. The reason is the answer: replacing the sodium chloride and water allows the kidney itself to correct the acid-base abnormality.B&L 67 · p.1169 Do not give bicarbonate. SRB adds double strength saline in severe cases, slow correction, intravenous potassium under ECG monitoring, and magnesium and calcium as needed.SRB 20 · p.820

What would you do before endoscoping this patient?

Empty the stomach properly. A large nasogastric tube may not be big enough for the contents, so it may be necessary to pass an orogastric tube and lavage the stomach until it is completely empty, which is what makes endoscopy and contrast radiology possible.B&L 67 · p.1169 SRB names Ewald's tube and adds that a saline stomach wash also reduces the oedema of the stomach wall and improves gastric emptying.SRB 20 · p.819

Name the three physical signs of gastric outlet obstruction.

Visible gastric peristalsis, a positive auscultopercussion test and a positive succussion splash: stomach we see, stomach we feel, stomach we hear.SRB 20 · p.818, p.840 S Das calls visible peristalsis passing from left to right pathognomonic, and tells you to look for it whenever the history suggests pyloric stenosis.Das 34 · p.489, p.508 Note that Bailey and Love 28e offers only two findings here, a visible distended stomach and a succussion splash, and does not mention peristalsis anywhere in the chapter.

How do you perform the auscultopercussion test?

Place a stethoscope over the epigastrium, then scratch the skin with a finger from the left side downwards at several points away from the epigastrium and join the points where the sound changes, mapping the greater curvature. Normally it lies above the umbilicus, midway between umbilicus and epigastrium; in gastric outlet obstruction it lies below the umbilicus.SRB 20 · p.818 On a barium meal, the equivalent is a greater curvature below the level of the iliac crest.SRB 20 · p.819

How long must the stomach be empty before you test for a succussion splash?

At least four hours, and six is better. SRB itself gives both figures, four hours in its pyloric stenosis section and four to six hours in its gastric cancer section.SRB 20 · p.818, p.840 The reasoning is what matters: the test asks whether the stomach still holds fluid when it should be empty, so the longer the fast the more specific the sign. Neither S Das nor Bailey and Love states a time.

What does a barium meal show, and what is the risk?

Absence of the duodenal cap, because with a deformed first part of the duodenum the barium will not stay long enough to form one; a dilated stomach with the greater curvature below the iliac crest; a mottled stomach from retained food; and barium failing to pass into the duodenum.SRB 20 · p.808, p.819 The risk SRB flags is that it may precipitate intestinal obstruction.SRB 20 · p.808 S Das says barium meal will clinch the diagnosis,Das 34 · p.508 but endoscopy has superseded it because only endoscopy gives you the biopsy.

What is Goldstein's saline load test?

Instil 750 mL of saline into the stomach and aspirate half an hour later. If more than 250 mL remains, that suggests obstruction.SRB 20 · p.819

Gastroenterostomy or pyloroplasty, and why?

Gastroenterostomy. Bailey and Love says severe cases are treated surgically, usually with a gastroenterostomy rather than a pyloroplasty,B&L 67 · p.1169 and SRB gives the reason: there is no role for pyloroplasty, or for highly selective vagotomy alone, in a scarred duodenum, because it can cause disruption and bile leak.SRB 20 · p.820 You cannot safely cut and re-suture thick fibrotic tissue, so you bypass it. SRB's usual operation is gastrojejunostomy with vagotomy, the vagotomy being added because a drainage procedure alone leaves the acid and invites a stomal ulcer.

What is a stomal ulcer, and how common is it?

A gastro-jejunal ulcer at or near the anastomosis, after a Pólya gastrectomy or a gastro-jejunal anastomosis, and its incidence is very high when gastrojejunostomy is done without vagotomy. It recurs in about 3% after Pólya gastrectomy and 5% after vagotomy with gastro-jejunostomy, usually within two years, with boring pain within half an hour of food radiating from above and left of the umbilicus to the left iliac fossa.Das 34 · p.508 It is more prone to perforation than an ordinary peptic ulcer and may penetrate the transverse colon to form a gastro-jejunocolic fistula.

How is a gastro-jejunocolic fistula diagnosed?

By barium enema, not barium meal: in more than half of patients the barium meal fails to reveal the fistula.Das 34 · p.508 Clinically the anastomotic ulcer symptoms disappear and are replaced by severe diarrhoea, eructation of foul gas, and sometimes vomiting of fragments of formed faeces, from fouling of the jejunum by colonic contents.

What is highly selective vagotomy, and why does it need no drainage procedure?

Only the fibres supplying the parietal cells are divided, so acid secretion falls, but the nerve of Latarjet supplying the antral pump is preserved, which means gastric emptying is intact and no drainage procedure is required. The nerve fibres in the last 6 cm of stomach proximal to the pylorus, the crow's foot, are preserved, and clearance of the distal 6 cm of oesophageal fibres is essential. Described by Amdrup and Johnston in 1969, and also called parietal cell or superselective vagotomy. Mortality 0.2%, morbidity 0.5%, but lesser curve necrosis from ischaemia and recurrent ulcer in 10 to 15% at ten years.SRB 20 · p.818 It is not the operation for a scarred duodenum with obstruction.

Your patient is still vomiting on the fifth postoperative day. Are you worried?

Not immediately. SRB notes that after surgery for pyloric stenosis gastric emptying may be delayed for two to four weeks, although it usually recovers within seven days.SRB 20 · p.820 A stomach that has been dilated for months does not regain tone at once. Exclude a mechanical problem and an electrolyte abnormality, keep the stomach decompressed and nutrition going, and give it time.

Would you eradicate Helicobacter pylori?

Yes, routinely after recovery, even though infection may not be evident in many patients with outlet obstruction.SRB 20 · p.820 A proton pump inhibitor with clarithromycin 500 mg and metronidazole 400 mg twice daily, or amoxycillin with tinidazole; and follow-up gastroscopy is a must to confirm healing. Do not combine antacids or H2 blockers with the PPI, because the alkaline medium reduces the PPI's action.SRB 20 · p.818 Note separately that Bailey and Love says the evidence does not support eradication programmes in asymptomatic people for cancer prevention.B&L 67 · p.1170

Which previous operations increase the risk of gastric cancer?

Drainage procedures: Billroth II or Pólya gastrectomy, gastroenterostomy and pyloroplasty, which carry approximately four times the average risk, thought to be from bile reflux and intestinal metaplasia.B&L 67 · p.1170 It is worth volunteering, because it is the one risk factor the surgeon creates.

Give the Laurén classification and say why it matters.

Two principal forms. Intestinal type resembles a carcinoma elsewhere in the gut, forms polypoid tumours or ulcers, probably arises in intestinal metaplasia, and has the better prognosis, about 53% of cases. Diffuse type, often with signet ring cells, infiltrates deeply without forming an obvious mass but spreads widely in the gastric wall, shows reduced E-cadherin, and has a much worse prognosis; linitis plastica belongs here.B&L 67 · p.1170SRB 20 · p.837 It matters because the diffuse type is the one that will not give you a mass to feel or a clean margin to resect.

Define early gastric cancer and give its survival.

Cancer limited to the mucosa and submucosa, with or without lymph node involvement, that is T1 with any N. Five-year survival is around 90%, and the Japanese classification describes it as protruding, superficial or excavated.B&L 67 · p.1170 The figures that change management are SRB's: nodal metastases in about 10% overall, but only 3% when confined to mucosa against 20% once the submucosa is involved, which is why endoscopic mucosal resection suits mucosal lesions and not submucosal ones.SRB 20 · p.837

What is the difference between a D1 and a D2 resection?

D1 removes the perigastric nodes; D2 clears the major arterial trunks. In practice most specialist centres perform a radical total gastrectomy conserving the spleen and pancreas, with D2 lymphadenectomy sparing station 10 nodes.B&L 67 · p.1175 For a distal tumour it is unnecessary to remove the whole stomach; a subtotal gastrectomy suffices, with the remnant supplied by the short gastric arteries, margins more than 5 cm clear, frozen section if a margin is in doubt, and a Roux loop of at least 50 cm to prevent bile reflux oesophagitis.B&L 67 · p.1175

The tumour is unresectable and the patient is obstructed. What are the options?

A palliative resection if symptoms of obstruction or bleeding are significant, and it need not be radical: it is enough to remove the tumour and reconstruct. If resection is impossible, Bailey and Love is blunt that a high gastroenterostomy is a poor operation that frequently fails to let the stomach empty and may add bile reflux; a Roux loop with a wide gastrojejunal anastomosis may be better, though even that may not empty well. A duodenal stent may be considered, and for inoperable cardia tumours, intubation or stenting.B&L 67 · p.1169, p.1175

Name the signs of metastatic gastric cancer you would look for.

Troisier's sign, tumour in the left supraclavicular or Virchow's node; a hard nodular liver, with umbilication in about 50%; ascites; a rectovesical shelf, Blumer's shelf, on rectal examination; Sister Joseph's nodule at the umbilicus; Krukenberg's tumours in the ovaries from transcoelomic spread; and Trousseau's sign, migrating thrombophlebitis.SRB 20 · p.841B&L 67 · p.1171, p.1174 Transperitoneal spread is detected most effectively by laparoscopy rather than CT.B&L 67 · p.1174

Why is gastric cancer prognosis poor if it is curable?

Because of the advanced stage at presentation. Bailey and Love says gastric cancer is actually an eminently curable disease, with cure rates little better than 5 to 10% overall only because patients present late; better results in Japan come from high population incidence, screening programmes and high-quality surgery.B&L 67 · p.1169 Early gastric cancer has a five-year survival of about 90%.

Where is gastric cancer getting commoner, and where rarer?

In the West the incidence is falling at about 1% per year, but that fall is confined to the body and distal stomach; proximal cancer, particularly at the gastro-oesophageal junction, is rising, and adenocarcinoma at that site has doubled in the UK over thirty years. Distal and body cancer is commonest in low socioeconomic groups and is associated with H. pylori; proximal cancer affects higher socioeconomic groups, is not associated with H. pylori, is associated with obesity, and its aetiology remains an enigma. This shift has not been seen in Japan, where distal cancer still predominates.B&L 67 · p.1170

Would you dilate the stricture endoscopically?

In selected cases. Bailey and Love says endoscopic balloon dilatation may be effective and is most useful in early cases, but may have to be repeated several times.B&L 67 · p.1169 SRB puts the same observation as an objection: it benefits only temporarily, with a high recurrence rate.SRB 20 · p.820 So it has a role in early oedematous stenosis and in the patient unfit for surgery, but it is not definitive treatment for an established fibrotic stricture. What endoscopy is indispensable for is the biopsy.

A patient with a short history, no ulcer history, and a palpable epigastric mass. Diagnosis?

Carcinoma of the stomach causing gastric outlet obstruction. The short history, the absence of the long ulcer history that benign stenosis requires, and the palpable mass all point that way, since SRB says a mass is never palpable in benign pyloric stenosis.SRB 20 · p.818, p.819B&L 67 · p.1168 Expect a less marked alkalosis because of hypochlorhydria. Confirm with endoscopy and biopsy, stage with CT, and consider laparoscopy, which detects transperitoneal spread most effectively.
