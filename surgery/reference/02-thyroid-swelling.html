Thyroid Swelling

The case with the most eponyms per square centimetre in all of surgery, and the one where the three books overlap least. S Das owns the bedside, SRB owns the framework, and Bailey & Love 28e barely examines the patient at all. Knowing which book you are quoting is half the viva.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1The case in one breath

The seven things SRB says you must settle Before anything else, know what this case is for. SRB lists what must be made very clear in any thyroid case, and if you can answer these seven you have passed: functional status (hyperthyroid, euthyroid or hypothyroid); compression on trachea or recurrent nerve; neck lymph nodal status; tracheal deviation; carotid infiltration; retrosternal extension; and systemic features of toxicity or of malignant spread to bone, liver or lungs.SRB 6 · p.451 Every question and every manoeuvre below exists to answer one of those seven.
Goitre means generalised enlargement
B&L is precise about the vocabulary, and the vocabulary is worth marks. The normal thyroid is impalpable. Goitre (Latin guttur, the throat) means generalised enlargement. A discrete swelling in one lobe with no palpable abnormality elsewhere is isolated or solitary. A discrete swelling with abnormality elsewhere in the gland is dominant.B&L 55 · p.855 Use the word "dominant" correctly and you sound like someone who has read the chapter.
And the numbers that go with those words
Discrete thyroid swellings are palpable in 3–4% of the adult population and are three to four times more frequent in women. About 70% of discrete swellings are clinically isolated and 30% dominant, though clinical classification is subjective and overestimates how many are truly isolated: expose the gland at operation or scan it and impalpable nodules turn up.B&L 55 · p.857
Why a discrete swelling matters
15% of isolated swellings prove to be malignant and a further 30–40% are follicular adenomas; the remainder are non-neoplastic colloid degeneration, thyroiditis or cysts. The risk in a clinically dominant swelling is about half that of a truly isolated one, which is still substantial and cannot be ignored.B&L 55 · p.858
The normal gland, for the anatomy question that always follows
Weighs 20–25 g. The functioning unit is the lobule, supplied by a single arteriole and made of 24–40 follicles lined with cuboidal epithelium, containing colloid in which thyroglobulin is stored.B&L 55 · p.850
Why it moves on swallowing, which you will be asked
The gland is bound to the larynx by Berry's ligament, a condensation of pretracheal fascia running to the cricoid cartilage.B&L 55 · p.852 SRB completes the mechanism: the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus parts of the inferior constrictor are attached to the thyroid and cricoid cartilages, so on swallowing they contract and carry the cartilages, and with them the gland, upwards.SRB 6 · p.452 That is the full answer: a ligament for the attachment and a muscle for the movement.
The "rule of 12" for malignant risk, new and highly quotable B&L 28e expresses the risk of cancer in a thyroid swelling as a factor of 12, rising with three things: isolated rather than dominant, solid rather than cystic, and male rather than female.B&L 55 · p.858 You do not need the grid; you need the three axes, because they are exactly the three things the examiner will vary when he changes the stem of the question.

2Why this patient has a goitre

Iodine, and why geography is a history question

The daily requirement
0.1–0.15 mg of iodine a day. In nearly every district where simple goitre is endemic the iodide content of water and food is very low.B&L 55 · p.855
Where the belts are
B&L names the mountain ranges: the Rockies, the Alps, the Andes and the Himalayas, plus Derbyshire and Yorkshire in the UK; and lowland areas where the soil lacks iodide or the water comes from distant mountains, such as the Great Lakes, the plains of Lombardy, the Struma Valley, the Nile Valley and the Congo.B&L 55 · p.855 S Das gives the Indian belts you should name instead: the Himalayas, the Vindyas and the Satpuda.Das 27 · p.374 Ask where the patient lives and where he grew up, not just where he lives now.
Calcium is goitrogenic too
Goitre is common in low-iodine areas on chalk or limestone, which is why Derbyshire and southern Ireland appear on the list.B&L 55 · p.855
Adequate intake is not the same as adequate absorption
Failure of intestinal absorption may produce iodine deficiency even where iodide in food and water is adequate.B&L 55 · p.855
And too much iodine causes goitre as well
"Surprisingly, iodides in large quantities are goitrogenic", because they inhibit the organic binding of iodine, producing an iodide goitre; excessive intake is also associated with an increased incidence of autoimmune thyroid disease.B&L 55 · p.856

Goitrogens: the drug and diet history

Vegetables
The brassica family: cabbage, kale and rape, which contain thiocyanate.B&L 55 · p.856Das 27 · p.376
Drugs
Para-aminosalicylic acid (PAS) and the antithyroid drugs themselves.B&L 55 · p.856 S Das adds the sulphonylureas.Das 27 · p.376
And the mechanism, so you can say which step is blocked
Thiocyanates and perchlorates interfere with iodide trapping. Carbimazole and the thiouracils interfere with the oxidation of iodide and the binding of iodine to tyrosine.B&L 55 · p.856 Those are two different steps of the four-step synthesis, and naming the step is the difference between a pass and a good pass.
Hormone synthesis in four steps, worth memorising as a list Trapping of inorganic iodide from the blood → oxidation of iodide to iodine → binding of iodine with tyrosine to form iodotyrosine → coupling of monoiodotyrosines and di-iodotyrosines to form T3 and T4.B&L 55 · p.852 Then: only the small free fraction is biologically active, 0.3% of T3 and 0.03% of T4. T3 is the more important physiological hormone, is also made peripherally from T4, and acts within a few hours, where T4 takes 4–14 days.B&L 55 · p.852 SRB gives the same free fractions with values: free T3 3–9 nmol/L, free T4 8–26 nmol/L.SRB 6 · p.449

The natural history: how a diffuse goitre becomes a nodular one

This is the single most answerable "pathogenesis" question in the thyroid case, and both B&L and SRB give it as a sequence. Learn it as a chain, because that is how it is asked.

1. Diffuse hyperplasia
Persistent growth stimulation; all lobules composed of active follicles; iodine uptake uniform. This stage is reversible if stimulation ceases.B&L 55 · p.856 SRB draws the practical conclusion: because it is reversible, L-thyroxine is beneficial.SRB 6 · p.451
2. Mixed pattern
Fluctuating stimulation produces areas of active and areas of inactive lobules.B&L 55 · p.856 SRB adds the second mechanism: increased sensitivity of follicular cells to TSH.SRB 6 · p.451
3. Haemorrhage and necrosis
Active lobules become more vascular and hyperplastic until haemorrhage occurs, causing central necrosis and leaving only a surrounding rind of active follicles.B&L 55 · p.856
4. Nodule formation
Necrotic lobules coalesce into nodules filled with iodine-free colloid or inactive follicles.B&L 55 · p.856
5. Multinodular goitre
Repetition produces a nodular goitre in which most nodules are inactive and the active follicles are in the internodular tissue.B&L 55 · p.856SRB 6 · p.451 That last clause explains two separate things: why a toxic nodular goitre is toxic from its internodular tissue, and why an isotope scan of such a gland looks patchy.
SRB's three stages, if you prefer a shorter version Stage of hyperplasia and hypertrophy → stage of fluctuation in TSH → stage of formation of nodules, the nodules inactive and the internodular tissue active.SRB 6 · p.451 And its definition of a colloid goitre: long-standing iodine deficiency with localised accumulation of significant colloid in the gland.SRB 6 · p.451 B&L agrees it is the late stage of diffuse hyperplasia, when TSH stimulation has fallen off and many follicles are inactive and full of colloid.B&L 55 · p.856
Why women
All types of simple goitre are more common in the female because of oestrogen receptors in thyroid tissue.B&L 55 · p.856 SRB gives the ratio for multinodular goitre as 10:1 female, in middle age, and adds the line that should change your threshold in a male patient: thyroid enlargement in a male is rare, and a nodule in a male could be malignant.SRB 6 · p.451
When nodules appear
Early in endemic goitre; between 20 and 30 years in sporadic goitre, although the patient may be unaware of the goitre until the late forties or fifties.B&L 55 · p.856 This is why "how long have you had it" and "when did you first notice it" are different questions.
Dyshormonogenesis
Enzyme deficiencies of varying severity account for many sporadic goitres in non-endemic areas; there is often a family history, suggesting a genetic defect.B&L 55 · p.855 So ask about family, not only about geography. Environment can mask it: goitre is almost unknown in Iceland, where the fish diet is rich in iodine.B&L 55 · p.855
TSH is not the only stimulus
Other growth factors, including immunoglobulins, exert an influence, and the characteristic nodularity may be due to clones of cells particularly sensitive to growth stimulation.B&L 55 · p.855SRB 6 · p.451

3History, and why each question earns its place

Particulars

Age, sex, occupation and residence? Why residenceGoitre belts. S Das names the Himalayas, the Vindyas and the Satpuda.Das 27 · p.374 B&L names the mountain ranges and the river valleys.B&L 55 · p.855 A patient from an endemic area with a multinodular goitre has a different prior probability from a city patient with a solitary nodule.
Why ageThe risk of carcinoma is increased at either end of the age range, and a discrete swelling in a teenager of either sex must be provisionally diagnosed as carcinoma.B&L 55 · p.859 At the other end, prognosis in differentiated cancer worsens with age, and the AJCC staging system turns on whether the patient is over or under 55.B&L 55 · p.869
Why sexThe incidence of thyroid carcinoma in women is about three times that in men, but a discrete swelling in a male is much more likely to be malignant than in a female.B&L 55 · p.859 Hold both halves of that sentence: more cancers happen in women, but any given nodule is more worrying in a man. SRB puts it bluntly: a nodule in a male could be malignant.SRB 6 · p.451
Why occupationVoice. A teacher, a singer or a lawyer must be warned that any thyroid operation will change the voice even without nerve trauma.B&L 55 · p.865 That is a consent issue you can raise unprompted.
Any radiation exposure in childhood? Any family history of thyroid disease or thyroid cancer? Rules inB&L: radiation exposure and family history should be discussed at the first encounter in any suspected thyroid neoplasm.B&L 55 · p.868 The most important identifiable aetiological factor in differentiated thyroid carcinoma, particularly papillary, is irradiation of the thyroid under the age of 5.B&L 55 · p.867
AlsoFamily history matters twice over: 55% of Graves' patients have a family history of autoimmune endocrine disease,B&L 55 · p.861 and dyshormonogenetic goitre often runs in families.B&L 55 · p.855 And in medullary carcinoma, genetic screening of relatives is part of the management.B&L 55 · p.871

The swelling itself

How long, and has it changed recently? Did it appear suddenly? Rules inA painful nodule, sudden appearance or rapid enlargement raises the suspicion of carcinoma, but is usually due to haemorrhage into a simple nodule.B&L 55 · p.857 Say both halves: the worry and the likelier truth.
WhySRB agrees that a recent increase in size signifies malignant transformation or haemorrhage.SRB 6 · p.452
And the pattern of bleeding into a cystSudden painful swelling that resolves to a variable extent over weeks if untreated. Aspiration yields altered blood, but reaccumulation is frequent.B&L 55 · p.859
Rules outA multinodular goitre is slowly progressive with many years of history.SRB 6 · p.452 A story of months, not years, in a hard fixed gland in an elderly patient is anaplastic carcinoma until proved otherwise.B&L 55 · p.868
Is there pain? Does it go anywhere? Rules inPain referred to the ear is suggestive of nerve involvement from an infiltrating tumour.B&L 55 · p.868 This is a question most candidates never ask and it has a specific, sinister answer.
Also considerPain with a tender gland and a short history points to thyroiditis rather than neoplasia; de Quervain's granulomatous and the acute bacterial and viral thyroiditides are in the inflammatory arm of B&L's classification.B&L 55 · p.855

Pressure symptoms: four structures, four questions

This section answers two of SRB's seven: compression on trachea or recurrent nerve, and retrosternal extension.SRB 6 · p.451

Any difficulty breathing, especially lying flat or at night? Any noisy breathing? Rules inTrachea. B&L: tracheal obstruction may be from gross lateral displacement or from compression in a lateral or anteroposterior plane by retrosternal extension.B&L 55 · p.857 S Das's word for the resulting sound is stridor, and he teaches you to look for it.Das 27 · p.375
The emergencyAcute respiratory obstruction may follow haemorrhage into a nodule impacted in the thoracic inlet.B&L 55 · p.857 That is the one way a benign goitre kills quickly, and it is worth volunteering.
Any difficulty swallowing? Solids or liquids? Why the answer is usually noS Das gives the reason, and it is a favourite viva point: the oesophagus is rarely obstructed because it is a muscular tube that yields to pressure.Das 27 · p.375 So dysphagia from a goitre is uncommon and, when present, needs explaining.
When it does happenB&L: as a retrosternal gland enlarges within the thoracic inlet, where there is no room to yield, pressure may lead to dysphagia, tracheal compression and eventually airway symptoms.B&L 55 · p.860 Note the order: in the neck the trachea suffers first, in the thoracic inlet the oesophagus can too.
Rules in something worseDysphagia in a hard, fixed gland is infiltration, not compression, and that changes the diagnosis.
Has your voice changed? Is it hoarse? Do you choke on fluids? Rules inRecurrent laryngeal nerve. B&L: RLN paralysis, suggested by hoarseness and a non-occlusive cough and confirmed by laryngoscopy, is almost pathognomonic of malignancy in a thyroid swelling.B&L 55 · p.859 The phrase to use is "almost pathognomonic", because it is the book's own.
SoThis single answer can convert a reassuring nodule into a cancer, and it is free. Ask it early.
And thenConfirm it: flexible laryngoscopy has rendered indirect laryngoscopy obsolete and is used preoperatively to determine cord mobility.B&L 55 · p.859
Any drooping eyelid, small pupil or dry face on one side? Rules inCervical sympathetic chain. S Das teaches looking for Horner's syndrome, which indicates infiltration of the sympathetic chain.Das 27 · p.381 Rare, but when present it is malignant disease and nothing else.
A warning about the book, not the patientIn the scanned text the eponym is printed "Homer's", because the OCR reads rn as m. The passage is on that page even though a search for "Horner" misses it. The same substitution turns Dalrymple into something unrecognisable.

Functional status: the half of the history that decides treatment

This answers the first and the last of SRB's seven. Ask both directions, because a patient volunteers neither.SRB 6 · p.451

Toxic side: weight loss despite a good appetite? Palpitations? Heat intolerance? Sweating? Tremor? Diarrhoea? Irritability or poor sleep? In women, menstrual change? Why phrase it that wayWeight loss with preserved or increased appetite is the discriminating version of the question; weight loss with anorexia points elsewhere.
DistinguishesS Das's framework is the one to use: in primary thyrotoxicosis the brunt falls on the nervous system, in secondary thyrotoxicosis on the cardiovascular system.Das 27 · p.375, p.385 So a young woman with tremor, irritability and sweating is probably primary; an older patient whose first complaint is palpitation, breathlessness or ankle swelling is probably secondary.
And heat intolerance is specificS Das: the palms are hot and moist, the patient cannot tolerate hot weather, and tolerance to cold is increased.Das 27 · p.384
Myxoedema side: cold intolerance? Weight gain? Constipation? Dry skin and hair? Hoarse voice? Lethargy or slow thinking? Menorrhagia? WhyS Das sets out the symptoms of myxoedema as a counterpart list, and the examiner may well hand you a hypothyroid goitre.Das 27 · p.376
Why it matters mechanicallyAutoimmune thyroiditis may present with toxicity, failure or a euthyroid goitre, so you cannot infer function from morphology.B&L 55 · p.852 You have to ask, and then you have to measure.
In a multinodular goitre, have you ever had a spell of palpitations and weight loss that settled? Rules inTransient episodes of mild hyperthyroidism are common, occurring in up to 30% of patients with simple nodular goitre.B&L 55 · p.857 SRB's figure for secondary thyrotoxicosis complicating a multinodular goitre is the same 30%.SRB 6 · p.452
SoA currently euthyroid patient with a past toxic episode is not a diagnostic puzzle; it is the natural history of the disease in front of you.
Any bone pain, cough, breathlessness or abdominal swelling? Rules inSRB's seventh item: systemic features of malignant spread to bone, liver or lungs.SRB 6 · p.451
Why bone specificallyFollicular carcinoma spreads by the bloodstream far more than papillary does, and the eventual mortality, though still low, is twice that of papillary.B&L 55 · p.868 A pathological fracture or a skull lump with a thyroid swelling is a follicular carcinoma.
ContrastDistant metastases are uncommon in papillary carcinoma, which spreads instead to lymph nodes.B&L 55 · p.868
Any previous neck operation or neck irradiation? Any drugs, including iodine, amiodarone, lithium or antithyroid drugs? WhyA previous thyroid operation changes everything. B&L: reoperation for recurrent nodular goitre is more difficult and hazardous, and a second operation years later greatly increases the risk to the RLN and parathyroid glands.B&L 55 · p.857, p.860 SRB agrees: surgery for recurrent multinodular goitre is difficult from loss of anatomical plane and fibrosis, so total thyroidectomy is often advised.SRB 6 · p.453
And look for the scarSRB illustrates exactly this: a recurrent nodular thyroid with the scar of previous surgery.SRB 6 · p.452 Inspect the neck before you accept the history.

4Examination, and what each finding settles

Read this before you quote a book at the bedside Bailey & Love 28e does not teach thyroid clinical examination. Its chapter 55 contains no eye signs, no exophthalmos, no stridor, no deglutition test and none of the eponymous manoeuvres; it goes from physiology straight to ultrasound, cytology and operation. Everything in this section is S Das and SRB. That is not a criticism of either book, it is a division of labour, and knowing it saves you from hunting for Pemberton's sign in the wrong index. Where B&L does contribute here, it is anatomy: Berry's ligament, Beahrs' triangle and the tubercle of Zuckerkandl.B&L 55 · p.852

General inspection, before the neck

Build, facies, mental state, pulse, skin
S Das's own opening sequence, and it is a functional assessment disguised as an inspection.Das 27 · p.376
Pulse, and then the hands
Tachycardia without a rise in temperature is constantly present in primary toxic goitre. A rapid irregular pulse should arouse suspicion of atrial fibrillation.Das 27 · p.384 S Das makes it a routine practice to feel the hands just after feeling the pulse, because moist warm palms are part of the same finding.Das 27 · p.384
The sleeping pulse
More confirmatory than the waking pulse.Das 27 · p.384 S Das grades it: under 90 mild, 90–110 moderate, over 110 severe.Das 27 · p.376
Tremor, in two places
A fine tremor, almost always present in primary thyrotoxicosis. Arms straight out, fingers spread. Then ask the patient to put the tongue out and hold it for half a minute: fibrillary twitching will be seen.Das 27 · p.384 The tongue is the half everyone forgets.

Inspection of the neck

Pizzillo's method, when the neck is fat or short
Hands clasped behind the head, pushing the head backwards against them, which makes the thyroid more prominent.Das 27 · p.377SRB 6 · p.452
Does it move on deglutition?
Give the patient water. Movement on swallowing is the test that the swelling is thyroid.Das 27 · p.377 Know the mechanism: Berry's ligament binds the gland to the cricoid, and the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus pull the cartilages up.SRB 6 · p.452B&L 55 · p.852
What else moves on deglutition, so you do not over-claim
Anything attached to the larynx or trachea: a thyroglossal cyst, a prelaryngeal lymph node, a subhyoid bursa.Das 27 · p.377
Does it move on protruding the tongue?
A thyroglossal cyst does; a goitre does not. The embryology is the reason: the thyroglossal duct runs from the foramen caecum at the junction of the anterior two thirds and posterior third of the tongue, and passes in close continuity with, and sometimes through, the developing hyoid cartilage.Das 27 · p.378B&L 55 · p.850
Dilated veins over the upper chest
Suggests retrosternal extension with obstruction of the great veins.Das 27 · p.377 This is SRB's sixth item answered by inspection alone.SRB 6 · p.451
A scar
Previous thyroid surgery, and therefore a recurrent goitre.SRB 6 · p.452 Look for it before you are told.

Palpation

From behind, with the neck slightly flexed
The standard position for assessing the whole gland and counting nodules.Das 27 · p.378SRB 6 · p.451
Lahey's method
Push the gland from one side so the opposite lobe rotates forwards, where it can be felt.Das 27 · p.379 It is the manoeuvre for a lobe you cannot otherwise reach.
Crile's method
Palpation with the thumb while the patient swallows. Note the eponym carefully: see the disagreement below, because SRB uses the name Crile for something entirely different.Das 27 · p.379
Get below the gland
If you cannot feel the lower border, even on swallowing, suspect retrosternal extension.Das 27 · p.379
Consistency, and what each means
S Das maps consistency to pathology.Das 27 · p.379 B&L's warning goes with it: nodules are smooth, usually firm and not hard, but hardness and irregularity due to calcification may simulate carcinoma,B&L 55 · p.857 and conversely hard texture alone is not reliable because tense cystic swellings may be suspiciously hard.B&L 55 · p.859 SRB completes the pair: a calcified nodule becomes harder, necrosis softens it.SRB 6 · p.452
What is reliable
B&L: a hard, irregular swelling with any apparent fixity, which is unusual, is highly suspicious.B&L 55 · p.859 It is fixity, not hardness, that carries the weight.
The carotid, and what it tells you
S Das's sign: a benign swelling pushes the carotid backwards, so the pulse is still felt behind it; a malignant swelling engulfs the artery, and the pulse is lost.Das 27 · p.381 This answers SRB's fifth item, carotid infiltration, with one finger.SRB 6 · p.451
Lymph nodes, in a defined order
SRB's third item.SRB 6 · p.451 Palpate the deep cervical chain along the internal jugular vein. B&L tells you what it means: cervical lymphadenopathy along the internal jugular vein in association with a clinically suspicious swelling is almost diagnostic of papillary thyroid carcinoma.B&L 55 · p.859 Know the levels, because B&L describes drainage in them: level VI central compartment, including the Delphian and paratracheal nodes, then levels II to V deep cervical, then level VII mediastinal.B&L 55 · p.850
A "lateral aberrant thyroid" is not a thyroid
SRB is explicit that the term is a misnomer: a mass of tissue with the structure of thyroid lying lateral in the neck is a metastatic lymph node from an occult papillary carcinoma.SRB 6 · p.448 S Das teaches the same conclusion.Das 27 · p.382 Never call a lateral neck nodule aberrant thyroid tissue in a viva; call it a secondary and go looking for the primary.

Kocher's test, and the trachea

Compress the lateral lobes gently and listen. Rules inIf stridor appears, the test is positive: the trachea is already compressed or softened.Das 27 · p.380, p.381 SRB: a positive Kocher's test is due to compression over the trachea, that is tracheomalacia or scabbard trachea, in a long-standing multinodular goitre.SRB 6 · p.452

GentlyThis is a test that can precipitate the obstruction it is looking for. S Das's word is compress, not squeeze.
Scabbard tracheaLateral compression from both sides, narrowing the trachea side to side.Das 27 · p.381 On imaging B&L shows the same thing as tracheal deviation or compression in a lateral or anteroposterior plane, and says that if there are swallowing or breathing symptoms a CT of neck and chest is the best modality to assess it.B&L 55 · p.857

Pemberton's sign

Ask the patient to raise both arms until they touch the sides of the head, and hold. Rules inRetrosternal goitre. After some seconds the face becomes suffused and cyanosed, with distress and dyspnoea, because the raised arms draw the thoracic inlet upwards onto the goitre and obstruct the great veins.Das 27 · p.381
SoIt answers SRB's sixth item without imaging.SRB 6 · p.451 Then confirm it properly: B&L says retrosternal extension can often be predicted on a plain chest radiograph but requires more advanced techniques, usually CT, to determine the extent adequately before planning management.B&L 55 · p.853

Percussion and auscultation

Percuss the upper sternum
Dullness extending below the suprasternal notch supports retrosternal extension.Das 27 · p.382
Auscultate for a bruit, at the upper poles
A systolic bruit over the gland indicates a very vascular gland. The eyes

Get the first distinction right or the rest does not count Lid retraction is when the upper lid is higher than normal while the lower lid is in its normal position, from overactivity of the smooth muscle part of levator palpebrae superioris. Lid lag is a different thing: the upper lid cannot keep pace with the eyeball as the patient follows your finger downwards. Neither of them is exophthalmos.Das 27 · p.383 Exophthalmos is the eyeball pushed forwards by increased fat, oedema or cellular infiltration in the retro-orbital space, so that sclera becomes visible below the lower edge of the iris first, and then above the upper edge.Das 27 · p.383 Candidates lose marks here more often than anywhere else in the case.
von Graefe's sign
The upper eyelid lags behind the eyeball as the patient looks downwards.Das 27 · p.383
Joffroy's sign
Absence of wrinkling of the forehead when the patient looks upwards with the face inclined downwards.Das 27 · p.383
Stellwag's sign
Staring look and infrequent blinking with widening of the palpebral fissure, from toxic contraction of the striated fibres of levator palpebrae superioris.Das 27 · p.383–384
Moebius' sign
Failure of convergence of the eyeballs.Das 27 · p.384
Dalrymple's sign
Upper sclera visible because of retraction of the upper lid.Das 27 · p.384 In the scanned text the name is mangled by the same rn to m OCR fault that turns Horner into Homer; the sign is there.
Kocher's sign, which is an eye sign and nothing to do with the trachea

5Investigations, and why each one

Which thyroid function test do you ask for first? S Das 27 p.386 teaches PBI and T3 resin uptake; SRB 6 p.449 says free T3; B&L 55 p.853 says TSH
S Das
Teaches protein-bound iodine, serum thyroxine and the T3 resin uptake.Das 27 · p.386 These are obsolete. The book is a clinical-methods text of long standing and this is the one section where its age shows. SRB still quotes a PBI value too, 8 µg/100 mL.SRB 6 · p.449
SRB
Free T3 is "the best single test in assessing hyperthyroidism", free T3 being 0.3% of the total at 3–9 nmol/L and free T4 0.03% at 8–26 nmol/L.SRB 6 · p.449 It also notes correctly that in T3 toxicosis, where T3 alone is raised, T4 is normal and TSH is suppressed, free T3 estimation is what makes the diagnosis.SRB 6 · p.459, p.463
Bailey & Love 28e
Summary box 55.1 opens with "Serum: TSH (T3 and T4 if abnormal)", that is TSH first, and the others only if TSH is deranged.B&L 55 · p.853 The reason is the pituitary-thyroid axis: TSH is suppressed in hyperthyroidism and stimulated in hypothyroidism, so it is the most sensitive single indicator.B&L 55 · p.852 It adds the detail that TSH can be measured accurately down to very low concentrations with an immunochemiluminometric assay, which is what makes a TSH-first strategy possible at all.B&L 55 · p.852

The investigations, in the order you would ask for them

1. Serum TSH, with free T4 and free T3 if abnormal; and thyroid autoantibodies
Function first, because function decides treatment. Antibodies against thyroid peroxidase and thyroglobulin are useful in determining the cause of dysfunction and of the swelling: above 25 units/mL for TPO antibody and titres above 1:100 for antithyroglobulin are significant, although a proportion of patients with histological thyroiditis are seronegative.B&L 55 · p.852–853
The two consequences nobody mentionsFirst, antithyroglobulin antibody interferes with assays of serum thyroglobulin, which matters because thyroglobulin is the tumour marker used to follow thyroid cancer.B&L 55 · p.853 Second, the presence of circulating antibodies increases the risk of thyroid failure after lobectomy,B&L 55 · p.858 so it is a test that changes the consent conversation.
2. Corrected serum calcium
On B&L's list of standard thyroid investigations.B&L 55 · p.853 A baseline before any thyroidectomy, because the postoperative question will be whether the calcium has fallen.
3. Ultrasound: the workhorse
B&L: "the workhorse investigation in thyroid disease for the surgeon is ultrasonography", and it should be the primary investigation of any thyroid nodule, because a reassuring appearance removes the need for cytology.B&L 55 · p.853, p.858
What it reportsNumber, size, shape, margins, vascularity and specific features such as microcalcification, which together predict the risk of malignancy in a given nodule; and the regional lymphatics, particularly in the lateral neck.B&L 55 · p.853 SRB's benign-versus-malignant ultrasound features cover the same ground.SRB 6 · p.450
AdvantagesNo ionising radiation, non-invasive, cheap, dynamic, and it lets FNA be done more accurately than free-hand technique.B&L 55 · p.853
LimitsVisualisation of the central neck nodes, in particular those behind the sternum, is limited, which is why detected metastatic disease then needs cross-sectional imaging.B&L 55 · p.853 And only macroscopic capsular breach and nodal involvement are diagnostic of malignancy; microcalcification and vascularity merely raise suspicion.B&L 55 · p.858
4. Fine-needle aspiration cytology, ideally ultrasound-guided
The investigation of choice in discrete thyroid swellings: excellent compliance, simple, quick in the outpatient department, readily repeated, and now routine worldwide after being developed in Scandinavia.B&L 55 · p.855 Use it on all nodules that do not fulfil a fully benign (U2) classification on ultrasonography.B&L 55 · p.858
The limitation you must stateFNAC is reliable for papillary carcinoma but cannot distinguish a benign follicular adenoma from a follicular carcinoma, because that distinction depends not on cytology but on histological criteria: capsular and vascular invasion.B&L 55 · p.858 Say this before you are asked; it is the single most important thing about the test.
Report it properlyThe Thy system: Thy1 non-diagnostic, Thy1c non-diagnostic cystic, Thy2 non-neoplastic, Thy3 follicular, Thy4 suspicious of malignancy, Thy5 malignant.B&L 55 · p.855 SRB adds that the risk of malignancy in a follicular lesion of undetermined significance is 10%.SRB 6 · p.456
5. Chest radiograph and thoracic inlet view
Only if there is tracheal deviation or a retrosternal goitre.B&L 55 · p.853 Not a routine film in every goitre, which is how it is often wrongly offered.
6. CT
The modality of choice for retrosternal extent, for tracheal or oesophageal deviation or compression where there are swallowing or breathing symptoms, and for surgical planning when ultrasound has found nodal disease, because it also assesses the superior mediastinum and lungs. Rapid acquisition minimises breathing artefact and the lung fields are seen at the same time.B&L 55 · p.853, p.857, p.858 Contrast-enhanced CT is useful for the extent of airway invasion.B&L 55 · p.854
A myth B&L explicitly puts down"Concerns over the impact of iodine-containing contrast on delays to radioactive iodine therapy have been overplayed", and it is more important that the surgeon knows the true extent of disease before operating.B&L 55 · p.868 Quote that if someone tells you contrast is contraindicated.
7. MRI
Superior to CT at determining prevertebral fascia invasion.B&L 55 · p.854 Otherwise reserved for known cancer, some reoperations and some retrosternal goitres.B&L 55 · p.853
8. Isotope scan: one indication only
Routine isotope scanning is unnecessary and inappropriate for distinguishing benign from malignant, because 80% of "cold" swellings are benign and 5% of functioning or "warm" swellings will be malignant.B&L 55 · p.854 It "has been abandoned except when toxicity is associated with nodularity".B&L 55 · p.858
The one indicationThe toxic patient with a nodule or nodularity, where localising the overactivity distinguishes a toxic nodule with suppression of the rest of the gland from a toxic multinodular goitre with several areas of increased uptake, "with important implications for therapy".B&L 55 · p.854 Low-dose 123I or the cheaper 99mTc.B&L 55 · p.854 And remember hot nodules are very rarely malignant.B&L 55 · p.868
9. Serum calcitonin
On B&L's standard list, with carcinoembryonic antigen as an alternative screening test for medullary cancer.B&L 55 · p.853 Calcitonin comes from the parafollicular C cells, which are of neuroendocrine origin and reach the thyroid via the ultimobranchial body.B&L 55 · p.850, p.852
10. Flexible laryngoscopy
Preoperatively, to determine vocal cord mobility. It has rendered indirect laryngoscopy obsolete.B&L 55 · p.859 Do it before surgery or you will not know afterwards whether you caused the palsy.
11. Core biopsy: rarely
Rarely indicated because of the vascularity of the gland and the risk of post-procedure haemorrhage. It is useful for the rapid diagnosis of widely invasive disease, for example anaplastic carcinoma, or for lymphadenopathy.B&L 55 · p.859 In a rapidly growing fixed mass, core or even open biopsy may be required, because distinguishing anaplastic carcinoma, which is rapidly fatal and managed palliatively, from lymphoma or thyroiditis, which respond to treatment, is critical.B&L 55 · p.868
12. PET, and whole-body iodine scanning
PET has limited application; consider it in recurrent thyroid cancer that does not concentrate iodine, where FDG uptake rises and lesions become positive.B&L 55 · p.854 Whole-body iodine scanning demonstrates metastases, but all normally functioning thyroid tissue must first be ablated by surgery or radioiodine, because metastatic thyroid cancer cannot compete with normal thyroid in taking up iodine.B&L 55 · p.854 That sentence is the reason total thyroidectomy precedes radioiodine, and it is worth having verbatim.
The cyst, which is its own small case Over 30% of clinically isolated swellings contain fluid. Tense cysts may be hard and mimic carcinoma. About 55% are colloid degeneration or of uncertain aetiology; most of the rest are involution in follicular adenomas; and 10–15% of cystic follicular swellings are histologically malignant, 30% in men and 10% in women.B&L 55 · p.859 Papillary carcinoma is often associated with cyst formation. Ultrasound is the most useful tool: no discernible solid element means the cyst is almost certainly benign and needs no further investigation; a solid element should be targeted with guided FNAC. Simple aspiration has a high rate of reaccumulation, but ablation with ethanol or a thermal probe achieves resolution in up to 90% and should be considered for a recurrent symptomatic cyst.B&L 55 · p.859

6Treatment of the goitre itself

When not to operate

Prevention first
In endemic areas the incidence of goitre has been strikingly reduced by the introduction of iodised salt.B&L 55 · p.857 Worth one sentence in any Indian viva.
The reversible stage
In the early stages a hyperplastic goitre may regress if thyroxine is given at 0.15–0.2 mg daily for a few months.B&L 55 · p.857 SRB says the same in one line: diffuse hyperplastic goitre is a reversible stage, so L-thyroxine is beneficial.SRB 6 · p.451
The irreversible stage still often needs nothing
Although the nodular stage is irreversible, more than half of benign nodules will regress in size over 10 years, and most patients with multinodular goitre are asymptomatic and do not require operation.B&L 55 · p.857

Indications for operation

In a nodular goitre
Features of underlying malignancy; pressure symptoms once other causes are excluded; cosmesis if the patient finds the goitre unsightly; and tracheal compression, where surgery should be considered.B&L 55 · p.857
And the counterweightMany such patients are found incidentally, asymptomatic and often very elderly, and these goitres often grow very slowly, so the risks and benefits should be considered carefully, particularly if a sternal split may be required for access.B&L 55 · p.857
In a discrete swelling
The main indication is the risk of neoplasia, which includes follicular adenoma as well as frank malignancy. The reason for removing all follicular neoplasms is that it is seldom possible to distinguish adenoma from carcinoma cytologically.B&L 55 · p.859 Even with negative cytology, age, sex and the size of the swelling may be relative indications, especially when a large swelling causes symptoms.B&L 55 · p.859
The printed listThy3 to Thy5 cytology; age; male sex; hard texture; fixity; recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy; lymphadenopathy; recurrent cyst; pressure symptoms; cosmesis; and the patient's wishes.B&L 55 · p.860
In a retrosternal goitre
Consider surgery if there is significant airway compression, if symptoms are present, or in young patients in whom symptoms are likely to develop. In elderly patients with an incidentally discovered retrosternal goitre, most surgeons would observe rather than treat prophylactically.B&L 55 · p.860
Retrosternal goitre: the numbers that decide the incision More than 95% of retrosternal goitres can be removed transcervically. Those most likely to need conversion to sternotomy are: malignant disease; revision surgery; goitres extending into the posterior mediastinum; and those whose diameter exceeds that of the thoracic inlet. In such cases plan a joint case with thoracic surgery.B&L 55 · p.860 Two technical points worth quoting: the blood supply comes from the neck, which reduces the risk of catastrophic bleeding from the great vessels; and if the goitre arose from a posteriorly placed nodule the RLN may be displaced anteriorly, so apparent fascial bands over the gland must not be divided casually.B&L 55 · p.861 And a small practical one: when reading the CT, check the arm position, because arms up for a standard chest CT puts the thyroid in a very different place from arms down with the neck extended.B&L 55 · p.860–861

Which operation, and the vocabulary for naming it

All thyroid operations from three building blocks B&L's summary box, and the cleanest way to answer "what operations are there?": the three elements are total lobectomy, isthmusectomy and subtotal lobectomy. Then: lobectomy = total lobectomy + isthmusectomy · total thyroidectomy = 2 × total lobectomy + isthmusectomy · subtotal thyroidectomy = 2 × subtotal lobectomy + isthmusectomy · near-total thyroidectomy = total lobectomy + isthmusectomy + subtotal lobectomy, which is the Dunhill procedure.B&L 55 · p.860 SRB names the same operation Hartley Dunhill: removal of one entire lateral lobe with the isthmus and partial or subtotal resection of the opposite lobe, about 4 g retained.SRB 6 · p.453, p.479, p.481
What the choice depends on
B&L lists it: diagnosis if known preoperatively; risk of thyroid failure; risk of RLN injury; risk of recurrence; Graves' disease; multinodular goitre; differentiated thyroid cancer; risk of hypoparathyroidism.B&L 55 · p.860
The fact that makes lobectomy attractive
In two-thirds of patients with negative antithyroid antibodies, one thyroid lobe will maintain normal function.B&L 55 · p.860 Said the other way round, around one in three patients who has a lobectomy will require supplementation, and rates are higher with positive antibodies.B&L 55 · p.865
In asymmetrical multinodular goitre
Total lobectomy on the more affected side is the appropriate management, particularly in older patients. It can be combined with subtotal resection of the other lobe, the Dunhill procedure, but most surgeons now prefer no intervention on the less affected side, because of regrowth and the higher complication rate of reoperation.B&L 55 · p.857 And the strategic reason to leave it alone: it may be preferable to leave the least affected lobe untouched to permit a straightforward lobectomy in the future rather than to do subtotal resections on both sides.B&L 55 · p.860
In bilateral disease
Unless there is a local shortage of thyroxine, most surgeons now favour total thyroidectomy.B&L 55 · p.857 Note the conditional: B&L itself makes the availability of replacement thyroxine the deciding factor, which is exactly the point of difference with Indian practice. SRB: when the entire gland is diseased, total thyroidectomy is a better option, and for recurrent multinodular goitre total thyroidectomy is often advised.SRB 6 · p.453
And the incidental benefit
Total lobectomy and total thyroidectomy have the additional advantage of being therapeutic for incidental carcinomas.B&L 55 · p.857

The operation itself, enough to describe it

The aim, in one sentence
Remove the entire thyroid lobe, bilaterally if total, encompassing all disease and preserving the cervical strap muscles, the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, the RLN, and the parathyroid glands and their blood supply, while minimising cosmetic impact.B&L 55 · p.863
Position
Supine, arms at the side, head on a ring, neck extended with a shoulder roll or by extending the head of the table with the bed head raised (reverse Trendelenburg); field prepared from lower lip to upper chest.B&L 55 · p.863
The nerve, in three places
It can be found in the tracheo-oesophageal groove, where it forms one side of Beahrs' triangle with the carotid artery and the inferior thyroid artery; under the tubercle of Zuckerkandl, the most posterolateral part of the gland, as the lobe is mobilised laterally; and at the cricothyroid joint.B&L 55 · p.852 SRB's framing of the same thing as the three critical points of RLN relation is the Indian exam version.SRB 6 · p.482, p.483
Why the two sides differ
The nerve recurs round the arch of the aorta on the left and the subclavian artery on the right. So on the left it has more distance in which to reach the groove and runs in a medial plane; on the right there is less distance and it runs more obliquely. And approximately 2% of nerves on the right are non-recurrent and enter the larynx from above.B&L 55 · p.851 That 2% is a classic viva question and it is right-sided.
Berry's ligament: the dangerous moment
The nerve enters the larynx at the cricothyroid joint, at the level of Berry's ligament, and this is the point at which the nerve is at most risk of injury during surgery.B&L 55 · p.852 B&L's operative warning: if the nerve lies medially it may actually lie on the thyroid, and small vessels within the ligament retract if not controlled, so great care must be taken at this point.B&L 55 · p.864
Nerve monitoring
Intermittent monitoring identifies a damaged nerve. Continuous monitoring theoretically identifies a nerve whose function is threatened, for example by excessive traction, but requires an electrode on the vagus nerve and has not gained widespread acceptance.B&L 55 · p.865
Minimally invasive and remote-access approaches
Video-assisted techniques allow an incision under 2 cm but are only appropriate for small-volume disease. Remote access through axillary, breast or transoral incisions, with laparoscopic or robotic instruments, requires extended dissection over the chest wall or neck, so B&L calls these "maximally invasive" and notes the significant extra time that currently limits their application.B&L 55 · p.865
Sutures
Use absorbable ligatures and sutures throughout, because non-absorbable material, particularly silk, causes stitch granuloma with or without sinus formation.B&L 55 · p.866

First, which kind of toxicity

TypeMechanismClinical
Diffuse toxic goitre
Graves' disease		 Whole of the functioning thyroid involved. Hypertrophy and hyperplasia due to TSH receptor antibodies binding TSH receptor sites with a disproportionate and prolonged effect. Why the word is thyrotoxicosis and not hyperthyroidism B&L keeps the older term deliberately: "the term thyrotoxicosis is retained because hyperthyroidism, i.e. symptoms due to a raised level of circulating thyroid hormones, is not responsible for all manifestations of the disease".B&L 55 · p.861 The eye disease is the obvious example: it is autoimmune, not hormonal, which is why it can get worse after the hormones are controlled.

The three treatments, each with its own advantages and disadvantages

Antithyroid drugs: carbimazole and propylthiouracil
Used to restore the patient to euthyroid and hold them there in the hope of a permanent remission, that is, that TSH receptor antibody production diminishes or ceases.
Cannot work inA toxic nodule. The overactive tissue is autonomous, so recurrence of the hyperthyroidism is certain when the drug is discontinued.B&L 55 · p.862 That is a complete, mechanism-based answer to "why not just give drugs?"
AdvantagesNo surgery, no radioactive materials.
DisadvantagesTreatment is prolonged and the failure rate is at least 55%. Duration can be tailored to severity: 6 months for mild cases, 2 years for severe before stopping.B&L 55 · p.862
Surgery
In diffuse toxic goitre and toxic nodular goitre with overactive internodular tissue, surgery cures by reducing the mass of overactive tissue below a critical mass. In an autonomous toxic nodule, surgery cures by removing all the overactive tissue, which allows the suppressed normal tissue to function again.B&L 55 · p.862 Two different mechanisms for the same operation: say both.
AdvantagesThe goitre is removed, the cure is rapid, and the cure rate is high if surgery has been adequate.
DisadvantagesRecurrence in at least 5% of cases when subtotal thyroidectomy is carried out; risk of permanent hypoparathyroidism and nerve injury; and young women tend to have a poorer cosmetic result from the scar.B&L 55 · p.862
The principle to quoteThyroid failure should not be regarded as a failure of treatment, but recurrent toxicity is.B&L 55 · p.860 So in Graves' disease it is better to err on the side of removing too much thyroid tissue rather than too little.B&L 55 · p.860
Radioiodine
Destroys thyroid cells and, like surgery, reduces functioning mass below a critical level.
AdvantagesNo surgery, no prolonged drug therapy.
DisadvantagesIsotope facilities must be available; the patient must be quarantined while radiation levels are high and must avoid pregnancy and close physical contact, particularly with children; and eye signs may be aggravated.B&L 55 · p.862

Choosing between them

Graves' disease
Most patients have an initial course of antithyroid drugs with radioiodine for relapse. The exceptions, who go to surgery, are: those who refuse radiation; those with large goitres; those with progressive eye signs; and those who are pregnant.B&L 55 · p.862 Those four exceptions are the answer to "when would you operate in Graves' disease?"
Toxic nodular goitre
Often large and uncomfortable, and enlarges still further with antithyroid drugs. Large goitres should be treated surgically because they do not respond as well or as rapidly to radioiodine or drugs as a diffuse toxic goitre does.B&L 55 · p.862
Toxic nodule
Surgery or radioiodine. Resection is easy, certain and has limited morbidity. Radioiodine is a good alternative over the age of 45, because the suppressed surrounding tissue does not take up iodine and so there is minimal risk of delayed thyroid insufficiency.B&L 55 · p.862 That is an elegant piece of reasoning and examiners like it.
The caveat B&L itself adds
These principles "must be modified according to the facilities available and the personality and wishes of the individual patient", and access to post-treatment care and availability of replacement thyroxine can be important considerations in some areas.B&L 55 · p.862 The book is telling you to adapt it, which is permission worth quoting in an Indian exam.
Subtotal or total thyroidectomy for Graves' disease? SRB 6 p.465 teaches subtotal; B&L 55 p.860 tabulates the trade-off and p.865 says subtotal is all but abandoned
SRB
The operation is subtotal thyroidectomy: both lobes with the isthmus removed and a tissue equivalent to the pulp of a finger retained on each side.SRB 6 · p.465 Elsewhere it concedes total may be better, particularly for ophthalmopathy, because it clears the antigenic focus in the thyroid completely.SRB 6 · p.465
Bailey & Love 28e, the table
Table 55.5 sets the two side by side. Subtotal: return to euthyroid is variable, up to 12 months; lifelong risk of recurrence up to 5%; permanent hypoparathyroidism 5%; follow-up minimal. Total: thyroid failure 100%, so lifelong thyroxine; permanent hypoparathyroidism 1%; follow-up lifelong. With the footnote that matters: the risks of recurrence and late failure are a function of the size of the remnant as a proportion of total gland weight, so a large remnant in a small gland recurs and a small remnant in a large gland fails.B&L 55 · p.860
Bailey & Love 28e, the verdict
Blunter than the table: subtotal thyroidectomy was done to leave enough tissue to maintain function, "however, this is difficult to judge" and the benign process may recur, requiring difficult revision. "For this reason, the practice of subtotal thyroidectomy has been more or less abandoned outside environments where exogenous thyroxine is not available."B&L 55 · p.865
This is a genuine live disagreement, and the resolution is in B&L's own last clause. Answer it as a trade-off, not a preference. Say: total thyroidectomy eliminates recurrent toxicity at the price of guaranteed lifelong thyroxine and a higher rate of permanent hypoparathyroidism; subtotal preserves function in some but recurs in at least 5% and leaves a difficult reoperation. Then give the deciding principle: thyroid failure is not a treatment failure, recurrent toxicity is, so err towards removing too much.B&L 55 · p.860 Then land it where you are sitting the exam: the reason Indian textbooks still teach subtotal is precisely the condition Bailey & Love names, reliable lifelong access to thyroxine and to follow-up. Saying that shows you understand why two books can both be right about the same operation. If pressed for one answer in a young patient with ophthalmopathy, say total, and cite SRB's own reason: it clears the antigenic focus completely.SRB 6 · p.465

Preparing a toxic patient for surgery

What dose of carbimazole? SRB 6 p.464 gives two different regimes in one chapter; B&L 55 p.862 gives a third
SRB, first regime
5–10 mg exactly 8-hourly, "as the half-life of carbimazole is 8 hours", each tablet 5 mg, usually given for 12–18 months.SRB 6 · p.464
SRB, second regime, same chapter
20 mg three times a day, up to 120 mg.SRB 6 · p.464 That is up to eight times the first regime's daily dose.
Bailey & Love 28e
Carbimazole 30–40 mg/day is the drug of choice for preparation. When euthyroid, after 8–12 weeks, the dose may be reduced to 5 mg 8-hourly, or a "block and replace" regime used, in which the high dose is continued to inhibit T3 and T4 production and a maintenance dose of 0.1–0.15 mg thyroxine is given daily. The last dose of carbimazole may be given on the evening before surgery.B&L 55 · p.862
These are not really three answers; they are a high induction dose and a low maintenance dose, and SRB has printed both without labelling them. Answer it that way and the contradiction becomes your explanation: a higher dose to induce a euthyroid state, 30–40 mg a day, for 8 to 12 weeks, then down to 5 mg 8-hourly for maintenance, or continue the high dose with 0.1–0.15 mg of thyroxine as block and replace.B&L 55 · p.862 Add SRB's pharmacological reason for the 8-hourly interval, the 8-hour half-life, because it justifies the frequency rather than just reciting it,SRB 6 · p.464 and SRB's note that carbimazole also suppresses the autoimmune process in Graves' disease.SRB 6 · p.464 Do not quote 120 mg a day as a starting dose.
The traditional aim
Make the patient biochemically euthyroid at operation. Preparation is as an outpatient; admission is rarely necessary, and only for severe symptoms, failure of control, or non-compliance. Care should be coordinated with endocrinology input.B&L 55 · p.862
The beta-blocker alternative, and why it is faster
Beta-adrenergic blockade abolishes the clinical manifestations by acting on the target organs and not on the gland. Propranolol also inhibits the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3. Start propranolol 40 mg three times daily, increasing as needed, and larger doses are quite often required: propranolol 80 mg three times daily or nadolol 320 mg once daily. The clinical response is rapid, so the patient can be made clinically euthyroid and operated on within days rather than weeks.B&L 55 · p.862
And the trap that goes with it
Beta-blockers do not interfere with hormone synthesis, so hormone levels remain high during treatment and for some days after thyroidectomy. It is therefore important to continue treatment for 7 days postoperatively.B&L 55 · p.863 Forgetting this is how a thyroid crisis happens in a patient who looked well on the table.
Iodine
May be given with carbimazole or a beta-blocker for 10 days before operation. Iodide alone produces only a transient remission and may reduce vascularity, thereby marginally improving safety; it is not used alone, because if the patient needs preoperative treatment a more effective drug should be given. Its real value is as an additional measure of safety in case the early morning dose of beta-blocker is mistakenly omitted on the day of operation.B&L 55 · p.862–863 That is a very specific and very quotable reason.

8Complications, and how to talk about them

Haemorrhage: the one that kills

The mechanism, which is not what students expect Haemorrhage is the most frequent life-threatening complication of thyroidectomy. Around 1 in 50 patients will develop a haematoma, and in almost all cases within the first 24 hours. The mechanism matters: if an arterial bleed occurs, pressure in the central compartment can rise until it exceeds venous pressure, so venous oedema of the larynx develops and that causes airway obstruction and death. It is not the haematoma pressing on the trachea; it is the larynx swelling because its venous drainage has stopped.B&L 55 · p.865 Despite better understanding and better haemostatic technology, this complication has not been eliminated.B&L 55 · p.865
What to do, in order
Remove the skin sutures at once to release pressure, and seek senior help immediately. Then secure the airway by endotracheal intubation while the haematoma is evacuated and the bleeding point controlled.B&L 55 · p.865 Ward staff should know this before the patient arrives on the ward.
Prevention
Intraoperative attention to detail in haemostasis is critical. When closing, avoiding a watertight closure of the strap muscles may allow a haematoma to escape into the subcutaneous tissues rather than build pressure centrally. Wound drains have not been shown to have a protective effect.B&L 55 · p.865 The drain question is a favourite; the answer is no.
And the policy consequence
Although many surgeons worldwide practise day-case thyroidectomy, bleeding is the reason that in the UK thyroidectomy remains an inpatient procedure.B&L 55 · p.865

The nerves

Recurrent laryngeal nerve
Injury may be unilateral or bilateral, transient or permanent. Early routine postoperative laryngoscopy reveals a much higher incidence of transient cord paralysis than assessment of voice and cough detects, but such temporary dysfunction is not clinically important; voice and cord function should be assessed at first follow-up, 4 weeks postoperatively.B&L 55 · p.865
The audit figureA British Association of Endocrine and Thyroid Surgeons audit found an RLN palsy rate of 1.8% at one month, declining to 0.5% at three months, for first-time operations. Permanent paralysis is rare if the nerve has been identified at operation.B&L 55 · p.865
If you injure itIf the transected ends are identified, reanastomose them. If a length has been excised, for instance because of tumour invasion, consider anastomosis of the ansa cervicalis: this does not return cord mobility but maintains neurological input to the laryngeal muscles, and by avoiding denervation atrophy it improves vocal quality.B&L 55 · p.865
Established palsyTreat conservatively with speech therapy; if voice quality is unacceptable, medialisation procedures can be done. Nerve grafting has shown promise but experience is limited.B&L 55 · p.865
External branch of the superior laryngeal nerve
Injury is more common than RLN injury, because of the nerve's proximity to the superior thyroid artery. It causes loss of tension in the vocal cord, with diminished power and range.B&L 55 · p.865 Students almost always forget this nerve; mentioning it unprompted is worth a mark.
The consent point
Patients, particularly those who use their voice professionally, must be advised that any thyroid operation will result in change to the voice even in the absence of nerve trauma. For most the change is subtle and only demonstrable on formal voice assessment.B&L 55 · p.865

Calcium and thyroxine

Parathyroid insufficiency
From removal of the glands or infarction through damage to the parathyroid end arteries; often both. Vascular injury is probably far more important than inadvertent removal. The incidence of permanent hypoparathyroidism should be less than 1%. Most cases present dramatically 2–5 days after operation; very rarely the onset is delayed 2–3 weeks, and a patient with marked hypocalcaemia may be asymptomatic.B&L 55 · p.866
Why lobectomy is safeThe complication is limited to total thyroidectomy, because after a lobectomy the contralateral parathyroid glands are sufficient to maintain calcium levels.B&L 55 · p.866
The high-risk operationTotal thyroidectomy with central neck dissection places the parathyroid glands and their vascular supply at great risk and should only be performed when there is evidence of metastatic disease or a high risk of occult disease in the regional nodes.B&L 55 · p.866
What to do about it postoperatively
After total thyroidectomy, check calcium postoperatively. Not all patients develop immediate hypocalcaemia, so they must be educated about the signs: paraesthesia of the fingers and toes or around the mouth. Serial calcium monitoring for those at highest risk.B&L 55 · p.866
Thyroid insufficiency
Thyroxine replacement is required after total thyroidectomy and should start on day 1 postoperatively.B&L 55 · p.866 After lobectomy, around one in three will need supplementation, more if autoantibodies are present.B&L 55 · p.865

The overall prognosis, which surprises people
Although the incidence is rising, mortality rates remain static at over 80% five-year survival for all groups. Anaplastic carcinoma predicts a poor outcome.B&L 55 · p.867
Papillary microcarcinoma
Papillary carcinoma under 10 mm. Common, detected in about 10% of benign thyroid resections, and not associated with adverse outcomes. Management is controversial: in Korea national screening has caused a surge in cases, in Japan groups observe without surgery and have shown at least two-thirds never progress, in the USA some attempt ablation with ethanol or radiofrequency, and most of the world tries to avoid diagnosing them by limiting biopsy to lesions over 10 mm.B&L 55 · p.868
And the related oddityUp to 30% of patients who die of non-thyroid disease have deposits of papillary carcinoma at autopsy, suggesting many people live with it undetected.B&L 55 · p.868
Occult nodal disease
Occult micrometastases are present in as high as 40% of clinically N0 patients in the central neck, yet few progress to clinically meaningful disease, so the role of elective nodal surgery is in question.B&L 55 · p.868
Risk, for prognosis
In a young patient with a low-risk tumour the risk of death after appropriate treatment is almost zero. In an older patient with a high-risk tumour, meaning extrathyroid extension or distant metastases, the risk is as high as 55% at five years. Older patients with low-risk tumours and younger patients with high-risk tumours are intermediate.B&L 55 · p.869

Staging, in one breath
The American Joint Committee on Cancer system stages all patients under 55 as stage I unless they have distant metastases, when they are stage II. Edition note, not a live disagreement: AMES, AGES and MACIS B&L 26e gave these prognostic scores; 28e has dropped them for risk stratification plus AJCC staging
What the 26th edition had
Named prognostic scoring systems, AMES, AGES and MACIS, which are still taught and still appear in Indian notes and question banks.
What the 28th edition has instead
No named score. If the examiner asks for AMES or MACIS by name, answer the acronym, since it is harmless to know, and add that the current edition sets them aside in favour of risk stratification and AJCC staging. Thyroiditis, briefly, because it is the commonest wrong answer

Hashimoto's, chronic lymphocytic autoimmune thyroiditis
Common, usually with raised titres of thyroid antibodies.B&L 55 · p.871 It frequently coexists with simple nodular goitre, and B&L warns that differential diagnosis from autoimmune thyroiditis may be difficult, which is why antibodies are part of the first-line panel.B&L 55 · p.857
Riedel's thyroiditis
Very rare, accounting for 0.5% of goitres. Thyroid tissue is replaced by fibrous tissue, which is why it is hard and fixed and mimics carcinoma.B&L 55 · p.872
And the rule that follows
A hard, fixed gland is not automatically cancer: anaplastic carcinoma, lymphoma, Riedel's thyroiditis and a calcified benign nodule can all feel the same, and the distinction matters enormously because anaplastic carcinoma is rapidly fatal while lymphoma and thyroiditis respond to treatment.B&L 55 · p.868 That is the argument for core or open biopsy in such a case.

10Rapid-fire viva

What seven things must be clear in any thyroid case?

Functional status, hyperthyroid, euthyroid or hypothyroid; compression on the trachea or recurrent nerve; neck lymph nodal status; tracheal deviation; carotid infiltration; retrosternal extension; and systemic features of toxicity or of malignant spread to bone, liver or lungs.SRB 6 · p.451

Why does a thyroid swelling move on swallowing, and what else does?

The gland is bound to the cricoid cartilage by Berry's ligament, a condensation of pretracheal fascia,B&L 55 · p.852 and the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus parts of the inferior constrictor are attached to the thyroid and cricoid cartilages, so they contract on swallowing and carry the cartilages and the gland upwards.SRB 6 · p.452 Anything else attached to the larynx or trachea will also move: a thyroglossal cyst, a prelaryngeal lymph node, a subhyoid bursa.Das 27 · p.377 Only a thyroglossal cyst moves on protruding the tongue.Das 27 · p.378

Define goitre, isolated swelling and dominant swelling.

The normal thyroid is impalpable. Goitre means generalised enlargement of the gland. A discrete swelling in one lobe with no palpable abnormality elsewhere is isolated or solitary. A discrete swelling with abnormality elsewhere in the gland is dominant. About 70% of discrete swellings are clinically isolated and 30% dominant, though clinical assessment overestimates how many are truly isolated.B&L 55 · p.855, p.857

How likely is a solitary thyroid nodule to be cancer?

About 15% of isolated swellings are malignant and a further 30 to 40% are follicular adenomas, so nearly half are neoplastic; the rest are colloid degeneration, thyroiditis or cysts. The risk in a dominant swelling is about half that of a truly isolated one, which is still too high to ignore.B&L 55 · p.858 Bailey and Love expresses the risk as a "rule of 12", rising with three things: isolated rather than dominant, solid rather than cystic, and male rather than female.B&L 55 · p.858

Which single investigation would you ask for first, and why?

Serum TSH, with free T4 and free T3 only if the TSH is abnormal, because TSH secretion is under negative feedback from circulating thyroid hormones and so is the most sensitive single indicator: suppressed in hyperthyroidism, raised in hypothyroidism.B&L 55 · p.852, p.853 The exception is suspected T3 toxicosis, where T4 is normal and free T3 estimation makes the diagnosis.SRB 6 · p.459 Protein-bound iodine and the T3 resin uptake, which appear in older texts, are obsolete.Das 27 · p.386

Interpret: low-normal T3 and T4 with a raised TSH.

Incipient or developing thyroid failure. Florid failure gives depressed T3 and T4 with gross elevation of TSH; in toxic states TSH is suppressed; in the euthyroid state all three are normal.B&L 55 · p.852

What is the single most important limitation of FNAC in the thyroid?

It cannot distinguish a benign follicular adenoma from a follicular carcinoma, because that distinction rests on histological criteria, capsular and vascular invasion, not on cytology. It is reliable for papillary carcinoma.B&L 55 · p.858 That limitation is the reason all follicular neoplasms are excised: it is seldom possible to tell adenoma from carcinoma cytologically.B&L 55 · p.859

When would you order an isotope scan?

Only when toxicity and nodularity coexist, to localise the overactivity and so distinguish a toxic nodule with suppression of the rest of the gland from a toxic multinodular goitre with several areas of uptake, which have different treatments. Routine scanning is inappropriate for telling benign from malignant, because 80% of cold swellings are benign and 5% of warm ones are malignant, and it has otherwise been abandoned.B&L 55 · p.854, p.858 Hot nodules are very rarely malignant.B&L 55 · p.868

Why must the thyroid be ablated before a whole-body iodine scan?

Because metastatic thyroid cancer tissue cannot compete with normal thyroid tissue in the uptake of iodine, so with a functioning gland in place the metastases will not show.B&L 55 · p.854 It is the same reason total thyroidectomy precedes radioiodine therapy.

Describe the natural history of a simple goitre.

Persistent stimulation causes diffuse hyperplasia with all lobules active and uniform iodine uptake, and this stage is reversible. Fluctuating stimulation then produces a mixed pattern of active and inactive lobules. Active lobules become vascular and hyperplastic until haemorrhage occurs, with central necrosis leaving a rind of active follicles. Necrotic lobules coalesce into nodules. Repetition gives a multinodular goitre in which most nodules are inactive and the active follicles lie in the internodular tissue.B&L 55 · p.856SRB 6 · p.451 That last point explains why a toxic nodular goitre is toxic from its internodular tissue.

Name the goitrogens and say which step each blocks.

Brassicas, cabbage, kale and rape, which contain thiocyanate; PAS; the sulphonylureas; and the antithyroid drugs themselves. Thiocyanates and perchlorates interfere with iodide trapping. Carbimazole and the thiouracils interfere with the oxidation of iodide and the binding of iodine to tyrosine.B&L 55 · p.856Das 27 · p.376 Paradoxically, iodides in large quantities are also goitrogenic, because they inhibit the organic binding of iodine.B&L 55 · p.856

Lid retraction, lid lag, exophthalmos: distinguish them.

Lid retraction is the upper lid sitting higher than normal with the lower lid normal, from overactivity of the smooth muscle part of levator palpebrae superioris. Lid lag is the upper lid failing to keep pace with the eyeball as the patient looks down following your finger. Neither is exophthalmos. Exophthalmos is the eyeball pushed forwards by increased fat, oedema or cellular infiltration in the retro-orbital space, so sclera becomes visible below the lower edge of the iris first and then above the upper edge.Das 27 · p.383

Kocher's test, or Kocher's sign?

Ask which is meant, because they are unrelated. Kocher's test is compression of the lateral lobes producing stridor, indicating tracheal compression or tracheomalacia.Das 27 · p.380SRB 6 · p.452 Kocher's sign is an eye sign: with the examiner's hands on the eyes lifting them higher, the upper lid springs up faster than the eyebrow.SRB 6 · p.462 Neither appears in Bailey and Love 28e.

Crile's method, or Crile's grading?

Again, two unrelated things. Crile's method is palpation of the gland with the thumb while the patient swallows.Das 27 · p.379 Crile's grading is a severity grading of thyrotoxicosis by sleeping pulse rate checked over three consecutive nights and averaged.SRB 6 · p.462 The grades to attach: under 90 mild, 90 to 110 moderate, over 110 severe.Das 27 · p.376

How do you demonstrate a retrosternal goitre at the bedside?

Pemberton's sign: ask the patient to raise both arms until they touch the sides of the head and hold. The face becomes suffused and cyanosed with distress and dyspnoea, because the raised arms draw the thoracic inlet onto the goitre and obstruct the great veins.Das 27 · p.381 Supporting signs are dilated veins over the upper chest, dullness on percussing the upper sternum, and inability to feel the lower border of the gland even on swallowing.Das 27 · p.377, p.379, p.382 Confirm with a chest radiograph and then CT, because the plain film predicts it but CT is needed to define the extent.B&L 55 · p.853

Can a retrosternal goitre be removed through the neck?

Yes, in more than 95% of cases. Those most likely to need sternotomy are malignant disease, revision surgery, extension into the posterior mediastinum, and a goitre whose diameter exceeds that of the thoracic inlet; those should be planned jointly with thoracic surgery. The blood supply comes from the neck, which reduces the risk of catastrophic bleeding from the great vessels, but if the goitre arose from a posterior nodule the recurrent laryngeal nerve may be displaced anteriorly, so apparent fascial bands over the gland must not be divided casually.B&L 55 · p.860, p.861

Build all the thyroid operations from their elements.

Three elements: total lobectomy, isthmusectomy, subtotal lobectomy. Lobectomy is total lobectomy plus isthmusectomy. Total thyroidectomy is two total lobectomies plus isthmusectomy. Subtotal thyroidectomy is two subtotal lobectomies plus isthmusectomy. Near-total thyroidectomy is total lobectomy plus isthmusectomy plus subtotal lobectomy, which is the Dunhill procedure.B&L 55 · p.860 SRB calls it Hartley Dunhill and gives the remnant as about 4 g.SRB 6 · p.479

Why is the recurrent laryngeal nerve at different risk on the two sides?

It recurs round the arch of the aorta on the left and the subclavian artery on the right. On the left it therefore has more distance in which to reach the tracheo-oesophageal groove and runs in a medial plane; on the right there is less distance so it runs more obliquely. And about 2% of right-sided nerves are non-recurrent and enter the larynx from above.B&L 55 · p.851

Where do you look for the nerve, and where is it most at risk?

Three places: in the tracheo-oesophageal groove, where it forms one side of Beahrs' triangle with the carotid artery and the inferior thyroid artery; under the tubercle of Zuckerkandl, the most posterolateral part of the gland, as the lobe is mobilised laterally; and at the cricothyroid joint. It is at most risk at the level of Berry's ligament, where it enters the larynx, and if the nerve lies medially it may actually lie on the thyroid itself.B&L 55 · p.852, p.864

Which nerve injury is more common than recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, and what does it do?

Injury to the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, because of its proximity to the superior thyroid artery. It causes loss of tension in the vocal cord, with diminished power and range in the voice.B&L 55 · p.865 And every patient should be warned that any thyroid operation changes the voice even without nerve trauma.

Your patient's neck is swelling two hours after thyroidectomy. What is happening and what do you do?

A haematoma, the most frequent life-threatening complication, occurring in about 1 in 50 patients and almost always within 24 hours. The danger is not direct tracheal compression: pressure in the central compartment rises above venous pressure, venous oedema of the larynx develops and that obstructs the airway. Remove the skin sutures immediately to release pressure and call for senior help; secure the airway by endotracheal intubation while the haematoma is evacuated and the bleeding point controlled. Drains do not prevent this.B&L 55 · p.865

Four days after total thyroidectomy the patient has tingling round the mouth. Explain.

Hypocalcaemia from parathyroid insufficiency, due to removal of the glands or more often to infarction from damage to the parathyroid end arteries; vascular injury is probably far more important than inadvertent removal. Most cases present dramatically 2 to 5 days after operation. Permanent hypoparathyroidism should be under 1%. It is limited to total thyroidectomy, because after lobectomy the contralateral glands maintain the calcium. Not all patients develop immediate hypocalcaemia, so they are taught the signs, paraesthesia of fingers, toes or around the mouth, and the high-risk ones have serial calcium monitoring.B&L 55 · p.866

A thyrotoxic patient becomes hyperpyrexial and agitated after an unrelated operation. Manage it.

Thyrotoxic crisis, or storm. Supportive: intravenous fluids for dehydration, cooling with ice packs, oxygen, diuretics for cardiac failure, digoxin for uncontrolled atrial fibrillation, sedation, and intravenous hydrocortisone. Specific: carbimazole 10 to 20 mg 6-hourly; Lugol's iodine 10 drops 8-hourly orally or sodium iodide 1 g intravenously; and propranolol 1 to 2 mg intravenously or orally 6-hourly to block the beta-adrenergic effects.B&L 55 · p.866 It is now extremely rare after thyroidectomy, because preparation is better.

You prepared a patient with propranolol rather than carbimazole. What must you not forget?

To continue the beta-blocker for 7 days postoperatively. Beta-blockers act on the target organs and not on the gland, so they do not interfere with hormone synthesis and the hormone levels remain high during treatment and for some days after the gland is removed.B&L 55 · p.863 That is also why iodine is sometimes added for 10 days beforehand: as a safety net if the morning dose of beta-blocker is missed on the day of surgery.B&L 55 · p.863

Why will antithyroid drugs never cure a toxic nodule?

Because the overactive tissue is autonomous and not driven by TSH or by TSH receptor antibodies, so there is nothing for the drug to switch off permanently; recurrence is certain once the drug is stopped. Surgery cures it by removing all the overactive tissue, which lets the suppressed normal tissue function again.B&L 55 · p.862

When would you operate in Graves' disease rather than give radioiodine?

Four situations: the patient refuses radiation; a large goitre; progressive eye signs; or pregnancy. Otherwise most patients have an initial course of antithyroid drugs with radioiodine for relapse.B&L 55 · p.862 And radioiodine has the specific disadvantage here that eye signs may be aggravated.B&L 55 · p.862

Subtotal or total thyroidectomy in Graves' disease?

Present it as a trade-off. Subtotal: variable return to euthyroid over up to 12 months, lifelong recurrence risk up to 5%, permanent hypoparathyroidism 5%, minimal follow-up. Total: 100% thyroid failure and lifelong thyroxine, permanent hypoparathyroidism 1%, lifelong follow-up. The risks of recurrence and of late failure depend on the size of the remnant as a proportion of gland weight.B&L 55 · p.860 The deciding principle is that thyroid failure is not a treatment failure but recurrent toxicity is, so err towards removing too much. Bailey and Love 28e goes further and says subtotal thyroidectomy has been more or less abandoned except where exogenous thyroxine is not available,B&L 55 · p.865 which is exactly why Indian texts such as SRB still teach it.SRB 6 · p.465

FNAC shows papillary carcinoma, a single 1.5 cm intrathyroid focus, no nodes, patient aged 30. What operation?

The Indian textbook answer is total or near-total thyroidectomy.SRB 6 · p.456 Current Bailey and Love offers a thyroid lobectomy for a low-risk patient with a single focus limited to the thyroid, because it protects the contralateral recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands, and says this approach is now considered appropriate unless there are high-risk features.B&L 55 · p.869 Give both and say which is which. Total thyroidectomy is clearly indicated for nodal or distant metastases or any high-risk feature, and when radioiodine is planned, since metastases will not take up iodine while normal thyroid remains.B&L 55 · p.854

Would you do an elective neck dissection in papillary carcinoma?

No, not routinely. A therapeutic compartment-orientated dissection is done when disease is present. Elective lateral neck dissection has been abandoned, because of morbidity and because few occult metastases become clinically meaningful, even though occult micrometastases are found in as high as 40% of clinically N0 central necks. Elective central neck dissection was popular but has moved out of favour for lack of evidence that outcomes improve, and it is now reserved for the highest-risk patients, such as those with extrathyroid extension. Total thyroidectomy with central neck dissection puts the parathyroid glands and their blood supply at great risk.B&L 55 · p.866, p.868, p.869

Which is worse, papillary or follicular carcinoma, and why?

Follicular. Papillary is commoner, 80% against 10%, and spreads to lymph nodes, with distant metastases uncommon and nodal disease in the young predicting recurrence but not death. Follicular spreads by the bloodstream, and its eventual mortality, though still low, is twice that of papillary.B&L 55 · p.866, p.868 Hürthle cell cancers, a rare variant of follicular neoplasm in which oxyphil cells predominate, carry a poor prognosis.B&L 55 · p.868

A patient has a hard, fixed, rapidly growing thyroid mass. What now?

Treat it as possible anaplastic carcinoma, but do not assume it, because the differential includes thyroid lymphoma and occasionally thyroiditis, and the distinction is critical: anaplastic carcinoma is rapidly fatal and managed palliatively, whereas the others respond to therapy. Core or even open biopsy may be required for a confident diagnosis, and with tracheal compression isthmusectomy is the most appropriate form of biopsy.B&L 55 · p.868, p.871 Also remember Riedel's thyroiditis, very rare at 0.5% of goitres, in which thyroid tissue is replaced by fibrous tissue.B&L 55 · p.872

What is a lateral aberrant thyroid?

A misnomer. It is a mass of tissue with the structure of normal or pathological thyroid lying laterally in the neck, and it is in fact a metastatic lymph node from an occult papillary carcinoma.SRB 6 · p.448Das 27 · p.382 Bailey and Love supports the conclusion from the other direction: cervical lymphadenopathy along the internal jugular vein with a suspicious thyroid swelling is almost diagnostic of papillary carcinoma.B&L 55 · p.859

Which eye sign is which: von Graefe, Joffroy, Stellwag, Moebius, Dalrymple?

von Graefe, the upper lid lags behind the eyeball as the patient looks down. Joffroy, absence of forehead wrinkling when the patient looks up with the face inclined down. Stellwag, staring look with infrequent blinking and a widened palpebral fissure, from toxic contraction of the striated part of levator palpebrae superioris. Moebius, failure of convergence. Dalrymple, upper sclera visible from retraction of the upper lid.Das 27 · p.383–384 None of these appears in Bailey and Love 28e.

Does Bailey and Love support the eponymous thyroid tests?

Not the clinical ones. Chapter 55 of the 28th edition contains none of Pemberton, Lahey, Kocher, Crile, Pizzillo or any of the eye-sign eponyms, and does not use the words exophthalmos, stridor, dysphagia or deglutition in its text. The eponyms it does keep are operative: Berry's ligament, Beahrs' triangle, the tubercle of Zuckerkandl, and Dunhill for the procedure.B&L 55 · p.852, p.860 The bedside eponyms belong to S Das and SRB. Knowing which book owns which eponym stops you hunting in the wrong index.

Which patients get a goitre from where they live, and which from their genes?

Endemic goitre is iodine deficiency, in mountain ranges and in lowlands whose soil or water lacks iodide: the Rockies, Alps, Andes and Himalayas, the Great Lakes, Lombardy, the Struma and Nile valleys, the Congo; and in India the Himalayas, the Vindyas and the Satpuda.B&L 55 · p.855Das 27 · p.374 Sporadic goitre in a non-endemic area is more often dyshormonogenesis, an enzyme deficiency, with often a family history suggesting a genetic defect.B&L 55 · p.855 Calcium is also goitrogenic, which is why goitre is common in low-iodine areas on chalk or limestone.

A multinodular goitre in an asymptomatic 78-year-old. What do you advise?

Usually nothing. Most patients with multinodular goitre are asymptomatic and do not require operation, and although the nodular stage is irreversible, more than half of benign nodules regress in size over 10 years. These goitres often grow very slowly, and the risks and benefits of surgery must be weighed carefully, particularly if a sternal split might be needed for access. Operate for features of malignancy, pressure symptoms once other causes are excluded, tracheal compression, or cosmesis if it troubles the patient.B&L 55 · p.857

One lobe is much more nodular than the other. What operation?

Total lobectomy on the more affected side, particularly in an older patient. It can be combined with subtotal resection of the other lobe, the Dunhill procedure, but most surgeons now prefer no intervention on the less affected side, because of regrowth and the higher complication rate of reoperation; and leaving the better lobe untouched keeps a straightforward completion lobectomy available later if needed.B&L 55 · p.857, p.860 Total lobectomy has the incidental advantage of being therapeutic for an unsuspected carcinoma.
