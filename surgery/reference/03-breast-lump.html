Long case 03 · General Surgery

Breast Lump

A case you can pass on clinical method alone and fail on vocabulary. The examiner will test three things: whether you can tell tethering from fixity, whether you know that triple assessment is one thing and not three, and whether you can say what you would do next without reciting a chemotherapy regimen nobody asked for.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1The case in one breath

Triple assessment is the spine of this case Every patient presenting with a breast lump, nipple discharge or other breast symptom is assessed by clinical examination, radiological imaging and tissue sampling for cytology or histology. That combination is called triple assessment.B&L 58 · p.915 B&L's figure claims a confident diagnosis in 99.9% of cases, and the text says the positive predictive value and diagnostic accuracy of the combination approach 100%.B&L 58 · p.915 SRB gives the same three limbs: clinical assessment including age; radiological imaging, with mammography after 40; and cytological or histological analysis by FNAC or core biopsy.SRB 8 · p.538

Say "triple assessment" early and then structure your whole answer around it. It is the single phrase that tells the examiner you know how a breast clinic works.

The anatomy that the clinical signs depend on

Extent, so you know where to look
Second rib above to the sixth rib or the inframammary crease below; lateral border of the sternum medially; anterior or mid-axillary line laterally. In the adult male it is rudimentary, about 2 cm in diameter, lying deep to the areola and extending to the areolar edge.B&L 58 · p.914
The axillary tail, and the trap it sets
Palpable in some women and visible premenstrually or during lactation. A well-developed axillary tail is sometimes mistaken for a mass of enlarged lymph nodes, a breast mass or a lipoma.B&L 58 · p.914 S Das's instruction follows from it: also feel the axillary tail,Das 30 · p.416 and remember that carcinoma can arise in it.Das 30 · p.416
The terminal ductal lobular unit, and why the upper outer quadrant matters
The terminal ductule with its lobule is the TDLU, the most active part of the breast, responding to oestrogen, progesterone, prolactin and growth hormone. Most diseases of the breast arise from the TDLU. About 50% of the ductolobular tissue lies in the upper outer quadrant and about 20% centrally, so examination must pay particular attention to the upper outer quadrant, the retroareolar region and the nipple-areola complex.B&L 58 · p.914 That is why the commonest site of a carcinoma is the upper outer quadrant,B&L 58 · p.932 and S Das notes fibroadenoma also occurs there most often.Das 30 · p.416
The ligaments of Cooper, which produce half the signs you will be asked about
Fibrous suspensory ligaments attached to the undersurface of the dermis superficially and to the pectoral fascia deeply.B&L 58 · p.914–915 Everything from dimpling to nipple retraction is these ligaments being shortened.
Two muscles in the nipple worth a mark
Circular fibres are Sappey's muscle, which erects the nipple; longitudinal fibres are Myerholtz muscle, which retracts it. The sebaceous glands of the areola enlarge in pregnancy to lubricate the nipple in lactation: Montgomery's tubercles.B&L 58 · p.915
How many lobes, and how many ducts open on the nipple? SRB 8 p.504–505 says 15 to 20; B&L 58 p.914 says five to nine major ducts
SRB
About 15 to 20 lobes develop during puberty, each draining into a single lactiferous duct; the breast parenchyma contains 15 to 20 lobes; and the nipple is pierced by 15 to 20 lactiferous ducts, each opening independently. Each duct is 2 to 4 mm in diameter.SRB 8 · p.504, p.505
Bailey & Love 28e
"There are five to nine major lactiferous (milk) ducts carrying milk from the lobes." Approximately 10 to 100 lobules empty via ductules into a lactiferous duct, and each duct is lined with a spiral arrangement of contractile myoepithelial cells.B&L 58 · p.914
These are different claims about the same structure, and the modern anatomical literature is on B&L's side: the classical 15 to 20 is a count of lobes, and duct injection studies find far fewer ducts actually reaching the nipple surface. How to answer: give the number the examiner is marking against, 15 to 20 lobes, and then be precise about what you are counting. Say: "classically 15 to 20 lobes, each with a lactiferous duct; Bailey and Love 28e puts the number of major ducts carrying milk at five to nine." The distinction between a lobe, a duct and a major duct is the whole content of the disagreement, and showing you have noticed it is worth more than either number. Do not volunteer "five to nine" as your opening answer in an Indian viva.

The lymphatics, which decide the operation

Where the lymph goes
Predominantly to the axillary and internal mammary nodes. The axillary nodes receive approximately 85% of the lymph from the breast. The internal mammary nodes lie along the internal mammary vessels deep to the costal cartilages and just superficial to the parietal pleura, and drain the medial half of the breast.B&L 58 · p.915
The six anatomical groups
Lateral, along the lower border of the axillary vein lateral to the thoracodorsal pedicle. Anterior or pectoral, between the lateral borders of pectoralis major and minor with the lateral thoracic vessels: these are the sentinel nodes in most patients. Posterior, along the subscapular and thoracodorsal vessels. Central, embedded in fat in the centre of the axilla. Interpectoral (Rotter's), between pectoralis major and minor. Apical, above and medial to the pectoralis minor tendon and lateral to the first rib, which receive the efferent channels from all the other axillary groups.B&L 58 · p.915
The three surgical levels, which is what you will actually be asked
Level I: below and lateral to the lateral border of pectoralis minor, the majority. Level II: in front of and behind pectoralis minor, including Rotter's nodes. Level III: above and medial to the medial border of pectoralis minor.B&L 58 · p.915 Learn the levels by their relation to pectoralis minor and you can never muddle them.
And onwards
The apical nodes are continuous with the supraclavicular nodes and drain into the subclavian lymph trunk, entering the great veins directly or via the thoracic duct or jugular trunk.B&L 58 · p.915 S Das's clinical sequence matches: spread goes first to the pectoral, then central, then apical group, the subscapular and lateral groups being involved later.Das 30 · p.420

2Why this patient has a breast lump

Is breast cancer a Western disease? S Das 30 p.410 frames it as one; B&L 58 p.931–933 treats it as a major problem of low- and middle-income countries
S Das
Carcinoma of the breast and fibroadenosis are "more common in the western world with high incidence in England and Wales", and "breast carcinoma is rare in Japan". Both are more seen in the developed world and are less common in the under-developed world where women give birth to multiple children.Das 30 · p.410 The mechanism he offers is parity, breastfeeding and a diet rich in saturated fatty acids.Das 30 · p.410
Bailey & Love 28e
Treats breast cancer as a global disease with a different presentation rather than a lower incidence, and gives three facts that matter for an Indian student. First, the median age at presentation is around 60 years in the West but around 48 years in low- and middle-income nations. Second, Parsis in India appear in its list of higher-risk ethnic groups, alongside American white, African American under 45 and Ashkenazi Jewish women.B&L 58 · p.931 Third, and most important: "Few breast cancers in high-income countries present with either locally advanced disease or symptoms of metastatic disease; however, the incidence is much greater in resource-poor countries, where up to 60% of women still present late."B&L 58 · p.933
This one is not an academic disagreement, it is a clinical trap, so answer it as a correction rather than a compromise. The parity and breastfeeding risk factors S Das gives are real and B&L lists them too: nulliparity or first pregnancy after 35 raises risk, and more than 12 months of breastfeeding is protective.B&L 58 · p.931 What has dated is the conclusion. Say: the classical teaching is that breast cancer is commoner in the West, and the risk factors behind that are genuine, but incidence in India is rising, Indian women present about twelve years younger, and up to 60% present late. Then draw the practical consequence, which is the mark: a lump in a 40-year-old Indian woman carries a materially higher prior probability of cancer than the older textbook framing suggests, so never let age or geography reassure you. S Das himself gives you the licence, in the same paragraph: carcinoma usually occurs above 40 "though rarely it may occur earlier, so age should not be the criterion to exclude the diagnosis of breast carcinoma".Das 30 · p.410

3History, and why each question earns its place

Age? DistinguishesS Das's age map: fibroadenoma usually below 35; fibroadenosis at any age but more in middle age; intramammary abscess most commonly in young lactating women; carcinoma usually above 40.Das 30 · p.410
Does not rule outCarcinoma "rarely may occur earlier, so age should not be the criterion to exclude the diagnosis of breast carcinoma".Das 30 · p.410 And in India the median presenting age is around 48, not 60.B&L 58 · p.931
And it changes the imagingUltrasound is the primary modality in young women with dense breast tissue; mammography should be the first investigation in older women.B&L 58 · p.915, p.916 So the age answer determines your next sentence.
Tell me about the lump. When, how did you notice it, and has it grown? WhyS Das: enquire about mode of onset, duration and rate of growth. Long history with slow growth suggests fibroadenosis or fibroadenoma. Short history with fast growth is probably carcinoma.Das 30 · p.410
The exception that proves you have read itAtrophic scirrhous carcinoma is a slow growing tumour.Das 30 · p.410 A slow lump in an elderly woman is not thereby benign.
If there was traumaA lump may follow trauma as a haematoma or fat necrosis, but remember the commoner story: trauma may simply attract the patient's attention to a pre-existing lump.Das 30 · p.410 Never let a history of injury close the case.
A number worth quotingThe average delay between a patient finding a lump and reporting it is about 6 weeks in carcinoma.Das 30 · p.410
Is it painful? Does the pain change with your periods? Rules out nothing, and this is the pointS Das puts it in italics for a reason: "carcinoma of the breast is a painless condition", and all neoplasms of the breast, benign or malignant, are painless to start with.Das 30 · p.410 So a painless lump found accidentally while washing may be a carcinoma, and the clinician must be more particular in examining that case rather than ignoring it.Das 30 · p.410 Absence of pain is a reason to worry, not to relax.
Rules inCyclical pain, worse before menstruation, in a young woman: fibroadenosis, that is ANDI. Throbbing pain: pus has formed in an acute mastitis.Das 30 · p.410
After the menopauseLocalised breast pain may be periductal mastitis or referred pain from musculoskeletal disorders.Das 30 · p.410 B&L formalises the assessment with a pain chart, and the version to know is the All India Institute of Medical Sciences modification of the Cardiff Breast Pain Chart.B&L 58 · p.920
And pain is sometimes malignantSRB: pain in the lump occurs in 10% of carcinomas.SRB 8 · p.527 So neither its presence nor its absence decides anything on its own.
Any discharge from the nipple? What colour? DistinguishesS Das maps colour to cause. Fresh or altered blood: duct papilloma or carcinoma. Pus: mammary abscess. Milk: lactation, galactocele, or a mammary fistula from a chronic subareolar abscess. Serous or greenish: fibroadenosis or mammary duct ectasia.Das 30 · p.411
Why it mattersNipple discharge is one of the three presentations that triggers full triple assessment.B&L 58 · p.915 For unilateral discharge SRB offers ductography: a fine cannula into the duct opening and 0.2 mL of dilute water-soluble contrast, with craniocaudal and mediolateral films.SRB 8 · p.538
Has the nipple changed? Pulled in, and since when? DistinguishesRetraction for a long time or since puberty may be developmental. Recent retraction is of importance and is usually due to underlying carcinoma.Das 30 · p.411 The question is not "is the nipple retracted" but "when did it change", and candidates routinely ask the wrong one.
MechanismExtension of growth along the lactiferous ducts with subsequent fibrosis.Das 30 · p.430 B&L's version is the Cooper's ligament one: tumour growth factors drive a desmoplastic reaction, and contraction of the new collagen shortens Cooper's ligaments, giving dimpling from a single shortened ligament, puckering or tethering from many, or nipple retraction.B&L 58 · p.932
CarefulS Das's caveat: any tumour lying immediately deep to the nipple will fix the nipple, benign or malignant, because the main mammary ducts may travel through the growth.Das 30 · p.418 A fixed nipple is not by itself malignant.
And if the nipple is eroded, not retractedThink Paget's disease, which is a unique type of ductal carcinoma in situ arising in the nipple. If a nipple lesion fails to heal, exclude Paget's by taking a wedge biopsy of the lesion.B&L 58 · p.924
Any pain in the back, hip or shoulder? Cough, breathlessness, headache, fits, abdominal swelling, jaundice? WhyS Das warns that in a long-standing carcinoma patients ignore back, hip or shoulder pain, considering it rheumatism and unrelated to the lump of the breast, when it is bony metastasis.Das 30 · p.411 You have to ask directly.
The full list to run throughB&L: bony pain, cough, breathlessness, haemoptysis, headache, visual disturbance, neurological deficit, epileptic fits, abdominal distension, jaundice, anorexia, weakness, weight loss, hypercalcaemia.B&L 58 · p.933 Plus any swelling in the neck or armpit.
Where bone secondaries go, in orderLumbar vertebrae, neck of femur, thoracic vertebrae, rib, skull. Generally osteolytic, though osteosclerotic and mixed types occur. In the limbs the deposits occur above the elbow and above the knee, because haematopoietic vascular marrow is confined to the axial skeleton and to the limbs above those joints.B&L 58 · p.932 That last clause is the reason, and giving the reason is what earns the mark.
One named consequenceExtensive marrow replacement releases immature blast cells into the blood, giving leukoerythroblastic anaemia.B&L 58 · p.932
Have you lost weight? Rules inCarcinoma. But S Das gives two other causes you must keep in the frame in India: tuberculosis of the breast, and chest wall tuberculosis leading to a retromammary abscess.Das 30 · p.411 A breast mass with weight loss and night sweats in a young Indian woman is not automatically cancer.
Menstrual, marital, obstetric and lactational history. Any previous breast problem? Any family history? Why obstetricFibroadenosis and carcinoma are commoner in nulliparous women and in those who have not breastfed.Das 30 · p.410, p.411 B&L quantifies both sides: first pregnancy after 35 raises risk, and more than 12 months of breastfeeding is more protective than less.B&L 58 · p.931
Why lactationalSuppurative mastitis particularly occurs during the first lactational period.Das 30 · p.411
Why past historyRecurrent abscess occurs with a congenital retracted nipple; tuberculosis of the breast may recur; fibroadenosis may recur after a long gap, so ask carefully and the patient will often remember a similar episode years ago. And above all, carcinoma may recur in the opposite breast.Das 30 · p.411
Why family history, preciselyOne first-degree relative RR 2; two first-degree relatives RR 3.B&L 58 · p.931 It also decides whether to refer for genetic testing, and B&L's indications include breast cancer at or under 50, triple negative at or under 60, two breast primaries, male breast cancer, and breast cancer at any age with a relative affected under 50.B&L 58 · p.942
And ask about radiationPrior radiotherapy, for example for Hodgkin's lymphoma, is a risk factor for breast sarcoma, as is chronic lymphoedema.B&L 58 · p.942

4Examination, and what each finding settles

Before you touch the patient The examiner is marking this before you have found anything. Bailey & Love gives it as a sequence: "ensure you have followed appropriate hand hygiene guidance. Introduce yourself and check the patient's name and date of birth. Request presence of a chaperone as appropriate. Explain what you are going to do, obtain verbal consent and ensure that the patient is comfortable."B&L 35 · p.472 And the standard it is held to: "patients should be treated with respect and dignity, receive a clear explanation of the examination undertaken and be kept as comfortable as possible. A chaperone should be present, especially for intimate examinations."B&L 21 · p.285

Do it, and be heard doing itWash your hands. Introduce yourself. Say what you want to examine and why, and ask permission. Ask for a chaperone where the examination is intimate. Expose what you need and cover the rest. Warm your hands. Tell the patient what each step will feel like and that they can ask you to stop, examine the tender part last, and watch their face rather than only your hands. Cover them and thank them before you turn to the examiner.

This one is intimate, so the chaperone is not a courtesy B&L's wording is "especially for intimate examinations", and it adds that this "should be part of a local guideline or policy".B&L 21 · p.285 Offer a chaperone whatever your sex and the patient's, say that you have offered, and say you would record it. For a child, explain it to the child and ask the parent. If consent is refused, say that you would explain again, offer a chaperone, document the refusal, and not proceed. Examiners ask what you would do if she declined, and that is the whole answer.
Examine her sitting up or lying down? S Das 30 p.411 says mainly sitting; B&L 58 p.916 illustrates supine. S Das supplies the compromise himself
S Das
"The examination of breast is performed mainly with the patient in sitting posture. This gives more information regarding the level of the nipples, a lump and palpation of the axillary lymph nodes." He then adds both alternatives and judges them: examination may also be performed semi-recumbent at 45 degrees, which "is a good compromise between lying flat, which makes the breasts flatten out and fall sideways, and sitting upright, which makes the breasts pendulous and bulky"; and in the recumbent position, so as to palpate the lump against the chest wall.Das 30 · p.411
Bailey & Love 28e
Its clinical examination figure shows the patient lying supine for palpation, using the pad of three fingers and the dial of a clock method for a comprehensive examination.B&L 58 · p.916
Live difference of method, and unusually, S Das has already resolved it for you, so quote his own resolution. Do it in three stages and say why as you go: sitting first, for inspection, because that is where nipple level, dimpling and asymmetry show, and for palpating the axilla with the arm supported; semi-recumbent at 45 degrees as the compromise position for palpating the breast itself; and supine to press a lump against the chest wall and to test fixity to pectoralis major. Use B&L's technique within those positions, the pads of three fingers working round the dial of a clock, and name the three areas that must not be skipped: upper outer quadrant, retroareolar region and nipple-areola complex,B&L 58 · p.914, p.916 plus the axillary tail.Das 30 · p.416 Saying which position you are in and what you are looking for in it turns a disagreement into a demonstration of method.

Inspection: the four positions, and what each is for

S Das's sequence, and each position exists to reveal a different sign. Do all four and say what you are looking for.Das 30 · p.412

1. Sitting, arms by the side
The baseline. Compare the level of the nipples. Note the position of each breast, its size and shape, and any puckering or dimpling.Das 30 · p.412
2. Arms raised straight above the head
The lump or dimple may be more marked. Raising the arms puts Cooper's ligaments on stretch, so a shortened one shows.Das 30 · p.412
3. Hands on hips, pressing and relaxing to command
Abnormal movement of the nipple or exaggeration of skin dimples becomes evident, because contracting pectoralis major moves anything tethered to its fascia.Das 30 · p.412
4. Bending forwards from the waist so the breasts fall away from the body
Any failure of one nipple to fall away from the chest indicates abnormal fibrosis behind the nipple.Das 30 · p.412 S Das shows that retraction due to carcinoma "can be so well detected in bending forward position".Das 30 · p.412 This is the position most candidates omit and the one that most often shows the sign.

What to look for on the skin

Red, warm and oedematous
Acute mastitis. But S Das's warning: a similar picture may be seen, very rarely, in acute mastitis carcinomatosa, the acute lactational carcinoma.Das 30 · p.412, p.416 SRB describes the same entity as diffuse, painful, warm, often involving the whole breast with peau d'orange and often extending to the chest wall, with more than a third of the skin involved.SRB 8 · p.525 B&L's figure gives the threshold the same way: diffuse erythema and skin oedema involving more than one-third of the breast are the features of inflammatory carcinoma.B&L 58 · p.932 A hot red breast in a non-lactating woman that does not settle on antibiotics is a cancer until proved otherwise.
Engorged veins
Commonly seen in a large soft fibroadenoma (cystosarcoma phyllodes) and in rapidly growing sarcoma; also in acute lactational mastitis with a huge abscess.Das 30 · p.412–413
Dimple, retraction or puckering
Often noticed in scirrhous carcinoma, where the breast may be shrunken and drawn in towards the growth. Dimpling may be made prominent by lifting the breast gently upwards.Das 30 · p.412, p.413
Ulceration, fungation, satellite nodules
Advanced disease. Cancer en cuirasse is extensive tumour infiltration of the skin of the breast and chest, and in post-mastectomy recurrence also of the upper limb and abdomen.B&L 58 · p.932SRB 8 · p.528 S Das says hard nodules may be felt in the skin as part of it.Das 30 · p.418
Brawny oedema of the arm
Extensive neoplastic infiltration of the axillary nodes, mainly from lymphatic blockage.Das 30 · p.415 SRB characterises it: indurated, painful, non-pitting, occurring with fixed nodes.SRB 8 · p.530 Note the contrast with post-operative lymphoedema, which S Das says may also follow treatment.Das 30 · p.415
And inspect the axilla and supraclavicular fossa
Not only palpate them. These regions should particularly be inspected for any swelling.Das 30 · p.415
Peau d'orange: which glands, and does it mean the skin is invaded? S Das 30 says sweat glands on p.413 and sebaceous glands on p.430; SRB 8 p.527 says sebaceous; B&L 58 p.932 names no gland and adds a second mechanism
S Das, p.413
Due to blockage of subcuticular lymphatics with oedema of the skin, "which deepens the mouths of the sweat glands and hair follicles".Das 30 · p.413
S Das, p.430, on the same sign
Oedema with pitting, "oedema being due to obstruction of the lymphatics by cancer cells and pitting being due to fixation of the hair follicles and sebaceous glands to the subcutaneous tissue".Das 30 · p.430 So the book names sweat glands in one chapter-section and sebaceous glands in another.
SRB
Due to obstruction of dermal lymphatics, affecting the openings of the sebaceous glands and hair follicles.SRB 8 · p.527
Bailey & Love 28e
Names no gland at all, and makes a clinically larger point instead. Peau d'orange is a sign of locally advanced disease due to obstruction of cutaneous lymphatic drainage, "by infiltration of either subdermal lymphatics or axillary lymph nodes by tumour cells".B&L 58 · p.932 It also notes that in people with darker skins the erythema takes on a brownish hue,B&L 58 · p.932 which matters at an Indian bedside and appears in no other text here.
The gland question is trivia on which one book contradicts itself, so do not die on that hill: answer the mechanism, and name the structures as "the openings of the skin appendages, the hair follicles and the glands that open with them". If pressed for a gland, say sebaceous, because that is where SRB and the later S Das passage agree. Then say the thing that is actually worth marks, which is B&L's addition: peau d'orange does not require the skin itself to be invaded. The lymphatic obstruction can be at the subdermal lymphatics or at the axillary nodes, so the sign tells you the disease is locally advanced without telling you where the block is. And add the pigmentation point for a dark-skinned patient: the accompanying erythema is brownish, not red.B&L 58 · p.932

Palpation of the lump: the sequence that answers the examiner's next question

Begin on the normal side
S Das: start with the normal side so you have the findings to compare against; then palpate the four quadrants systematically, and do not miss the axillary tail.Das 30 · p.416
Situation
Which quadrant. Carcinoma can occur anywhere including the axillary tail, but is commonly in the upper and outer quadrant; so is fibroadenoma.Das 30 · p.416 The reason is the TDLU distribution: 50% of terminal ductal lobular units lie there.B&L 58 · p.932
Consistency
An encapsulated tumour is firm or globular; carcinoma is stony hard; in sarcoma consistency may vary from place to place.Das 30 · p.418
Fluctuation, done properly
Stand behind the seated patient with both hands over her shoulders. Hold the cyst with one hand and tap its centre gently with the index finger of the other. Positive also in a chronic abscess, which may not be tender, and in a lipoma. A very tense cyst may not fluctuate.Das 30 · p.418 So a negative fluctuation test does not exclude a cyst, and a tense cyst can feel hard enough to be mistaken for a carcinoma: let the ultrasound, not your finger, decide whether a lump is cystic.B&L 58 · p.915
Transillumination
Only in a dark room. Torch on the undersurface of the breast, light directed through to the examiner. Fat is translucent, a solid tumour is opaque.Das 30 · p.418
Tethering versus fixity: learn this verbatim These two words are the commonest place to lose marks in the whole case, and S Das defines both.Das 30 · p.418

Tethered means the disease has spread to the fine fibrous septa running from the glandular tissue to the skin, Astley Cooper's ligaments. Infiltration makes them shorter and inelastic, pulling the skin inwards and puckering it. The lump can still be moved independently of the skin for some distance, after which the skin dimples. So you test for tethering by moving the lump side to side and watching for a dimple at the extremes of movement.

Fixity means direct and continuous infiltration of the skin by the tumour: the lump cannot be moved independently of the skin at all, and the overlying skin cannot be pinched up.

And the sign that connects this to peau d'orange: when a wide area of skin over the tumour is attempted to be pinched up, peau d'orange becomes prominent.Das 30 · p.418
Fixity to the breast tissue, and the eponym that follows
Hold the breast tissue with one hand and gently move the tumour with the other. A fibroadenoma is not fixed to the breast tissue and moves easily within the breast substance, which is why it is called a "breast mouse". A carcinoma is fixed to the breast substance and cannot be moved within it, and fibrous strands can be felt radiating from the mass into the breast substance.Das 30 · p.418
Fixity to pectoralis major, step by step
Ask the patient to place her hand on her hip lightly. Move the lump along the direction of the pectoralis major fibres, then at right angles to them: it should be mobile both ways. Now ask her to press on the hip as hard as she can, and feel the anterior axillary fold to verify the muscle is taut. Move the lump again in the same two directions and compare the range. Restriction indicates fixity to the pectoral fascia and pectoralis major, and if truly fixed there is total restriction along the line of the muscle fibres with perhaps slight movement at right angles to them.Das 30 · p.418 Verifying that the muscle is actually contracted is the step candidates skip and examiners watch for.
Fixity to serratus anterior
For a lump in the outer and lower quadrant, which lies on serratus anterior. Ask the patient to push against a wall with the outstretched hand of the affected side while you test mobility; if fixed, the swelling moves very little.Das 30 · p.418
And fixity to the chest wall
The end of the same sequence: a tumour fixed with the muscle relaxed is fixed to the chest wall itself. B&L's pathological sequence is the justification: the tumour invades adjacent parenchyma, then skin with ulceration and satellite nodules, then pectoralis major, serratus anterior and even the chest wall.B&L 58 · p.932

The axilla, in the order S Das palpates it

Pectoral (anterior) group
Just behind the anterior axillary fold. Support the patient's arm so it rests comfortably on your forearm, which relaxes the fold and lets your fingers behind it.Das 30 · p.419 These are the sentinel nodes in most patients.B&L 58 · p.915
Central group
In the fat of the centre of the axilla, felt against the chest wall.Das 30 · p.421
Subscapular (posterior) group
On the posterior axillary fold; palpated from behind.Das 30 · p.420
Lateral (brachial) group
Along the medial side of the upper arm against the humerus, up to the axillary vein.Das 30 · p.421
Apical, and then supraclavicular
Apical nodes above and medial to the pectoralis minor tendon. Then the supraclavicular fossa, and S Das's instruction: both sides should be palpated.Das 30 · p.421
Why that order
Because spread follows it: first to the pectoral, then the central, then the apical group, with the subscapular and lateral groups involved later.Das 30 · p.420 Reporting nodes in the sequence disease takes shows you understand the anatomy rather than reciting a list.
And the contralateral axilla
Involvement of contralateral nodes in the absence of a contralateral primary represents metastatic disease. B&L gives five routes, and the elaborate one is worth knowing because it gets asked: from one breast via the ipsilateral internal mammary nodes, then the interconnecting lymphatics behind the sternum, then the contralateral internal mammary nodes, then the other breast, then the opposite axilla. The others are haematogenous spread from a contralateral primary; spread via subdermal lymphatics in front of the sternum; a tumour in an epithelial embryonic cell rest trapped in a node, which is rare; and simply a primary tumour of the opposite breast.B&L 58 · p.932
Why a lump becomes a cancer, in one paragraph of mechanism The tumour releases fibroblast growth factor, transforming growth factors alpha and beta and vascular endothelial growth factor. FGF induces mitosis in adjacent fibrocytes, which become fibroblasts and lay down collagen: the desmoplastic reaction. Contraction of that collagen shortens Cooper's ligaments, producing dimpling from one shortened ligament, puckering or tethering from many, or nipple retraction. And at a tumour size of only 1 to 2 mm, about 105 cells, neoangiogenesis occurs, which ushers in rapid growth, invasion and metastatic potential.B&L 58 · p.932 That paragraph connects every physical sign you have just elicited to a molecular cause, and it is the single most useful thing to be able to say in this case.

7Treatment, and the reasoning behind each choice

The sentence that frames the whole answer "There has been a general de-escalation towards more conservative techniques, backed up by clinical trials and meta-analyses showing equal efficacy in locoregional cancer control and survival between mastectomy and wide local excision or breast conservation surgery followed by radiotherapy."B&L 58 · p.935 Every modern answer in this case follows from that: smaller breast operation plus radiotherapy, smaller axillary operation, and systemic therapy chosen by tumour biology rather than by size alone.

The aim, and what counts as a clear margin

The aim
Remove all disease in the breast and the axilla with negative margins.B&L 58 · p.935
The margin rule, which differs by pathology
Indelible India ink is applied to the specimen surfaces and the pathologist reports the distance from tumour to the nearest margin. For invasive cancer there should be no tumour cells on the inked margin. For DCIS, a minimum of 2 mm is considered safe.B&L 58 · p.935 "No ink on tumour" for invasive, "2 mm" for DCIS, is the pair to memorise.
Orient the specimen, or you cannot act on the report
Sutures: long lateral, "L" for lateral; short superior, "S" for superior. This matters if a margin is positive. Patients with involved margins should have a revision of margins called a "cavity shave".B&L 58 · p.935

Surgery to the breast: mastectomy or conservation

When mastectomy
Large tumours in relation to breast size; multicentric disease; diffuse microcalcification on mammography indicating DCIS; BRCA-positive cancers; local recurrence after breast conservation; or the patient's preference.B&L 58 · p.935
How far the breast actually extends, which 28e has changed
Breast tissue extends to where the anterior premammary fascia fuses with the posterior pectoral fascia, so remove to that point of fusion: second rib above, parasternal edge medially, inframammary crease below, anterior border of latissimus dorsi laterally. B&L flags this explicitly as different from the traditional view of raising flaps up to the inferior border of the clavicle and 2 to 3 cm below the inframammary crease.B&L 58 · p.935 If you describe the old flap limits, know that you are describing the old ones.
When breast conservation
BCS removes the tumour with a 1 cm margin of normal breast tissue, and all patients with BCS receive radiotherapy. The equation to quote: BCS + RT = BCT.B&L 58 · p.935
When conservation is best avoided, which is the better question
Multicentric tumour; diffuse microcalcification; a large tumour-to-breast ratio; twice positive surgical margins after re-excision; previous breast or chest wall radiation; systemic lupus erythematosus or other collagen vascular disease, because of a high risk of radiation reaction; ankylosing spondylitis; and severe orthopnoea, because the patient cannot lie on the radiation table.B&L 58 · p.935 That last one is a beautifully concrete reason and worth remembering.
SRB's overlapping listEarlier breast irradiation; tumour beneath the nipple; extensive intraductal carcinoma.SRB 8 · p.548
Skin- and nipple-sparing mastectomy
An option in DCIS and early cancer where mastectomy is indicated and the tumour is more than 1 cm from the skin and more than 2 cm from the nipple; the breast is then reconstructed with autologous tissue, fat, or a silicone implant.B&L 58 · p.935
Oncoplasty, and the 20% rule
Wide local excision of up to 20% of breast volume can be closed by approximating breast tissue. Volume loss greater than 20%, or an unfavourable breast-to-tumour ratio, requires an oncoplastic procedure. Radical mastectomy, and why it is history

Surgery to the axilla

The two jobs
Axillary surgery exists to stage the patient, by sentinel lymph node biopsy, and to treat disease, by axillary lymph node dissection in node-positive patients.B&L 58 · p.936 Say which job you are doing and you will not be caught out.
Sentinel node biopsy: the concept
Sentinel means "a guard". Like a guard, the first echelon of axillary nodes sits at the gateway of the axilla and reports on the status of the rest.B&L 58 · p.936 SRB: the first node draining the breast by direct drainage; its involvement or otherwise predicts the rest of the axilla.SRB 8 · p.536 Anatomically these are usually the anterior or pectoral group.B&L 58 · p.915
How it is found, with the detection rates
Blue dye (patent blue or methylene blue) and technetium-99m-labelled albumin, sulphur colloid or antimony. Where radioisotope is unavailable, fluorescein or indocyanine green. Which nodes come out
All nodes with more than 10% of the ex vivo count of the hottest node, plus all blue nodes.B&L 58 · p.936

Systemic therapy, enough to answer without reciting

Neoadjuvant systemic therapy, and what it is for
NAST is chemotherapy, targeted therapy or hormonal therapy before surgery, to downsize disease. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is used in locally advanced disease, inflammatory cancer, triple negative cancer, premenopausal women under 50, and patients with axillary node metastasis. Neoadjuvant trastuzumab and pertuzumab for HER2/neu-positive tumours over 5 mm.B&L 58 · p.933
How response is reported, and what to do with each answer
RECIST, four categories: complete response, not detectable on palpation or imaging; partial response, 30% or more reduction in maximal diameter; stable disease, under 30% reduction; progressive disease, 20% or more increase. With complete or partial response, the entire regimen may be delivered before surgery. With stable or progressive disease after the initial two cycles, operate and give second-line chemotherapy afterwards.B&L 58 · p.935
The practical problem neoadjuvant therapy creates, and the low-cost answer
If the tumour vanishes, the surgeon cannot find it. So a radio-opaque clip or magnetic marker is placed under image guidance at the epicentre before treatment. If clip placement is unavailable, a 0.5 cm piece of silicone or a polyvinylchloride catheter tip may be inserted through a small skin incision just anterior to the tumour: it remains palpable even after complete regression and lets the surgeon excise 2 cm all round it.B&L 58 · p.935 That improvisation is in the book, it is clearly written for a resource-limited theatre, and almost no candidate will know it.
Adjuvant chemotherapy: the benefit, and who gets it
Purpose: control micrometastases, delay relapse, prolong survival. The NSABP trials and the Oxford overview meta-analyses by the EBCTCG show chemotherapy improves relapse-free survival by approximately 30% and overall survival by 10% at 15 years.B&L 58 · p.940 Indicated for all invasive carcinomas over 1 cm, tumours over 0.5 cm with poor prognostic factors (lymphovascular invasion, high grade, HER2 positive, triple negative), and node-positive tumours.B&L 58 · p.940 Regimens: CMF; anthracycline-based CAF or CEF; and taxane-based regimens.B&L 58 · p.940
By molecular subtype, which is how it is actually decided
Luminal A may avoid chemotherapy with a low Oncotype DX or clinical risk score. Luminal B should receive anthracycline or taxane, because of the greater risk of relapse. HER2-positive: trastuzumab plus pertuzumab with taxane and anthracycline. Triple negative: taxane and anthracycline, with carboplatin-based regimens possibly beneficial for aggressive biology.B&L 58 · p.940
Gene signatures, and the one number to quote
For ER-positive, HER2-negative, node-negative tumours: Oncotype DX (21-gene), Prosigna PAM-50, MammaPrint (70-gene). Oncotype DX is the most widely used and measures 16 cancer-related and 5 reference genes; it scores risk of recurrence as low under 18, moderate 19 to 30, high over 30. In patients with a low score, chemotherapy can be avoided.B&L 58 · p.940
Hormone therapy, with durations
Premenopausal: tamoxifen only, for 5 years in low-risk patients and 10 years where the risk of relapse is high (node positive, tumour over 5 cm, lymphovascular invasion). Postmenopausal: aromatase inhibitors, anastrozole, letrozole or exemestane, which beat tamoxifen for relapse-free and overall survival but are more expensive and cause bone density loss and fracture risk, so a bone density scan is advised before starting and bisphosphonates with vitamin D and calcium are used, which may also reduce recurrence.B&L 58 · p.941
Targeted therapy
Trastuzumab (Herceptin) against the HER2/neu receptor, with pertuzumab and chemotherapy. T-DM1 conjugates the cytotoxic emtansine to trastuzumab, delivering chemotherapy to HER2-positive cells selectively.B&L 58 · p.941
Follow-up
Clinical examination every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 3 years, then yearly; mammogram yearly. Contrast breast MRI annually for patients with an implant and for those with BRCA or other genetic mutations.B&L 58 · p.941
Prognostic factors, if asked for a list Size of tumour, stage, axillary node involvement and grade, which are the first three of which the Nottingham Prognostic Index is built, plus: histopathological variant, with metaplastic carcinoma aggressive; HER2/neu positive and triple negative; lymphovascular invasion; an extensive DCIS component; high Ki-67; younger age and premenopausal status; BRCA-associated; family history; prior history; obesity and a sedentary lifestyle; and failure to complete intended treatment.B&L 58 · p.942 That last item is the one worth saying aloud in an Indian clinic, because it is the one the surgeon can actually do something about.

Special situations, in a sentence each

Paget's disease of the nipple
A unique type of DCIS arising in the nipple, presenting as erosion of the nipple and areola, and it may become invasive with metastasis to the axillary nodes. Triple assessment is needed to exclude underlying malignancy. Without underlying malignancy: central core excision of the nipple and areola down to pectoralis major, followed by radiotherapy. With underlying malignancy: mastectomy and evaluation of the axilla.B&L 58 · p.924
Male breast cancer
Under 0.5% of breast cancers. Commonest presentation a painless subareolar lump, and involvement of the nipple-areolar complex and underlying pectoral muscle occurs early. Treatment: mastectomy with a 2 cm margin along with a portion of underlying pectoralis major, then radiotherapy; sentinel node biopsy if node-negative; tamoxifen 20 mg daily for 5 years if ER positive.B&L 58 · p.942 And BRCA mutation is more common in males with breast cancer, so offer genetic evaluation.B&L 58 · p.941, p.942
Breast cancer in pregnancy
Associated with aggressive biology such as triple negative. Ultrasound, mammogram and chest radiograph with abdominal shielding; MRI without gadolinium if needed; CT and PET-CT should be avoided. Surgery can be performed in any trimester, and mastectomy is preferred in the first and second trimester, because delaying radiotherapy until delivery raises the risk of recurrence in the breast. Sentinel node biopsy with low-dose technetium is considered safe for the fetus. Chemotherapy must not be given in the first trimester, the period of organogenesis, but is safe in the second and third until 34 weeks; anthracyclines and taxanes are preferred and 5-fluorouracil should be avoided. Anti-HER2 and endocrine therapy wait until after delivery.B&L 58 · p.941, p.942
Hereditary and familial disease
Hereditary breast cancer has an identifiable mutation and accounts for 5 to 10%; familial affects several relatives with no known mutation and accounts for 20 to 30%. Hereditary cancers are more aggressive, earlier, more often multicentric and bilateral. BRCA1 (17q21): 50 to 85% lifetime breast cancer risk, up to 40% ovarian, and mostly triple negative tumours. BRCA2 (13q12.3): 50 to 60% breast, 20% ovarian, and also prostate, colon, gallbladder, bile duct, stomach and pancreas.B&L 58 · p.941
Risk reduction, with the numbersBilateral risk-reducing mastectomy with immediate reconstruction reduces risk by 90%; chemoprophylaxis with tamoxifen or anastrozole by 50%; and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy may be offered once the family is complete, at around 35 to 40 years.B&L 58 · p.941
Metastatic disease
Aim: palliate symptoms, improve quality of life, prevent disabling complications, attempt to prolong life. Endocrine therapy for hormone receptor-positive disease with bony and limited visceral metastasis; chemotherapy for receptor-negative, hormone-refractory, or visceral crisis. Oral low-dose metronomic chemotherapy has cytostatic and antiangiogenic effects and may improve quality of life. Bony metastases: palliative radiotherapy to weight-bearing and painful sites, with bisphosphonates. Pleural effusion: intercostal drainage and pleurodesis.B&L 58 · p.941
Local recurrence
Biopsy it, because receptor status may have changed and that changes therapy. Stage with whole-body MRI or PET-CT. Systemic chemotherapy then surgical excision; most surgeons perform mastectomy, though second BCS with re-irradiation may be considered.B&L 58 · p.941
Sarcoma and angiosarcoma
Fibrosarcoma and angiosarcoma may arise de novo. Associated with Li-Fraumeni, neurofibromatosis type 1, alkylating agents, vinyl chloride, arsenic, prior radiotherapy and chronic lymphoedema. Angiosarcoma is the most aggressive of all breast tumours, arises from vascular or lymphatic endothelium, is associated with prior radiotherapy and carries a very poor prognosis.B&L 58 · p.942

What is triple assessment, and how accurate is it?

Clinical examination, radiological imaging, and tissue sampling for cytology or histology, applied together to every patient with a breast lump, nipple discharge or other breast symptom. Bailey and Love gives a confident diagnosis in 99.9% of cases, with the positive predictive value and diagnostic accuracy of the combination at 100%.B&L 58 · p.915 SRB's version specifies mammography after 40 and FNAC or core biopsy for the third limb.SRB 8 · p.538

Why is the upper outer quadrant the commonest site?

Because about 50% of the ductolobular tissue, and so 50% of terminal ductal lobular units, lies there, and most breast disease arises from the TDLU. About 20% lies centrally, which is why examination must also concentrate on the retroareolar region and the nipple-areola complex.B&L 58 · p.914, p.932

Distinguish tethering from fixity, and say how you test each.

Tethering means malignant spread to Astley Cooper's ligaments, the fine fibrous septa from glandular tissue to skin; infiltration shortens them and makes them inelastic, puckering the skin, but the lump can still be moved independently of the skin for some distance. Test it by moving the lump side to side and watching for a dimple at the extremes of movement. Fixity means direct continuous infiltration of the skin: the lump cannot be moved independently of the skin and the skin cannot be pinched up.Das 30 · p.418 And if you try to pinch up a wide area of skin over the tumour, peau d'orange becomes prominent.

How do you test fixity to pectoralis major?

Hand on the hip lightly first: move the lump along the line of the pectoralis major fibres and then at right angles to them, and it should be mobile both ways. Then ask the patient to press hard on the hip, and feel the anterior axillary fold to confirm the muscle is actually taut, which is the step most candidates omit. Move the lump again in both directions and compare the range. Restriction means fixity to the pectoral fascia and muscle, and if truly fixed there is total restriction along the line of the fibres with perhaps slight movement at right angles.Das 30 · p.418 For an outer and lower quadrant lump, test serratus anterior instead by asking her to push against a wall with the outstretched hand of that side.

What causes peau d'orange, and does it mean the skin is invaded?

No, it does not. It is obstruction of cutaneous lymphatic drainage, and Bailey and Love is explicit that the obstruction may be either infiltration of subdermal lymphatics or infiltration of the axillary lymph nodes, so the skin itself need not be involved.B&L 58 · p.932 The oedematous skin is tethered at the openings of the hair follicles and their associated glands, giving the orange-peel pitting.SRB 8 · p.527 It is a sign of locally advanced disease. In a dark-skinned patient the accompanying erythema is brownish rather than red.B&L 58 · p.932

Why is a painless lump more worrying, not less?

Because carcinoma of the breast is a painless condition, and all breast neoplasms, benign or malignant, are painless to start with. S Das's instruction is that a painless lump found accidentally while washing may be a carcinoma and the clinician must be more particular about examining that case rather than ignoring it.Das 30 · p.410 Cyclical pain before menstruation in a young woman suggests ANDI instead. But pain does occur in about 10% of carcinomas,SRB 8 · p.527 so neither its presence nor its absence settles anything alone.

Name the three axillary levels.

All defined by pectoralis minor. Level I, below and lateral to its lateral border, which is the majority of nodes. Level II, in front of and behind the muscle, including the interpectoral or Rotter's nodes. Level III, above and medial to its medial border.B&L 58 · p.915 The apical nodes receive the efferents from all the other axillary groups and are continuous with the supraclavicular nodes.

Which axillary group do you palpate first, and why?

The pectoral or anterior group, just behind the anterior axillary fold, with the patient's arm resting on your forearm so the fold relaxes. Then central, subscapular, lateral or brachial, apical, and finally both supraclavicular fossae.Das 30 · p.419–421 The order matters because spread follows it: pectoral first, then central, then apical, with the subscapular and lateral groups involved later.Das 30 · p.420 The pectoral group is also the sentinel node in most patients.B&L 58 · p.915

There is a palpable node in the opposite axilla and no lump in that breast. Explain it.

Contralateral nodal involvement without a contralateral primary represents metastatic disease. Five routes: haematogenous spread from a contralateral primary; via subdermal lymphatics in front of the sternum; via the ipsilateral internal mammary nodes to the interconnecting lymphatics behind the sternum, to the contralateral internal mammary nodes, to the other breast and then its axilla; a tumour developing in an epithelial embryonic cell rest trapped in a node, which is rare; and simply an occult primary in the opposite breast.B&L 58 · p.932

Ultrasound or mammogram first?

Both are first-line, but the choice is by age. Ultrasound is the primary modality in young women with dense breasts, where mammograms are hard to interpret, and it distinguishes cystic from solid. Mammography should be the first investigation in older women with breast symptoms.B&L 58 · p.915, p.916 SRB puts mammography after 40.SRB 8 · p.538 And neither is diagnostic: biopsy is required for a BI-RADS 4 or 5.B&L 58 · p.916

A mammogram is reported BI-RADS 4b. What does that mean and what do you do?

A suspicious abnormality with intermediate suspicion of malignancy, corresponding to a probability of more than 10% and up to 50%. Biopsy should be considered.B&L 58 · p.917 For context: BI-RADS 3 is probably benign at 2% or less and gets six-month follow-up; BI-RADS 5 is 95% or more and requires biopsy or surgical treatment; BI-RADS 0 means the assessment is incomplete and more imaging is needed.

Why might you avoid PET in an Indian patient?

Two reasons, both in the book. Inflammatory lesions, specifically mediastinal lymphadenopathy and pleuropulmonary lesions due to pulmonary tuberculosis, give false-positive results, especially in Asian patients. And PET is very expensive and insurance may not cover it.B&L 58 · p.917 Its proper indications are staging T3, T4, N2 or N3 disease, or T1 to T2, N0 to N1 disease when there are symptoms or signs suggesting metastasis.

Which early breast cancer patients need staging scans?

Patients with T1, T2 and N0, N1 disease need metastatic evaluation only if they have symptoms suggesting metastasis or a raised serum alkaline phosphatase. Locally advanced disease, T3, T4, N2 or N3, needs contrast-enhanced CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis plus an isotope bone scan.B&L 58 · p.933 Where cross-sectional imaging is limited, SRB's work-up is a chest radiograph, ultrasound abdomen and an isotope bone scan, which is a must in locally advanced disease.SRB 8 · p.538

What is an adequate margin?

For invasive cancer, no tumour cells on the inked margin. For DCIS, a minimum of 2 mm. Indelible India ink is applied to the specimen surfaces and the pathologist reports the distance from tumour to the nearest margin. Breast conservation removes the tumour with a 1 cm margin of normal tissue. Involved margins get a revision called a cavity shave, and the specimen must be oriented with sutures, long lateral and short superior, or you cannot act on the report.B&L 58 · p.935

Mastectomy or breast conservation?

Trials and meta-analyses show equal locoregional control and survival between mastectomy and wide local excision followed by radiotherapy, so conservation is preferred where possible.B&L 58 · p.935 Mastectomy is indicated for a large tumour relative to breast size, multicentric disease, diffuse microcalcification indicating DCIS, BRCA-positive cancer, local recurrence after conservation, or the patient's preference. Conservation is best avoided with multicentric tumour, diffuse microcalcification, a large tumour-to-breast ratio, twice-positive margins after re-excision, previous chest wall radiation, collagen vascular disease or ankylosing spondylitis because of radiation reaction, and severe orthopnoea, since the patient cannot lie on the radiation table.B&L 58 · p.935

What is BCT?

Breast conservation therapy, which is breast conservation surgery plus radiotherapy: BCS + RT = BCT. All patients having breast conservation surgery receive radiotherapy, which is why the two are named as one treatment.B&L 58 · p.935

Describe Patey's operation, and say how Scanlon and Auchincloss differ.

Patey's is a total mastectomy with en bloc axillary clearance of all levels. An elliptical incision encloses nipple, areola and tumour, extending into the axilla along the anterior axillary fold; flaps are raised; the breast is lifted off pectoralis major; level I nodes are cleared at the lateral border of pectoralis major; pectoralis minor is divided from the coracoid to clear level II and the interpectoral nodes; and level III is cleared from the apex. Medial and lateral pectoral nerves are preserved, or the pectoral muscle atrophies, as are the nerves to serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi, the intercostobrachial nerve, the axillary and cephalic veins and pectoralis major.SRB 8 · p.538 Scanlon incises rather than removes pectoralis minor to reach level III. Auchincloss leaves pectoralis minor intact and does not take level III, clearing Rotter's nodes by sweeping a finger under pectoralis major, and it is what is commonly done now.SRB 8 · p.539 Bailey and Love puts the same thing as one axis: pectoralis minor removed in Patey and Madden, divided but not removed in Scanlon, retracted in Auchincloss.B&L 58 · p.937

Why is Halsted's radical mastectomy not done?

It removed the breast, all the axillary nodes and both pectoralis major and minor, and causes excessive morbidity: limited shoulder movement, extensive upper limb lymphoedema, pain and chest wall deformity, with no survival benefit over less radical surgery.B&L 58 · p.935

What is a sentinel node, and how do you find it?

Sentinel means a guard: the first echelon node in the axilla draining the breast, sitting at the gateway of the axilla, whose status predicts the rest. Anatomically it is usually in the anterior or pectoral group. It is found with blue dye, patent blue or methylene blue, and technetium-99m-labelled albumin, sulphur colloid or antimony; where radioisotope is unavailable, fluorescein or indocyanine green, the latter identifying 95 to 100% of sentinel nodes. The dye is injected peritumorally, periareolarly, subareolarly or intradermally, and the node is detected as blue, as hot on a gamma probe, or as fluorescent. All blue nodes and all nodes with more than 10% of the ex vivo count of the hottest node are removed.B&L 58 · p.915, p.936

When can you not do a sentinel node biopsy?

Inflammatory breast cancer, T4 disease, previous breast or chest wall surgery, breast scarring from burns, and previous radiotherapy.B&L 58 · p.937 The reason in each case is that the lymphatic trunks are disrupted, so the false-negative rate is high.SRB 8 · p.536 Note also that frozen-section evaluation of a sentinel node itself has a false-negative rate of 10 to 12%.B&L 58 · p.937

Which structures must you protect during an axillary dissection?

The axillary vein, the thoracodorsal vessels, the medial and lateral pectoral nerves, the intercostobrachial nerves, and the long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerves. The intercostobrachial nerve may be deliberately divided when the nodal burden is heavy, to achieve oncological clearance.B&L 58 · p.937 Losing the pectoral nerves causes atrophy of the pectoral muscle.SRB 8 · p.538 One anatomical point: the axillary vein is not covered by axillary fascia, whereas the axillary artery and brachial plexus lie within it.SRB 8 · p.539

Calculate and interpret the Nottingham Prognostic Index.

NPI = (0.2 × tumour size in cm) + lymph node stage + tumour grade. Classically three bands: under 3.4 good with 80% survival at 15 years, 3.4 to 5.4 moderate with 40%, over 5.4 poor with 15%.SRB 8 · p.525 Bailey and Love 28e uses four bands, subdividing the good group at 2.4: I excellent 2.4 or less, II good over 2.4 to 3.4, III moderate over 3.4 to 5.4, IV poor over 5.4.B&L 58 · p.942 Give the formula and the 3.4 and 5.4 cut-offs, which both books share.

How is tumour grade determined?

The modified Bloom-Richardson system, scoring three variables 1 to 3 each: the percentage of tumour cells with tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and the size and number of mitoses per high-power field. Total 3 to 5 is grade I, 6 or 7 grade II, 8 or 9 grade III.B&L 58 · p.931

Name the molecular subtypes and say how each changes treatment.

Luminal A, hormone receptor positive with low Ki-67, which may avoid chemotherapy altogether if the Oncotype DX or clinical risk score is low. Luminal B, receptor positive with high Ki-67, which should have anthracycline or taxane because relapse risk is higher. HER2/neu enriched, which gets trastuzumab and pertuzumab with taxane and anthracycline. Basal or triple negative, which gets taxane and anthracycline, with carboplatin possibly beneficial in aggressive biology. And a normal-like group. Where gene array such as PAM-50 is not available, immunohistochemical receptor staining is the surrogate marker.B&L 58 · p.931, p.940

Who gets neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and how do you judge the response?

Locally advanced disease, inflammatory cancer, triple negative cancer, premenopausal women under 50, and node-positive patients; plus neoadjuvant trastuzumab and pertuzumab for HER2-positive tumours over 5 mm. Response is reported by RECIST: complete response, not detectable on palpation or imaging; partial, 30% or more reduction in maximal diameter; stable, under 30% reduction; progressive, 20% or more increase. Complete or partial response means the whole regimen may be given before surgery; stable or progressive disease after two cycles means operate now and give second-line chemotherapy afterwards.B&L 58 · p.933, p.935

The tumour has disappeared on neoadjuvant chemotherapy and you planned conservation. How will you find it?

A radio-opaque clip or magnetic marker placed under image guidance at the epicentre of the tumour before treatment. If clips are unavailable, a 0.5 cm piece of silicone or a polyvinylchloride catheter tip can be inserted through a small skin incision just anterior to the tumour; it stays palpable even after complete regression, and the surgeon excises 2 cm of tissue all round it.B&L 58 · p.935

Tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor, and for how long?

Premenopausal patients get tamoxifen only, for 5 years in low-risk disease and 10 years where relapse risk is high, meaning node positive, tumour over 5 cm, or lymphovascular invasion. Postmenopausal women get an aromatase inhibitor, anastrozole, letrozole or exemestane, which outperform tamoxifen for relapse-free and overall survival, but are more expensive and cause bone density loss and fracture risk, so get a bone density scan before starting and add bisphosphonates with vitamin D and calcium, which may themselves reduce recurrence.B&L 58 · p.941

An erosion of the nipple that will not heal. What is it and what do you do?

Paget's disease of the nipple, a unique type of ductal carcinoma in situ arising in the nipple, which may become invasive and metastasise to the axillary nodes. Take a wedge biopsy of the lesion, and do triple assessment to exclude underlying malignancy. Without underlying malignancy: central core excision of nipple and areola down to pectoralis major, followed by radiotherapy. With underlying malignancy: mastectomy and evaluation of the axilla.B&L 58 · p.924

A hot, red, painful breast in a non-lactating woman. What are you worried about?

Inflammatory carcinoma. The threshold all three books use is skin involvement of more than one-third of the breast, with diffuse erythema and skin oedema, often with peau d'orange and often extending onto the chest wall.B&L 58 · p.932SRB 8 · p.525 S Das's name for it is acute mastitis carcinomatosa, or acute lactational carcinoma, and he warns it can mimic acute mastitis exactly.Das 30 · p.412, p.416 Remember also that in a dark-skinned patient the erythema is brownish rather than red. And a sentinel node biopsy is contraindicated in inflammatory cancer.

A breast mass with weight loss in a 25-year-old Indian woman. What else is on your list?

Tuberculosis of the breast, and chest wall tuberculosis producing a retromammary abscess, both of which S Das lists as causes of weight loss with a breast mass, and tuberculosis of the breast may recur.Das 30 · p.411 This is a differential the British text does not emphasise and an Indian examiner expects. Carcinoma remains on the list: Indian women present at a median of about 48 years, and age is never a reason to exclude it.B&L 58 · p.931Das 30 · p.410

Why is a mobile lump in the breast of a 22-year-old called a breast mouse?

Because a fibroadenoma is not fixed to the breast tissue and can be moved easily within the breast substance, unlike a carcinoma, which is fixed to the breast substance with fibrous strands radiating from it.Das 30 · p.418 On ultrasound it is a well-circumscribed mobile solid mass with an extremely low likelihood of malignancy, so reassurance and imaging follow-up are appropriate.B&L 58 · p.915

Where do breast secondaries go, and why above the elbow and knee?

Haematogenous spread goes to the skeleton, in order of frequency lumbar vertebrae, neck of femur, thoracic vertebrae, rib and skull, generally osteolytic; and to liver, lungs and brain, occasionally adrenals and ovaries. In the limbs the deposits are above the elbow and above the knee, because haematopoietic vascular bone marrow is confined to the axial skeleton and, in the limbs, to above those joints. Extensive marrow replacement releases immature blast cells and gives leukoerythroblastic anaemia.B&L 58 · p.932

At what size does a breast tumour acquire metastatic potential?

At 1 to 2 mm, about 105 cells, when neoangiogenesis occurs. The onset of angiogenesis ushers in rapid growth, invasion and metastatic potential.B&L 58 · p.932

How is breast cancer screened for in India?

Not by mammography, which is not available as a population programme in low- and middle-income countries. India's national screening is multidisease screening for cancer of the mouth, breast and cervix for all women aged 30 to 65, using clinical breast examination by a trained healthcare professional, with breast health awareness through self-examination.B&L 58 · p.943 Keep the two roles distinct: clinical breast examination can detect disease early, whereas self-examination makes women aware and brings them in sooner. For comparison, the UK invites women aged 50 to 70 for mammography every 3 years, and opportunistic screening mammography is usually offered from 40.SRB 8 · p.507

What is ANDI, and why does it matter?

Aberrations of Normal Development and Involution, a concept developed by the Cardiff Breast Clinic to replace the confusing older labels fibroadenosis, fibrocystic disease and mammary dysplasia, which treated normal variation as disease.B&L 58 · p.918 Nodularity under it is often bilateral, most conspicuous in the upper outer quadrant, and may be cyclical, appearing one to two weeks before menstruation; it is graded on the Cardiff-Lucknow nodularity scale, and painless nodularity still gets triple assessment.B&L 58 · p.919, p.921 Treatment after normal imaging is reassurance, or an antioestrogen: tamoxifen 10 mg daily, ormeloxifene 30 mg, or danazol 50 to 300 mg daily, for 3 to 6 months.B&L 58 · p.921

Would you offer Manchester staging?

No. SRB itself prints that Manchester staging and the Columbia classification are obsolete and not used now, referring readers who want them to its own fourth edition.SRB 8 · p.533 Stage with the eighth edition UICC-AJCC TNM system, which also incorporates grade, ER, PR, HER2/neu, Ki-67, multigene testing and neoadjuvant response.B&L 58 · p.933

A man with a subareolar lump. How does management differ?

Male breast cancer is under 0.5% of all breast cancer, presents most often as a painless subareolar lump, and involves the nipple-areolar complex and underlying pectoral muscle early. Treatment is mastectomy with a 2 cm margin including a portion of underlying pectoralis major, followed by radiotherapy, with sentinel node biopsy if node-negative and tamoxifen 20 mg daily for 5 years if ER positive.B&L 58 · p.942 BRCA mutation is more common in men with breast cancer, so offer genetic evaluation.
