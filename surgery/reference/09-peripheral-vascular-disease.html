Long case 09 · General Surgery

Peripheral Vascular Disease

A case you pass by being precise about pain. Three questions separate claudication from every mimic, one angle tells you how ischaemic the limb is, and one word, whether you say critical or limb-threatening, tells the examiner which edition you read.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1The case in one breath

The principle that organises everything
B&L: arterial disease produces symptoms and signs that relate to the organ supplied by the artery: lower limb, claudication, rest pain and gangrene; brain, transient ischaemic attacks and stroke; myocardium, angina and myocardial infarction; intestine, abdominal pain and infarction.B&L 61 · p.997 So a patient with claudication has a systemic disease, and you must examine for the other three.
And what determines severity
"The severity of the symptoms relates to the size of the vessel occluded and whether the stenosis or occlusion occurs suddenly (acute) in a previously normal artery or gradually (chronic) with progressive narrowing over time."B&L 61 · p.997 Gradual narrowing allows collaterals, which is why a complete chronic occlusion can hurt less than an acute partial one.
Causes, as SRB lists them
Atherosclerosis; embolism, which is acute; the arteriopathies, that is Buerger's disease, Raynaud's disease and Takayasu's disease; diabetes; scleroderma; and physical agents such as trauma, tourniquet and radiation injury.SRB 1 · p.163
Where the eponym comes from
B&L's footnote: claudication from the Latin claudicare, to limp. The Roman emperor Claudius walked with a limp, possibly due to poliomyelitis.B&L 61 · p.997 S Das makes the same point and then corrects the implication: "though literally 'claudio' means 'I limp', yet the term claudication is used here to describe the muscle pain due to accumulation of excessive" metabolites.Das 6 · p.80 The patient does not limp; he stops.
Who gets which disease, which is the first thing to settle S Das's epidemiological discriminators, and they are worth memorising because they narrow the case before you touch the patient.Das 6 · p.80

Atherosclerosis: a disease of old age, affecting men more often than women.
Buerger's disease, thromboangiitis obliterans: men between 20 and 40.
Raynaud's disease: young women.
Diabetic arteriopathy: middle age.

And by limb: Buerger's disease and atherosclerotic gangrene commonly affect the lower limbs, whereas Raynaud's affects the upper. By laterality: Buerger's and Raynaud's are usually bilateral, atherosclerosis more often unilateral.Das 6 · p.80

2The two pains, and how to be precise about them

Intermittent claudication: three features that exclude every mimic B&L's definition is built to be discriminating, and each clause excludes something specific. Claudication occurs as a result of anaerobic muscle metabolism and is debilitating cramp-like pain felt in the muscles that is:B&L 61 · p.998

1. Brought on by walking.
2. Not present on taking the first step, unlike osteoarthritis.
3. Relieved by rest in both the standing and sitting positions, usually within 5 minutes, unlike nerve compression from a lumbar disc prolapse, spinal osteoarthritis or spinal stenosis, which are typically relieved only when resting in the sitting position for longer than 5 minutes.

And one more: the claudication distance varies only slightly from day to day.B&L 61 · p.998

So the three questions are: does it come on with walking, is the first step painful, and does standing still relieve it? If standing still relieves it, it is vascular; if he has to sit down for ten minutes, it is neurogenic.
The mechanism, in S Das's words
Claudication is muscle pain due to accumulation of excessive metabolites, and "the patient often complains that after walking a distance, called the claudication distance, the pain starts".Das 6 · p.80
Walking through the pain, which SRB explains elegantly
SRB's Boyd grade I: the patient complains of pain after walking, and if he continues to walk, increased blood flow in the muscle and opening of collaterals wash out the metabolites causing the pain, so the pain can ease.SRB 1 · p.162 S Das notes the same phenomenon: if the patient continues to walk, the metabolites may be cleared.Das 6 · p.80 That paradox, pain that improves on walking further, is real and is worth knowing so it does not confuse you.
Rest pain, and what it means
B&L: "As disease progression occurs the claudication distance decreases and perfusion to the leg may be so severely" compromised that anaerobic respiration occurs even at rest.B&L 61 · p.998, p.999 S Das classifies the two pains of impaired limb circulation as exactly these: intermittent claudication and rest pain.Das 6 · p.80
SoRest pain is not a worse claudication; it is a different stage, and it means the limb is threatened.
Leriche syndrome
Aortoiliac disease with sexual impotence secondary to arterial insufficiency.B&L 61 · p.998 Ask about it: buttock and thigh claudication with impotence localises the disease to the aortoiliac segment, and almost no candidate asks.
Fontaine, Rutherford, Boyd, or chronic limb-threatening ischaemia? SRB 1 p.162–163 gives three classifications; B&L 61 has none of them and uses CLTI instead
SRB, Fontaine's classification of limb ischaemia
Stage 1: no clinical symptoms. Stage 2: intermittent claudication, split into 2a well compensated, can walk more than 200 metres, and 2b poorly compensated, can walk only less than 200 metres. Stage 3: rest pain. Stage 4: gangrene or ischaemic ulcer.SRB 1 · p.163
SRB, Rutherford's classification
Grade 0 asymptomatic; 1 mild claudication; 2 moderate; 3 severe; 4 ischaemic rest pain; 5 minor tissue loss; 6 major tissue loss.SRB 1 · p.163
SRB, Boyd's classification of claudication
Grade I: pain after walking a certain distance, the claudication distance, which may ease if he walks on as collaterals open and metabolites are washed out.SRB 1 · p.162
Bailey & Love 28e
Uses none of them: Fontaine, Rutherford and Boyd do not appear anywhere in chapter 61, nor does the phrase critical limb ischaemia. Instead it defines one category: "Patients with ischaemic rest pain with or without ulceration/gangrene (tissue loss) are termed to have chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI). These patients should be considered to have an imminently threatened leg and require urgent vascular assessment/revascularisation to prevent major amputation."B&L 61 · p.999 SRB still uses the older term, calling decompensated peripheral vascular disease "presently called critical limb ischaemia".SRB 1 · p.172
Live terminology difference with a real clinical point buried in it, and the right answer uses the Indian classification to stage and the British term to act.

Stage the patient with Fontaine, because that is what your examiner will ask for and it is the most useful shorthand: "This patient is Fontaine stage 2b, poorly compensated claudication at under 200 metres", or "Fontaine stage 3, rest pain".SRB 1 · p.163 Have Rutherford's grades ready as the alternative, because its separation of minor from major tissue loss is more useful than Fontaine's single stage 4.

Then use B&L's category to say what happens next, because that is where the urgency lives: "Rest pain with or without tissue loss is chronic limb-threatening ischaemia. The leg is imminently threatened and the patient needs urgent vascular assessment and revascularisation to prevent a major amputation."B&L 61 · p.999

Edition note. "Critical limb ischaemia" has been superseded by chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, which is both broader, because it includes tissue loss without rest pain, and more honest, because it names the thing at risk. SRB still uses the older phrase. Using CLTI and being able to say what it replaced is the cheapest way to show you have read the current edition.

3History, and why each question earns its place

How far can you walk before the pain starts, and where exactly is the pain? Why the distanceIt stages the disease: Fontaine 2a above 200 metres, 2b below.SRB 1 · p.163 And B&L's point that the distance varies only slightly from day to day is itself diagnostic: a distance that varies wildly is not claudication.B&L 61 · p.998
Why the siteIt localises the lesion. Calf claudication means superficial femoral disease; thigh and buttock claudication means aortoiliac disease, and with impotence that is Leriche syndrome.B&L 61 · p.998
Does it hurt on the very first step? Rules outOsteoarthritis. Claudication is not present on taking the first step; arthritic pain is.B&L 61 · p.998 One question, one differential excluded.
When the pain comes, does standing still relieve it, or do you have to sit down? Rules inClaudication is relieved by rest in both the standing and sitting positions, usually within 5 minutes.B&L 61 · p.998
Rules outNeurogenic claudication from a lumbar disc prolapse, spinal osteoarthritis or spinal stenosis, which are typically relieved only when resting in the sitting position for longer than 5 minutes.B&L 61 · p.998
SoThis is the single best discriminating question in the whole history, because spinal stenosis is the commonest mimic and it is relieved by flexing the spine, which is what sitting does. Ask it in exactly those words.
Do you get pain at night, in bed? What do you do about it? Rules inRest pain, and therefore chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, which means urgent referral rather than risk-factor advice.B&L 61 · p.999
The answer that confirms itThe patient hangs the leg out of bed or sleeps in a chair, because dependency improves perfusion. That history is worth more than any single sign.
Any ulcer, black patch or non-healing wound on the foot? Rules inTissue loss, which with or without rest pain defines CLTI.B&L 61 · p.999 Rutherford's classification separates minor from major tissue loss,SRB 1 · p.163 which is a more useful distinction than Fontaine's single stage 4.
And ask about infectionB&L: superadded infection often makes the gangrene wet,B&L 61 · p.999 which changes the urgency and the antibiotic decision.
Do you smoke? How much, and for how long? Any diabetes, hypertension or high cholesterol? WhyBecause this is a systemic disease of arteries, and because smoking is the one risk factor whose removal changes the natural history. In Buerger's disease it is not a risk factor but the disease's defining association, and the patient is typically a man between 20 and 40.Das 6 · p.80
And diabetes changes the patternSRB lists diabetes among the causes of limb ischaemia and groups it with the small peripheral vessel diseases.SRB 1 · p.163 B&L groups diabetes, Buerger's disease, Raynaud's disease and inadvertent intra-arterial injection together as obstruction in small peripheral vessels, as opposed to large-vessel atherosclerotic or embolic occlusion.B&L 61 · p.1014 That distinction decides whether revascularisation is even possible.
Any chest pain on exertion? Any transient weakness, speech difficulty or visual loss? Any abdominal pain after meals? WhyBecause of B&L's organising principle: the same disease in other arteries gives angina and myocardial infarction, transient ischaemic attacks and stroke, and abdominal pain and infarction.B&L 61 · p.997
SoThe patient with claudication is at risk of dying of a myocardial infarction rather than losing the leg, which is why the medical management matters as much as the operation. Saying that unprompted is worth a mark.
In a young woman with colour change of the fingers: does cold bring it on, and what colours? Rules inRaynaud's disease, which S Das places in young women, affecting the upper limb and usually bilateral.Das 6 · p.80
ContrastBuerger's: young men, lower limbs, bilateral. Atherosclerosis: elderly men, lower limbs, more often unilateral.Das 6 · p.80

4Examination, and what each finding settles

The trap B&L warns about, which catches people every year A chronically ischaemic limb is not necessarily cold. B&L: "Unlike an acutely ischaemic foot that is often cold, white, paralysed and insensate, a chronically ischaemic limb tends to equilibrate with the temperature of its surroundings and may feel quite warm under the bedclothes. Chronic ischaemia does not produce paralysis and sensation is usually intact."B&L 61 · p.999

And worse: "Patients with CLTI who have been waiting for a consultation with their leg in dependence may have a red swollen foot that may be mistaken for cellulitis by the unwary clinician." The way out is to elevate the limb: elevation "reveals the severity of the ischaemia with venous guttering and foot pallor that changes to a red or purple colour when the limb is allowed to hang down again (dependent rubor or the sunset foot sign)".B&L 61 · p.999

So a warm, red, swollen foot can be a critically ischaemic one. Elevate before you diagnose cellulitis.

Inspection

Colour, and the two dynamic signs
S Das: note the colour of the limb; the purplish red and congested limb may be very cold.Das 6 · p.83 Then do the dynamic tests, because static colour deceives: venous guttering and pallor on elevation, and dependent rubor, the sunset foot sign, on dependency.B&L 61 · p.999
Trophic changes
Loss of hair, shiny atrophic skin, thickened brittle nails, wasted muscle, and ulceration at the pressure points and the tips of the toes.
Gangrene, and whether it is wet or dry
B&L illustrates chronic limb-threatening ischaemia with dry gangrene,B&L 61 · p.999 and notes that superadded infection often makes the gangrene wet.B&L 61 · p.999 Wet gangrene is a surgical emergency; dry gangrene is usually not.
And look at the other limb, and the fingers
Because Buerger's and Raynaud's are usually bilateral,Das 6 · p.80 and because tender cord-like superficial veins point to Buerger's: SRB notes superficial migratory thrombophlebitis in 30%, with vasospasm, intimal panarteritis and obliteration, and nerve involvement from vasa nervorum block or spasm; in 10% the disease is bilateral.SRB 1 · p.172 And the distinguishing pathology: in Buerger's the arterial lumen is blocked but not thickened as in atherosclerosis.SRB 1 · p.172

Palpation

Temperature, compared and with the back of the hand
Run down both limbs and find the level at which the temperature changes, remembering B&L's warning that a chronically ischaemic limb may be warm.B&L 61 · p.999
Capillary refilling, done properly
S Das: "The tip of the nail or the pulp of a toe or a finger is pressed for a few se"conds and released.Das 6 · p.83 Delay indicates impaired perfusion. B&L measures the same thing as the capillary refill time.B&L 61 · p.999
All the pulses, every time
Femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis, and in the upper limb the brachial and radial. Then auscultate: SRB notes a systolic bruit may be heard over a stenosed artery, such as the subclavian, femoral, carotid, iliac or renal artery.SRB 1 · p.166
And examine the abdomen
For an aortic aneurysm, and because aortoiliac disease is where buttock claudication and impotence come from.B&L 61 · p.998

Buerger's postural test, and the angle

Lie the patient on his back and raise the leg slowly, watching the sole. NormalSRB: "In normal individuals, limb (plantar aspect of foot) remains pink even after raising above 90 degrees."SRB 1 · p.166
Rules inAn ischaemic limb, when elevated, shows marked pallor and empty veins. The angle at which pallor develops is Buerger's angle of vascular insufficiency.SRB 1 · p.166
The number to quoteIf this angle is less than 30 degrees, it indicates severe ischaemia.SRB 1 · p.166
Then let it hangThe second half of the test: on dependency the pallor is replaced by dependent rubor, the sunset foot sign.B&L 61 · p.999 So the two halves of the postural test are elevation for pallor and dependency for rubor, and together they are the most informative bedside manoeuvre in this case.
Note where it comes fromBuerger's angle and the postural test are SRB's and S Das's; they do not appear in Bailey & Love 28e, which describes the same physiology without the eponym and quantifies with the ankle-brachial index instead. The amber box immediately below sets that out, with the index thresholds.
Buerger's test and Buerger's angle: still the manoeuvre that guides you, or has the ankle-brachial index replaced it? A live disagreement about method, the same one as the tourniquet tests in varicose veins. S Das 6 p.82 and SRB 1 p.166 teach the test with a threshold angle; neither "Buerger's test" nor "vascular angle" appears anywhere in B&L 28e
S Das and SRB
Both teach it as a measured bedside test with a number attached. S Das: the angle "between the limb and the horizontal plane at which such pallor appears is called 'Buerger's angle' or the 'vascular angle'. A vascular angle of less than 30 degrees indicates severe ischaemia."Das 6 · p.82 SRB gives the same threshold and the normal: "in normal individuals, limb (plantar aspect of foot) remains pink even after raising above 90 degrees... if this angle is less than 30 degrees, it indicates severe ischaemia."SRB 1 · p.166 S Das also uses the test to characterise disease: in Buerger's disease, "Buerger's test will be positive."Das 6 · p.92
Bailey & Love 28e
The eponym is not attached to any test. Across every chapter extracted for these guides, neither Buerger's test, Buerger's postural test nor vascular angle appears; B&L's only uses of the name are Buerger's disease and the eponym footnote.B&L 61 · p.1014B&L 61 · p.1024 It describes the same physiology, elevation pallor and dependent rubor,B&L 61 · p.999 and then quantifies with a different instrument: "quantitative assessment can be carried out at the bedside by performing an ankle-brachial pressure index (ABI), which is the ratio of the systolic pressure at the ankle to that in the ipsilateral arm." Normal resting ABI 0.9 to 1.4; under 0.9 indicates a haemodynamically significant lesion; under 0.4 suggests chronic limb-threatening ischaemia; and "a drop in the resting ABI of over 20% after exercise is indicative of flow-limiting arterial disease."B&L 61 · p.1001 It adds a toe pressure: "an absolute pressure from the hallux of under 50 mmHg indicates severe ischaemia that is likely to prevent healing of ulceration."B&L 61 · p.1001
They are not disagreeing about the physiology, only about what you measure with
Both books rest on the same observation, that a critically perfused limb cannot maintain capillary filling against gravity. The difference is that Buerger's angle is an estimate by eye of a continuous variable, read off a limb being raised by hand, while the ABPI is a number from a sphygmomanometer and a hand-held Doppler that is reproducible and comparable between visits and between observers, with thresholds tied to outcomes. The same substitution has happened in varicose veins, where B&L 62 states that the tourniquet tests have been abandoned in favour of duplex. B&L also warns that the ABPI is "merely a guide and normal values may be present with intermittent claudication", so it is not infallible either.B&L 61 · p.1001
How to answer: do the test, name it, and then quantify. "On elevation the sole becomes pale and the veins empty; the angle at which pallor appears is Buerger's angle, and an angle of less than 30 degrees indicates severe ischaemia. On dependency the pallor is replaced by a dusky rubor. I would then measure the ankle-brachial pressure index with a hand-held Doppler: normal is 0.9 to 1.4, below 0.9 indicates a significant arterial lesion, and below 0.4 suggests chronic limb-threatening ischaemia. If the resting index is normal but the history is typical, I would repeat it after exercise, where a fall of more than 20% indicates flow-limiting disease." That answer is complete in either room, and it shows you know the postural test is an estimate and the index is a measurement. In an Indian clinical examination you will be asked to perform Buerger's test, so practise it and know the 30-degree figure; do not reach for the Doppler instead of the manoeuvre.
And one more test with a name, for the upper limb Adson's test, the scalene manoeuvre, for thoracic outlet obstruction: with the patient sitting, feel the radial pulse and ask the patient to take a deep breath and turn the head, which tightens the scalenes "adequately and so pulse will disappear, unmasking the arterial obstruction".SRB 1 · p.166 Worth knowing if the case is an upper limb one.

5Investigations, and why each one

Ankle-brachial pressure index, with all four numbers The ABI is the ratio of the systolic pressure at the ankle to that in the arm,B&L 61 · p.1001 measured with a Doppler probe over the dorsalis pedis.B&L 61 · p.1001

A value below 0.4 suggests chronic limb-threatening ischaemia.
Values above 1.4 are artificially high, caused by media sclerosis and calcification of the arterial wall making the vessel incompressible, which is the pattern seen in diabetes and renal failure.
A drop of more than 20% in the resting ABI after exercise to the onset of pain is indicative of flow-limiting arterial disease.
And the caveat: "values are merely a guide and normal values may be present with intermittent claudication."B&L 61 · p.1001

That last clause is the one to say aloud. A normal resting ABI does not exclude claudication, which is exactly why you exercise the patient and repeat it.
Hand-held Doppler, and what the waveform tells you
B&L: "a normal artery has a triphasic signal whereas a diseased arter"y does not.B&L 61 · p.1001 The progression is triphasic to biphasic to monophasic as disease advances, and the quality of the signal is information in itself, separate from the pressure.
Duplex ultrasound
The non-invasive mapping study, which localises and grades stenoses and assesses the vessels for reconstruction.
Cross-sectional angiography
CT or MR angiography to plan intervention, and catheter angiography where intervention will be done at the same sitting. B&L illustrates aortoiliac bifurcation disease with an occluded right common iliac artery and a critically stenosed left one.B&L 61 · p.998
And the rest of the patient
Because this is systemic arterial disease: blood glucose and HbA1c, lipid profile, renal function, full blood count, ECG, and echocardiography where indicated. SRB's general vascular workup lists Doppler study, radiographs of the part, ECG and echocardiography.SRB 1 · p.211

6Treatment, and how urgently

First, decide which category the patient is in
Claudication is a risk-factor and exercise problem with selective intervention. Rest pain or tissue loss is chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, and B&L's instruction is unambiguous: "These patients should be considered to have an imminently threatened leg and require urgent vascular assessment/revascularisation to prevent major amputation."B&L 61 · p.999 Say the word urgent, and say to prevent major amputation, because that is what the urgency is for.
For claudication: the things that change the natural history
Stop smoking, which is the single most effective measure. Supervised exercise to the onset of pain, which works by the mechanism SRB describes, increased flow and opening of collaterals.SRB 1 · p.162 Antiplatelet therapy, a statin, and control of diabetes and hypertension, because the patient's greatest risk is the coronary and cerebral circulations, not the leg.B&L 61 · p.997 Foot care, particularly in diabetes.
Revascularisation: endovascular or open
Angioplasty with or without stenting for shorter, more proximal lesions; bypass grafting for long occlusions and for distal disease; and the choice depends on the lesion, the quality of the inflow and outflow vessels, the availability of vein for a graft, and the patient's fitness.
When revascularisation is not possible
This is where the small-vessel distinction matters. B&L groups obstruction in small peripheral vessels, that is diabetes, Buerger's disease, Raynaud's disease and inadvertent intra-arterial injection, separately from large-vessel atherosclerotic or embolic occlusion,B&L 61 · p.1014 because there is no target vessel to bypass. Management is then medical, with pain control, foot care, treatment of infection, and amputation of non-viable tissue.
Amputation, and the levels to know
SRB's named amputations include Boyd's amputation: the anterior part of the calcaneum is excised just distal to the peroneal tuberosity and a calcaneotibial arthrodesis created, done to prevent posterior migration of the heel pad.SRB 1 · p.310 Note that Boyd's name attaches both to a classification of claudication and to an amputation, which is the same hazard as Kocher and Crile: ask which Boyd the examiner means.
Lumbar sympathectomy: a treatment for the limb you cannot revascularise, or obsolete? A live disagreement, and B&L states it in one word. SRB 1 p.181 gives five indications; B&L 61 p.1024 says it "has also become obsolete"
SRB
Gives it a full entry with indications, principle and anatomy. The indications are "peripheral vascular disease like TAO; to promote healing of cutaneous ulcers; to change level of amputation and to make flaps to heal better after amputation; causalgia of lower limb; hyperhidrosis of lower limb."SRB 1 · p.181 The principle: "it increases the cutaneous blood supply thereby promoting healing of ulcer and skin flaps in amputation. It is a preganglionic sympathectomy. Ganglion L2 and L3 supplies legs below knee level." And it is honest about the magnitude of the effect: "it increases the blood flow for 2 to 4 weeks by abolishing constriction of arterioles and precapillary sphincters... it produces transient small increase in distal perfusion."SRB 1 · p.181 SRB uses it elsewhere for a Martorell's ulcer and for a tuberculous ulcer of the leg.SRB 1 · p.23SRB 1 · p.27
Bailey & Love 28e
One short section, and all three of its sentences are negative. "Endoscopic transthoracic sympathectomy is now reserved as a minimally invasive treatment of palmar and axillary hyperhidrosis. Open cervical sympathectomy was used in the past as a treatment of vasospastic disorders but was usually unsuccessful. Lumbar sympathectomy was used to treat lower limb ischaemia in the past but has also become obsolete."B&L 61 · p.1024 It is equally dismissive for the vasospastic conditions: in Raynaud's disease, "sympathectomy has been used in the past but it is either ineffective or its effects are short-lived", and in Raynaud's syndrome secondary to collagen disease, "sympathectomy yields disappointing results and is not recommended."B&L 61 · p.1024 So the only indication surviving in B&L is hyperhidrosis, which is also on SRB's list.
SRB half concedes the point itself
Read SRB's own two statements together and the gap narrows. Its indication is "peripheral vascular disease like TAO", that is Buerger's disease, and elsewhere SRB says plainly that "lumbar sympathectomy and omentoplasty are not much useful in atherosclerotic limb."SRB 1 · p.170 So SRB is not recommending it for the common atherosclerotic patient either. And its own stated effect, a transient small increase in perfusion lasting two to four weeks, is precisely the "effects are short-lived" that B&L objects to. The two books describe the same weak physiological effect and draw different conclusions about whether a weak effect is worth an operation.
Why it stopped being done
Sympathectomy dilates cutaneous arterioles, not the vessels supplying muscle, so it was never going to relieve claudication, which is a muscle symptom; the cutaneous effect is transient because denervated vessels become supersensitive to circulating catecholamines; and in a diabetic or long-standing ischaemic limb much of the sympathetic tone is already lost, so there is little left to interrupt. Meanwhile endovascular technique made revascularisation possible in vessels once considered untreatable, which removed most of the group sympathectomy was for.
How to answer: know the operation, give the indications SRB gives, and then say where it stands. If asked what a lumbar sympathectomy is: "a preganglionic sympathectomy removing the L2 and L3 ganglia, which supply the leg below the knee, to abolish sympathetic vasoconstriction of cutaneous arterioles and precapillary sphincters." If asked when you would do it: "its described indications are thromboangiitis obliterans, where there is no target vessel to bypass; promoting healing of a cutaneous ulcer; and improving the healing of skin flaps or lowering the level of an amputation; also causalgia and hyperhidrosis. But the effect on perfusion is small and lasts only two to four weeks, SRB itself says it is not much use in an atherosclerotic limb, and Bailey and Love 28e calls lumbar sympathectomy for lower limb ischaemia obsolete, keeping sympathectomy only for palmar and axillary hyperhidrosis. So I would not offer it for claudication, and in an unreconstructable limb I would manage medically, with pain control, foot care and treatment of infection, and amputate non-viable tissue."

For an Indian written paper, give SRB's indications, the principle and the L2 to L3 anatomy, because that is the marked answer, and add the obsolescence in a closing sentence. The one thing not to do is offer it as your management plan for an atherosclerotic limb.
Buerger's disease, as its own answer B&L: thromboangiitis obliterans "is characterised by occlusive disease of small and medium-sized" vessels,B&L 61 · p.1024 grouped under arteritis and vasospastic conditions. SRB's pathology: vasospasm, then intimal panarteritis, then obliteration, with tender cord-like veins and superficial migratory thrombophlebitis in 30%, and nerve involvement from vasa nervorum block or spasm; the arterial lumen is blocked but not thickened as in atherosclerosis.SRB 1 · p.172 S Das: men between 20 and 40, lower limbs, usually bilateral.Das 6 · p.80

So the exam answer is: a young male smoker with bilateral lower limb ischaemia, migratory thrombophlebitis, and distal small-vessel disease with a normal-calibre proximal artery. Treatment is absolute smoking cessation; there is usually no vessel to bypass.

7Rapid-fire viva

Define intermittent claudication so as to exclude the mimics.

Cramp-like pain in the muscles due to anaerobic muscle metabolism, which is brought on by walking, is not present on taking the first step, unlike osteoarthritis, and is relieved by rest in both the standing and sitting positions usually within 5 minutes, unlike nerve compression from a lumbar disc, spinal osteoarthritis or spinal stenosis, which are relieved only by sitting for longer than 5 minutes. The claudication distance varies only slightly from day to day.B&L 61 · p.998 The single best discriminating question is whether standing still relieves it: if the patient has to sit down, think spinal stenosis.

Why does the pain sometimes ease if the patient keeps walking?

Because increased blood flow in the muscle and the opening of collaterals wash out the metabolites that were causing the pain.SRB 1 · p.162Das 6 · p.80 It is a real phenomenon and should not confuse you into doubting the diagnosis. It is also the physiological basis of supervised exercise therapy.

Stage this patient.

Fontaine: stage 1 no symptoms; stage 2 claudication, 2a well compensated walking over 200 metres and 2b poorly compensated under 200 metres; stage 3 rest pain; stage 4 gangrene or ischaemic ulcer.SRB 1 · p.163 Rutherford grades 0 to 6, with the advantage of separating minor from major tissue loss. And then say what matters: rest pain with or without tissue loss is chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, an imminently threatened leg needing urgent vascular assessment and revascularisation to prevent major amputation.B&L 61 · p.999

Is it critical limb ischaemia or chronic limb-threatening ischaemia?

Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, CLTI, is the current term, and it is what Bailey and Love 28e uses; the phrase critical limb ischaemia does not appear in its arterial chapter.B&L 61 · p.999 SRB still uses the older term, describing decompensated peripheral vascular disease as "presently called critical limb ischaemia".SRB 1 · p.172 CLTI is broader, because it includes tissue loss with or without rest pain, and it names what is at stake. Fontaine, Rutherford and Boyd do not appear in Bailey and Love 28e at all.

Describe Buerger's postural test.

With the patient supine, the leg is raised slowly while watching the plantar surface. Normally the sole stays pink even beyond 90 degrees. An ischaemic limb shows marked pallor with empty veins, and the angle at which pallor appears is Buerger's angle of vascular insufficiency; an angle under 30 degrees indicates severe ischaemia.SRB 1 · p.166 Then let the limb hang: pallor is replaced by dependent rubor, the sunset foot sign.B&L 61 · p.999 Note that the eponym and the angle are SRB's; Bailey and Love describes the same physiology without them.

A warm, red, swollen foot in an elderly diabetic. Cellulitis?

Not necessarily, and this is the trap. A chronically ischaemic limb equilibrates with its surroundings and may feel quite warm under the bedclothes, and chronic ischaemia produces neither paralysis nor loss of sensation. Patients with CLTI who have been sitting with the leg dependent may have a red swollen foot that is mistaken for cellulitis by the unwary clinician. Elevate the limb: elevation reveals venous guttering and pallor, which changes back to red or purple on dependency, the dependent rubor or sunset foot sign.B&L 61 · p.999

How do you measure and interpret the ABI?

The ratio of systolic pressure at the ankle to that in the arm, measured with a Doppler probe over the dorsalis pedis. Below 0.4 suggests chronic limb-threatening ischaemia. Above 1.4 is artificially high, from media sclerosis and calcification making the vessel incompressible, as in diabetes and renal failure. A fall of more than 20% in the resting index after exercise to the onset of pain indicates flow-limiting disease. And the caveat that matters: values are merely a guide, and normal values may be present with intermittent claudication.B&L 61 · p.1001 So a normal resting ABI does not exclude claudication, which is why you exercise the patient.

What does the Doppler waveform tell you?

A normal artery gives a triphasic signal; a diseased artery does not.B&L 61 · p.1001 The signal degrades from triphasic to biphasic to monophasic as disease advances, and that is information independent of the pressure measurement.

Who gets which arteriopathy?

Atherosclerosis: old age, men more than women, lower limbs, more often unilateral. Buerger's disease: men between 20 and 40, lower limbs, usually bilateral. Raynaud's disease: young women, upper limbs, usually bilateral. Diabetic arteriopathy: middle age.Das 6 · p.80 SRB's full causal list adds embolism, Takayasu's disease, scleroderma and physical agents including trauma, tourniquet and radiation injury.SRB 1 · p.163

Describe Buerger's disease.

Thromboangiitis obliterans: occlusive disease of small and medium-sized vessels,B&L 61 · p.1024 in young male smokers between 20 and 40, affecting the lower limbs and usually bilateral.Das 6 · p.80 The sequence is vasospasm, intimal panarteritis, then obliteration; there is superficial migratory thrombophlebitis with tender cord-like veins in 30%, and nerve involvement from blockage or spasm of the vasa nervorum. Crucially the arterial lumen is blocked but not thickened as in atherosclerosis.SRB 1 · p.172 Treatment is absolute smoking cessation; because the disease is distal and small-vessel, there is usually no target for bypass.

Why does a patient with claudication need a statin and an antiplatelet?

Because peripheral arterial disease is a marker of systemic atherosclerosis, and the same process in other arteries produces angina and myocardial infarction, transient ischaemic attacks and stroke, and mesenteric ischaemia with abdominal pain and infarction.B&L 61 · p.997 The patient's greatest risk is cardiovascular death rather than limb loss, so risk-factor modification, above all stopping smoking, is as important as any intervention on the leg.

Buttock and thigh claudication with impotence. Where is the lesion?

Aortoiliac: Leriche syndrome, aortoiliac occlusive disease with sexual impotence secondary to arterial insufficiency.B&L 61 · p.998 Calf claudication by contrast points to superficial femoral disease. Examine the abdomen for an aneurysm and feel the femoral pulses.

Why can revascularisation be impossible?

Because there is no target vessel. Bailey and Love separates obstruction of large vessels, from atherosclerosis or embolism, from obstruction of small peripheral vessels in diabetes, Buerger's disease, Raynaud's disease and inadvertent intra-arterial injection.B&L 61 · p.1014 Small-vessel disease leaves nothing to bypass, so management becomes medical, with pain control, foot care, treatment of infection and amputation of non-viable tissue.

Wet or dry gangrene: does it matter?

Yes. Bailey and Love illustrates chronic limb-threatening ischaemia with dry gangrene, and notes that superadded infection often makes the gangrene wet.B&L 61 · p.999 Wet gangrene is an emergency needing antibiotics and urgent surgical debridement or amputation; dry gangrene can often be allowed to demarcate while the limb is revascularised.

What is Adson's test?

The scalene manoeuvre, for thoracic outlet obstruction. The patient sits, the radial pulse is felt, and the patient takes a deep breath and turns the head, which tightens the scalene muscles so the pulse disappears, unmasking the arterial obstruction.SRB 1 · p.166

What is Boyd's amputation, and the trap in the name?

The anterior part of the calcaneum is excised just distal to the peroneal tuberosity and a calcaneotibial arthrodesis created, to prevent posterior migration of the heel pad.SRB 1 · p.310 The trap is that Boyd's name also attaches to a classification of claudication in the same book,SRB 1 · p.162 and to a named calf perforating vein,SRB 1 · p.215 so ask which Boyd the examiner means before you answer, exactly as with Kocher and Crile in the thyroid case.

Why is a chronic complete occlusion sometimes less symptomatic than a partial one?

Because the severity of symptoms depends on the size of the vessel and on whether the occlusion happened suddenly in a previously normal artery or gradually with progressive narrowing over time; in chronic stenosis symptoms may be abated despite significant steno-occlusive disease, because collaterals have had time to develop.B&L 61 · p.997

Where would you auscultate, and for what?

A systolic bruit may be heard over a stenosed artery: subclavian, femoral, carotid, iliac or renal.SRB 1 · p.166 It indicates turbulent flow across a stenosis, and a carotid bruit in a claudicant is a reminder that this is a systemic disease.
