Long case 05 · General Surgery

Obstructive Jaundice

The case that turns on one question: stone or tumour. Everything you ask, feel and order exists to answer it, and the single sign the examiner will press you on, Courvoisier's law, is stated differently in all three books and contradicted by Bailey & Love between two of its own chapters.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1The case in one breath

Definition, in the words to use
SRB: jaundice developing due to biliary obstruction, partial or complete or intermittent, causing a conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia.SRB 12 · p.646 It is often called surgical jaundice, and occurs from intrahepatic or extrahepatic biliary outflow obstruction, leading to cholestasis, which means impaired bile flow and impaired bile formation.SRB 12 · p.647
The numbers to quote
Normal serum bilirubin 0.2 to 0.8 mg%. Scleral icterus becomes visible when serum bilirubin exceeds 2.5 mg%.SRB 12 · p.646 And the ceiling, which almost nobody knows: in obstructive jaundice the serum bilirubin will not go beyond about 40 mg%, because at that point the raised intrabiliary pressure, normally 12 to 22 cm of water, reaches about 25 cm and blocks further secretion.SRB 14 · p.699
The two questions the case is really asking
Is this obstruction? and is it stone or tumour? The answer to the second decides everything: a stone is removed endoscopically and the gallbladder taken out; a tumour is staged and either resected or palliated.
The mechanism, and why it explains every complication SRB's chain, which is the clearest account in the three books.SRB 12 · p.647

Bile acid secretion into the gut is impaired → defective absorption of fat and the fat-soluble vitamins → poor synthesis of prothrombin and impaired conversion of prothrombin to thrombin → widened PT and raised INR. The vitamin K-dependent clotting factors are II, VII, IX and X; PT measures the extrinsic pathway, partial thromboplastin time the intrinsic.

And each vitamin has its own deficiency syndrome: A, visual problems and thick skin; D, osteomalacia; E, peripheral neuropathy, cerebellar ataxia and posterior column dysfunction; K, bleeding. A trap worth knowing: cholestyramine and colestipol, given for the itching, bind bile salts and can make these deficiencies worse.SRB 12 · p.647
Why the stools are pale and the urine dark
SRB: the lack of bilirubin in the intestinal tract is responsible for the pale stools in biliary obstruction.SRB 12 · p.647 Conjugated bilirubin is water soluble, so it spills into the urine instead.
Why the patient itches, which is honestly uncertain
SRB is candid: "the cause of itching associated with biliary obstruction is not clear; may be related to the accumulation of bile acids in the skin; or may be related to the release of endogenous opioids".SRB 12 · p.647 Saying that the mechanism is unsettled, and naming both hypotheses, is a better answer than confidently asserting bile salts.
What unrelieved obstruction does to the liver
An enlarged green bile-stained liver, which SRB calls hydrohepatosis, with dilated intrahepatic biliary radicles. Once intraductal pressure rises, bile secretion falls and "white bile" forms in the common duct. Severe biliary obstruction causes cell damage usually within 1 month and, if unrelieved, may lead to secondary biliary cirrhosis.SRB 12 · p.647 That one-month figure is the answer to "how urgently must this be relieved?"
And the systemic consequences to name
Altered coagulation profile; hepatorenal syndrome and renal failure; sepsis; plus steatorrhoea with bulky fatty faeces, and retention of bile salts and pigments in blood and body fluids.SRB 12 · p.647

SRB's anatomical version, which is better for a viva
Extrahepatic obstruction is luminal (stones, infestations: ascariasis, Clonorchis sinensis, schistosomiasis), mural (growth, stricture from inflammation, surgery or radiotherapy, primary sclerosing cholangitis), or external compression (pancreatitis, pancreatic tumour, nodal mass). Intrahepatic cholestasis occurs at the level of the hepatocyte or the biliary canalicular membrane: viral hepatitis, drug-induced cholestasis such as thiazides and chlorpromazine, and biliary cirrhosis.SRB 12 · p.647
Luminal, mural, extrinsicSay it in that order for any obstruction anywhere and you will never leave a cause out.
Which cause is commonest
SRB: benign conditions, of which biliary tree stones are the most common cause.SRB 12 · p.647 But see the Courvoisier disagreement below for how to use that at the bedside.
And why conjugated bilirubin predominates even in liver disease
In hepatocellular disease all three steps of bilirubin metabolism are affected, uptake, conjugation and excretion, but excretion is the rate-limiting step and is impaired to the greatest extent, so conjugated bilirubin still predominates in the serum.SRB 12 · p.647 This is why a conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia does not by itself prove the obstruction is surgical.
Only one book has this: Benjamin's classification of biliary obstruction SRB 12 p.648 gives it in four types; B&L 71 has no such classification
SRB, Benjamin's 1983 classification
Type 1, complete obstruction: pancreatic tumours, cholangiocarcinoma, iatrogenic CBD ligation, primary or secondary liver tumours. Type 2, intermittent obstruction: choledocholithiasis, periampullary tumour, choledochal cyst, bile duct papilloma, haemobilia, duodenal diverticula. Type 3, chronic incomplete obstruction: bile duct stricture, congenital, traumatic, post-radiotherapy, chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis. Type 4, segmental obstruction: traumatic, sclerosing cholangitis, cholangiocarcinoma, intrahepatic biliary stones, that is hepatolithiasis.SRB 12 · p.648
Bailey & Love 28e
Carries no equivalent classification of biliary obstruction. The only Benjamin in its biliary chapter is John Benjamin Murphy's middle name, in the eponym footnotes.B&L 71 · p.1235
Not a disagreement, an asymmetry, and the practical point is to know which book it comes from before you cite it. Benjamin's classification is an Indian examination favourite and is worth reproducing if asked to classify biliary obstruction, but do not attribute it to Bailey & Love, and do not be surprised if an examiner trained on the British text does not recognise it. Its real value is clinical rather than taxonomic: it separates intermittent obstruction, where a stone or a periampullary tumour lets bile through between episodes, from complete obstruction, which is the pattern of pancreatic cancer. That distinction is what the history question "has the jaundice ever got better on its own?" is testing, so use Benjamin's types as a reason to ask it.

3History, and why each question earns its place

When did you turn yellow? Has it ever got better on its own, or has it only deepened? DistinguishesThe single most discriminating question in the case. Progressive, unremitting, deepening jaundice is the pattern of a neoplasm, Benjamin type 1. Fluctuating or intermittent jaundice is the pattern of a stone or a periampullary tumour, Benjamin type 2.SRB 12 · p.648
Why a periampullary tumour fluctuatesBecause it sloughs and bleeds, so the obstruction is relieved intermittently. A patient with waxing and waning jaundice and anaemia has a periampullary carcinoma until proved otherwise.
Is there pain? Where, and did it come before or after the jaundice? Rules inPainless progressive jaundice is the classical presentation of carcinoma of the head of the pancreas. B&L: jaundice "draws attention to ampullary and pancreatic head tumours. It is characteristically painless jaundice but may be associated with nausea and epigastric discomfort." Pruritus and dark urine accompany it.B&L 72 · p.1282
Rules in something worseBack pain is a worrying symptom, raising the possibility of retroperitoneal infiltration.B&L 72 · p.1283 S Das describes the same pain for the body and tail: it passes through to the back, is aggravated on lying down and relieved on sitting up, so the patient spends the night sitting with his arms folded across his chest.Das 34 · p.511 That posture is a sign you can see from the door.
Rules inPain with jaundice, colicky, in the right hypochondrium, points to a stone, and if it followed a cholecystectomy, SRB's rule: "If recurrence of biliary colic after cholecystectomy occurs, one should think of possible choledocholithiasis."SRB 12 · p.647
Any fever with chills and rigors? Rules inCholangitis, and this converts the case into an emergency. B&L: symptomatic patients with cholangitis have bouts of pain, jaundice and fever, Charcot's triad.B&L 71 · p.1253
Mechanism, worth recitingSRB's chain: bile flow stasis → colonisation and multiplication of organisms → increased intraductal pressure → reflux of biliary contents → bacteraemia, septicaemia, septic shock.SRB 12 · p.647 Normally bile is sterile; symptomatic gallstone disease shows positive cultures, commonest E. coli and Klebsiella.SRB 12 · p.625
And where it most often happensSRB: acute cholangitis is most commonly seen in stricture, most often at the level of the CBD.SRB 12 · p.647
What colour are your stools and your urine? Do the stools float or smell offensive? Rules inPale stools and dark urine confirm cholestasis. Add steatorrhoea: SRB lists more fatty stool among the clinical features, from absence of bile impairing fat absorption.SRB 12 · p.647, p.648
DistinguishesStools that are intermittently pale fit an intermittent obstruction. Stools that are persistently clay-coloured fit a complete, that is neoplastic, obstruction.
One more to ask aboutSilver stools: pale from obstruction plus blood from a bleeding tumour, classically a periampullary carcinoma.
Do you itch? Where worst? Any bleeding, bruising or bleeding gums? Rules inSRB: pruritus, more on the back and forearms.SRB 12 · p.648 It is often what brings the patient in, and B&L treats distressing pruritus in a deeply jaundiced patient as an indication to stent before surgery.B&L 72 · p.1283
Rules inBleeding tendency from vitamin K malabsorption, which is the thing that will kill him on the operating table if you miss it. Persistent cholestasis also deposits cholesterol in skin as cutaneous xanthomatosis, occasionally in bones and peripheral nerves.SRB 12 · p.647
SoCheck and correct the prothrombin time before any intervention. B&L: "The prothrombin time should be checked, and clotting abnormalities should be corrected with vitamin K or fresh-frozen plasma prior to ERCP."B&L 72 · p.1283 Before ERCP, not just before surgery.
Have you lost weight? How much, over how long? Rules inMalignancy. On examination B&L expects jaundice, weight loss, a palpable liver and a palpable gallbladder.B&L 72 · p.1283
ButWeight loss also occurs with chronic pancreatitis and with prolonged cholestasis and steatorrhoea, so it is suggestive, not diagnostic. The combination that matters is painless progressive jaundice plus weight loss plus a palpable gallbladder.
Any previous biliary or gastric surgery? Any blood transfusion, injections, alcohol or hepatotoxic drugs? Any family history of liver disease? WhyTo separate surgical from medical jaundice before you commit to the surgical pathway. Intrahepatic cholestasis from viral hepatitis or drugs such as thiazides and chlorpromazine produces the same conjugated picture and must not be operated on.SRB 12 · p.647
Rules inA post-cholecystectomy patient with jaundice has an iatrogenic stricture or a retained stone until proved otherwise; SRB lists iatrogenic CBD ligation as Benjamin type 1 and post-surgical stricture as type 3.SRB 12 · p.648 And B&L lists a biliary-enteric anastomosis among the risk factors for cholangiocarcinoma.B&L 71 · p.1256
Where do you live? Any history of worms passed in stool or vomit? Rules inCauses the British text barely covers and an Indian examiner expects. SRB's luminal causes include ascariasis, Clonorchis sinensis and schistosomiasis,SRB 12 · p.647 and its figure of the causes of obstructive jaundice shows a roundworm in the common bile duct.SRB 12 · p.647
And the malignant sequelB&L's risk factors for cholangiocarcinoma include the parasitic infections Opisthorchis viverrini and C. sinensis, and Oriental cholangiohepatitis among the chronic inflammatory conditions.B&L 71 · p.1256 S Das lists Oriental cholangiohepatitis as an exception to Courvoisier's law.Das 34 · p.491 So the same disease appears in all three books, under three headings.

4Examination, and what each finding settles

Courvoisier's law: what did he actually say, and what are the exceptions? Three different statements, two different exception lists, and B&L 28e contradicting itself between ch.71 and ch.72
What Courvoisier wrote in 1890, quoted by SRB
"...the gallbladder is rarely observed; the organ has already undergone contraction; with obstruction from other causes, dilatation is to be expected."SRB 12 · p.631 Note what this is: an observation about stone versus not-stone, framed around the state of the gallbladder wall. It says nothing about cancer.
SRB's statement of the law, and its exceptions
"In a patient with jaundice, if there is a palpable gallbladder, it is not due to stones." The reasoning: in stone disease the gallbladder is chronically inflamed, thickened, fibrotic, contracted and non-distensible; in malignancy such as carcinoma of the head of the pancreas or a periampullary carcinoma it is distended and palpable to the right of the rectus in the right hypochondrium, non-tender, globular, smooth, soft, dull, moving with respiration and with horizontal mobility. Exceptions: double impacted stone, one in the CBD and one in the cystic duct, with mucocele; a large stone in Hartmann's pouch; empyema of the gallbladder with a CBD stone.SRB 12 · p.646
S Das's statement, and a different exception list
In jaundice due to a calculus in the common bile duct the gallbladder is usually not distended owing to previous inflammatory fibrosis, whereas in obstruction due to growth, such as carcinoma of the head of the pancreas, the gallbladder becomes distended in an attempt to reduce the pressure in the biliary system.Das 34 · p.492 Exceptions: (i) double impaction of stones, one in the cystic duct and one in the CBD; (ii) Oriental cholangiohepatitis; (iii) a pancreatic calculus obstructing the ampulla of Vater; (iv) mucocele of the gallbladder due to a stone in the cystic duct.Das 34 · p.491 Only the double impaction appears on both lists.
Bailey & Love 28e, chapter 71
States the law in the negative form: "a palpable gallbladder in the presence of jaundice is unlikely to be due to gallstones (Courvoisier) and usually results from a distal common duct obstruction secondary to periampullary malignancy".B&L 71 · p.1244 It gives no exceptions at all, and its eponym footnote adds a historical correction: Courvoisier "made his observation in 1890. No mention was made of either gallbladder tenderness or malignancy."B&L 71 · p.1244
Bailey & Love 28e, chapter 72, which says the opposite about the history
"Courvoisier first drew attention to the association of an enlarged gallbladder and a pancreatic tumour in 1890, when he noted that, when the common duct is obstructed by a stone, distension of the gallbladder (which is likely to be chronically inflamed) is rare; when the duct is obstructed in some other way, such as a neoplasm, distension of the normal gallbladder is common."B&L 72 · p.1283 So chapter 71 says he made no mention of malignancy and chapter 72 says he first drew attention to the association with a pancreatic tumour and quotes him using the word neoplasm.
Answer the law in the form that is actually defensible, which is the original one, and keep the exceptions attached to whichever book you name.

Say: "Courvoisier's law states that in a jaundiced patient a palpably distended gallbladder is unlikely to be due to a stone, because a stone-bearing gallbladder is chronically inflamed, fibrotic and cannot distend. The usual cause is therefore an obstruction below the cystic duct in a previously normal gallbladder, most often a periampullary or pancreatic head carcinoma." That version is true to the 1890 observation, gives the mechanism, and reaches the clinically useful conclusion without over-claiming.

On exceptions, give the one both Indian books agree on first: double impaction, a stone in the cystic duct and another in the CBD, producing a mucocele with jaundice. Then add the rest as "S Das also lists" and "SRB also lists", because the lists genuinely differ. If you must memorise one combined list: double impaction with mucocele; a large stone in Hartmann's pouch; empyema of the gallbladder with a CBD stone; Oriental cholangiohepatitis; and a pancreatic calculus obstructing the ampulla.SRB 12 · p.646Das 34 · p.491

On the history, if an examiner says Courvoisier never mentioned malignancy, he is quoting Bailey & Love chapter 71; if another says Courvoisier first described the association with pancreatic tumour, he is quoting chapter 72 of the same book. Both are in print. The safe reply is that Courvoisier's original observation contrasted stone with non-stone obstruction, and the association with malignancy is the inference the profession drew from it.

General examination

Depth and character of the jaundice
Look in daylight. Scleral icterus appears above 2.5 mg%; a deep greenish-yellow tinge suggests long-standing obstruction rather than haemolysis.SRB 12 · p.646
Scratch marks
Over the back and forearms, where SRB says the pruritus is worst.SRB 12 · p.648 Free evidence of chronicity, and a sign you can report before touching the abdomen.
Bruising, petechiae, bleeding gums
The clinical face of the prolonged prothrombin time.
Xanthomas
Cutaneous cholesterol deposits in persistent cholestasis.SRB 12 · p.647
Nutritional state, and hydration
SRB makes dehydration a specific preoperative concern: "Dehydration is common in obstructive jaundice", and the liver's glycogen reserve will be inadequate and needs replenishing with preoperative glucose, orally or intravenously.SRB 14 · p.698
Signs of chronic liver disease, to exclude a medical cause
Spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, caput medusae, splenomegaly, asterixis. Their presence shifts you towards hepatocellular disease or cirrhosis, which is not a surgical jaundice.

Abdominal examination, in the order that answers the question

1. The gallbladder
Feel specifically for it, below the right costal margin lateral to the rectus edge. SRB's description of the malignant gallbladder is the one to reproduce: non-tender, globular, smooth, soft, dull, moving with respiration, with horizontal mobility.SRB 12 · p.646
And the distinction from everything elseHorizontal mobility and movement with respiration are what separate a distended gallbladder from a renal or a hepatic mass.
2. The liver
Enlarged and smooth in obstruction, from hydrohepatosis and bile stasis.SRB 12 · p.647 Hard and nodular means secondaries. S Das, on carcinoma head of pancreas: "the liver may be enlarged".Das 34 · p.511
3. A mass, and the honest caveat about it
S Das, on carcinoma of the head of the pancreas: "The tumour is seldom palpable."Das 34 · p.511 So the absence of a palpable mass tells you nothing, and candidates who report "no mass, therefore benign" are making an error the book warns against.
4. Ascites
S Das: in carcinoma of the head of the pancreas, ascites may be present only in the late cases.Das 34 · p.511 Elicit shifting dullness starting in the midline and percussing to the flank, keeping the finger in place while the patient turns, and waiting a minute for the fluid to move.Das 34 · p.489
5. The signs of incurability, which B&L tells you to seek deliberately
"Other signs of intra-abdominal malignancy should be looked for with care, such as a palpable mass, ascites, supraclavicular nodes and tumour deposits in the pelvis; when present, they indicate a grim prognosis."B&L 72 · p.1283 So palpate the left supraclavicular fossa and do a rectal examination. Both are free and both change the plan from resection to palliation.
6. Migrating thrombophlebitis
S Das: thrombophlebitis migrans may be an indication of the presence of pancreatic carcinoma.Das 34 · p.511 The same Trousseau's sign that appears in gastric cancer, and in fact Trousseau's own case proved to be pancreatic at post-mortem.
Charcot's triad, Reynolds' pentad, and what B&L actually uses SRB 12 p.643 and p.648 give both eponyms; B&L 71 p.1253 has Charcot only and no Reynolds anywhere
Bailey & Love 28e
Gives Charcot's triad: symptomatic patients with cholangitis have bouts of pain, jaundice and fever. Signs are febrile and icteric, with tenderness elicitable in the epigastrium.B&L 71 · p.1253 Reynolds' pentad does not appear anywhere in the chapter. Where it does use a formal severity framework for biliary sepsis it is the Tokyo Consensus Guidelines, which it prints as diagnostic criteria for acute cholecystitis.B&L 71 · p.1244
SRB
Carries both. It lists Charcot's triad and Reynolds' pentad as presentations of cholangitis,SRB 12 · p.648 and defines the pentad as belonging to acute obstructive cholangitis, that is suppurative cholangitis, which it puts at 5%, on a chain of obstruction → stasis → jaundice, pain, infection, bacteraemia and fever.SRB 12 · p.643
Live asymmetry, and the Indian eponym is the more useful one at the bedside, so lead with it and know where it comes from. Answer: "Charcot's triad is pain, jaundice and fever, and it tells me this is cholangitis. Reynolds' pentad adds hypotension and altered mental state, and it tells me this is suppurative cholangitis and the duct must be drained now." The clinical content is the escalation: the triad is a diagnosis, the pentad is an indication for urgent decompression rather than for more imaging. Attribute the pentad to SRB, since Bailey & Love does not carry it, and if asked for a grading system from the British literature, the one in B&L 28e is the Tokyo Consensus Guidelines.B&L 71 · p.1244

5Investigations, and why each one

B&L's sequence, which is the one to recite "In a jaundiced patient, the usual blood tests and ultrasound scan should be performed. Ultrasonography will determine if the bile duct is dilated. If it is, and there is a genuine suspicion of a tumour in the head of the pancreas, the preferred test is a contrast-enhanced CT scan. In the majority of instances, this should establish if there is a tumour in the pancreas and if it is resectable."B&L 72 · p.1283

Bloods, then ultrasound, then cross-sectional imaging. Say it in that order and you have given the whole algorithm in three sentences.

Blood tests

Liver function tests
A conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia with a disproportionately raised alkaline phosphatase and gamma-GT is the cholestatic pattern; B&L lists exactly elevated bilirubin, ALP and GGT as the biochemical findings in cholangiocarcinoma.B&L 71 · p.1256 SRB's threshold: direct bilirubin above a defined fraction of the total marks it as conjugated.SRB 12 · p.646
Prothrombin time and INR: the test that must not wait
Because vitamin K malabsorption prolongs it and it must be corrected before ERCP as well as before surgery, with vitamin K or fresh-frozen plasma.B&L 72 · p.1283 SRB names the dependent factors, II, VII, IX and X, and notes PT reflects the extrinsic pathway.SRB 12 · p.647
Renal function
Because hepatorenal syndrome and renal failure are listed consequences of obstructive jaundice,SRB 12 · p.647 and because dehydration is common and must be corrected before operating.SRB 14 · p.698
Urinary urobilinogen, which only one book teaches
SRB: bilirubin secreted in bile is converted by gut bacteria to urobilinogen, most of which is reabsorbed and recirculated with a fraction excreted in urine, so "absence of urobilinogen in the urine signifies obstructive jaundice".SRB 12 · p.624, p.625 Neither Bailey & Love ch.71 nor S Das ch.34 mentions urobilinogen at all. It is a sound test of complete obstruction and a reasonable thing to offer, but attribute it to SRB and remember that in a partial or intermittent obstruction urobilinogen will still be present.
CA19-9, with its limits stated
B&L: "not highly specific or sensitive, but a baseline level should be established; if it is initially raised, it can be useful later in identifying recurrence".B&L 72 · p.1283 So it is a follow-up marker, not a diagnostic test, and saying that is better than reciting a cut-off.

Imaging, in order

1. Ultrasound: first, always
B&L: in a patient with obstructive jaundice ultrasound can identify intra- and extrahepatic biliary dilatation, the level of the obstruction, and stones within the extrahepatic biliary tree.B&L 71 · p.1235 It answers the first question, is this obstruction, and begins to answer the second, where.
2. MRCP: the non-invasive map
"A non-invasive modality that provides excellent images of the gallbladder and" the ducts, with results comparable to those obtained at ERCP or percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography.B&L 71 · p.1236 B&L's own summary of what each modality adds: USG for stones and biliary dilatation; MRCP for anatomy and stones; CT for anatomy and for liver, biliary and pancreatic cancer.B&L 71 · p.1238
3. Contrast-enhanced CT: for the tumour and for resectability
The preferred test once ultrasound shows a dilated duct and a pancreatic head tumour is suspected.B&L 72 · p.1283 SRB's equivalent: spiral or three-dimensional CT is ideal, to detect operability, portal vein invasion, size and extent.SRB 14 · p.699
4. Endoscopic ultrasound, for the four situations it is better at
When CT fails to demonstrate a tumour; when a tissue diagnosis is needed before surgery, for instance to distinguish inflammation from neoplasia in a mass arising on chronic pancreatitis; when vascular invasion must be confirmed; and to separate cystic tumours from pseudocysts. And the reason to prefer its biopsy route: transduodenal or transgastric fine-needle or Trucut biopsy under endoscopic ultrasound guidance avoids spillage of tumour cells into the peritoneal cavity.B&L 72 · p.1283
5. The double duct sign
Concurrent narrowing of both the bile duct and the main pancreatic duct, which is the cholangiographic signature of a tumour at the head of the pancreas.B&L 72 · p.1265
6. Percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography
B&L: its main indication is to drain intrahepatic ducts when strictures cannot be accessed at ERCP, undertaken after confirming normal coagulation parameters and under antibiotic cover.B&L 71 · p.1238 SRB agrees: done in severe obstructive jaundice under cover of antibiotics and after controlling any bleeding tendency.SRB 12 · p.625
7. Staging the rest of the patient
CT chest and FDG-PET are routinely used to complete the staging. And diagnostic laparoscopy before an attempted resection can spare a proportion of patients an unnecessary laparotomy by identifying small peritoneal and liver metastases, and can be combined with laparoscopic ultrasonography.B&L 72 · p.1283
Is ERCP an investigation? SRB 12 lists it among the investigations; B&L 71 p.1237 says it is now therapeutic only
Bailey & Love 28e
Unambiguous: "This technique is now used only as a therapeutic modality in patients with obstructive jaundice; USG and MRCP have taken over the diagnostic aspect."B&L 71 · p.1237 Its indications for ERCP in a jaundiced patient are therapeutic or problem-solving: if there is any suggestion of cholangitis, if there is diagnostic doubt, or if there is likely to be a delay between diagnosis and surgery in a deeply jaundiced patient with distressing pruritus.B&L 72 · p.1283
SRB
Retains ERCP within the investigative armoury of biliary disease and describes its diagnostic and interventional uses together, including sphincterotomy, stone extraction with a Dormia basket, stenting and stricture dilatation.SRB 12 · p.623SRB 14 · p.690
Both are right about their own context, and the resolution is a rule about sequence rather than a choice of test. Answer: "I would not use ERCP to make the diagnosis. Ultrasound and MRCP give me the anatomy non-invasively, and CT tells me about a tumour and its resectability. I would use ERCP when I intend to do something at the same sitting: extract a stone, brush or biopsy a stricture, or stent a duct, and specifically if there is cholangitis, diagnostic doubt after non-invasive imaging, or distressing pruritus with a delay before surgery."B&L 71 · p.1237B&L 72 · p.1283 Then add the safety point, which is the mark: correct the prothrombin time before ERCP, not just before the operation, and give antibiotic cover. The reason ERCP has retreated to a therapeutic role is that it carries the risks of pancreatitis, bleeding, perforation and cholangitis, and there is no reason to accept them for information MRCP gives for free.

6Treatment, and the reasoning behind each choice

Before anything else

Correct the coagulopathy
Vitamin K or fresh-frozen plasma, guided by the prothrombin time, before any intervention including ERCP.B&L 72 · p.1283
Rehydrate, and protect the kidneys
SRB: adequate hydration is important in preventing postoperative dehydration, which is common in obstructive jaundice; and the inadequate hepatic glycogen reserve should be replenished with preoperative glucose.SRB 14 · p.698
Antibiotics, and nutrition
SRB: antibiotics from the day before surgery, cephalosporins or aminoglycosides; total parenteral nutrition often required preoperatively and continued postoperatively; pulmonary function assessment and respiratory physiotherapy. And a specific negative: "Preoperative albumin therapy is usually not done."SRB 14 · p.699
Treat cholangitis as an emergency
Antibiotics and drainage, endoscopic or percutaneous. B&L's general principle for biliary obstruction with infection: cholangitis or jaundice are treated with appropriate antibiotic therapy and endoscopic or interventional drainage.B&L 71 · p.1242 Reynolds' pentad means the duct is drained now, not investigated further.
Should you stent a resectable tumour before operating? B&L 72 p.1283 says not routinely; B&L 71 p.1256 says yes before major liver resection
Bailey & Love 28e, for pancreatic head cancer
"Otherwise, preoperative ERCP and biliary stenting is not routine in patients with resectable disease as it is associated with a higher incidence of infective complications after surgery." The exceptions are the three named indications: cholangitis, diagnostic doubt, or a delay before surgery in a deeply jaundiced patient with distressing pruritus. And a technical rider: if a stent is placed in a patient who may undergo resection, use a plastic stent or a covered metal stent, because these can be easily removed during surgery.B&L 72 · p.1283
Bailey & Love 28e, for hilar tumours requiring liver resection
The opposite advice, with a threshold: patients undergoing anticipated major liver resection need preoperative biliary drainage, as hyperbilirubinaemia with a bilirubin above 6 mg/dL may impair hepatic regeneration.B&L 71 · p.1256
These are not in conflict once you notice they concern different operations, and saying so is the whole answer. Answer: "For a resectable pancreatic head cancer I would not stent routinely, because preoperative stenting increases infective complications after surgery. I would stent for cholangitis, for diagnostic doubt, or if surgery must be delayed in a deeply jaundiced patient with distressing pruritus, and I would use a plastic or covered metal stent so it can be removed at operation. The exception is a hilar tumour needing a major liver resection, where biliary drainage is needed beforehand because a bilirubin above 6 mg per decilitre impairs hepatic regeneration."B&L 72 · p.1283B&L 71 · p.1256

The underlying principle to state if pressed: drain the biliary tree before surgery when the liver has to do something difficult afterwards, such as regenerate, or when the patient cannot safely wait; otherwise go straight to resection.

If the cause is a stone

Endoscopic clearance, then cholecystectomy
ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction, using a Dormia basket, and stenting if there is a stricture.SRB 14 · p.690 In gallstone pancreatitis SRB favours early endoscopic intervention within 48 hours, and then laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 2 weeks of the acute attack, during the same admission.SRB 14 · p.681
And the complication to know by name
Sump syndrome, after a choledochoduodenostomy for CBD stones: debris collects in the blind distal segment of duct below the anastomosis.SRB 12 · p.646

If the cause is carcinoma of the head of the pancreas

How many can be resected, which is the first thing to say
B&L: "At presentation, more than 85% of patients with ductal adenocarcinoma are unsuitable for resection because of advanced disease."B&L 72 · p.1284 SRB's numbers agree and break it down: only 10 to 15% of pancreatic head carcinomas are operable; 40 to 50% are locally advanced; another 40 to 50% have distant spread to liver or lungs.SRB 14 · p.699
What does and does not contraindicate resection
Clear contraindications: hepatic or peritoneal metastases; lymph node metastases distant from the pancreatic head; encasement of the superior mesenteric, hepatic or coeliac artery; and complete encasement and occlusion of the portal or superior mesenteric vein, as well as any degree of arterial involvement.B&L 72 · p.1283
Not contraindicationsTumour size; continuous invasion of the duodenum, stomach or colon; lymph node metastases within the operative field; and a tumour that abuts or minimally invades the portal or superior mesenteric vein, since part of the vein can be resected.B&L 72 · p.1283 Knowing the second list is what separates a candidate who has read the chapter from one who has read a summary.
The four categories, and the treatment of each
Resectable: surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy; some centres suggest neoadjuvant chemotherapy first, but the evidence is not strong. Borderline resectable, usually from significant venous occlusion or arterial abutment: neoadjuvant chemotherapy with or without chemoradiotherapy, then resection if downstaged, then adjuvant therapy. Locally advanced and unresectable: systemic chemotherapy, with surgery possible later in a small downstaged cohort. Metastatic: systemic chemotherapy.B&L 72 · p.1284
Whipple, and what the modern operation actually is
"The original pancreatoduodenectomy as proposed by Whipple included resection of the gastric antrum." Preserving the antrum and pylorus gives a more physiological outcome with no difference in survival or recurrence rates, so "the Whipple procedure is now reserved for situations in which the entire duodenum has to be" removed.B&L 72 · p.1284 In other words the standard operation today is the pylorus-preserving pancreatoduodenectomy, and calling it a Whipple is loose usage.
Histological confirmation: desirable, not essential
"Histological confirmation of malignancy is desirable but not essential, particularly if the imaging clearly demonstrates a resectable tumour. The lack of a tissue diagnosis should not delay appropriate surgical therapy." But in patients judged unresectable, a tissue diagnosis should be obtained before starting palliative therapy.B&L 72 · p.1283 And percutaneous transperitoneal biopsy of a potentially resectable pancreatic tumour should be avoided as far as possible, because of seeding.
And a decision rule about age
"Biological rather than chronological age should be the consideration."B&L 72 · p.1284 Worth quoting when asked whether a 78-year-old should have a pancreatoduodenectomy.

If it is a cholangiocarcinoma

Risk factors, which are mostly things that inflame the duct chronically
Chronic inflammatory: primary sclerosing cholangitis, Oriental cholangiohepatitis, hepatitis C. Parasitic: Opisthorchis viverrini, Clonorchis sinensis. Congenital: choledochal cysts, Caroli's disease. Chemical: Thorotrast, vinyl chloride, dioxin, asbestos. Post-surgical: a biliary-enteric anastomosis. And non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.B&L 71 · p.1256
Where the gallbladder is palpable, and where it is not
B&L: in cholangiocarcinoma the gallbladder is palpable if the obstruction is in the distal CBD, Courvoisier's sign.B&L 71 · p.1256 A hilar tumour, a Klatskin tumour, obstructs above the cystic duct, so the gallbladder is collapsed and Courvoisier's sign is negative. That is the one circumstance in which a negative Courvoisier localises the lesion for you, and it is worth volunteering.
Choledochal cyst, since it is both a cause and a risk factor
SRB: females 4:1, common in Japan, presenting in infants, children and adults, with obstructive jaundice in 80%, pain in the right hypochondrium, cholangitis, and a mass.SRB 12 · p.629 B&L: most patients present before the age of adulthood, with abdominal pain, cholangitis or end-stage liver disease, and after surgery bacterial cholangitis occurs in 40% of patients, repeated attacks leading to hepatic fibrosis, so timely referral for surgery is critical.B&L 71 · p.1241

Palliation

The aim, stated plainly
In locally advanced or metastatic disease "the primary objective should be to relieve symptoms, improve quality of life and extend survival, rather than achieve a cure".B&L 72 · p.1284
Relieve the jaundice
Endoscopic stenting is the usual route, and B&L notes that ERCP relieves the jaundice and can also provide brush cytology or a biopsy specimen to confirm the diagnosis.B&L 72 · p.1283 Percutaneous drainage when the stricture cannot be reached endoscopically.B&L 71 · p.1238
And relieve the itch
Cholestyramine or colestipol, remembering they worsen fat-soluble vitamin deficiency,SRB 12 · p.647 so give vitamin K alongside.

7Rapid-fire viva

Define obstructive jaundice and give the normal and visible bilirubin levels.

Jaundice developing from biliary obstruction, partial, complete or intermittent, causing a conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia; also called surgical jaundice, and it may be intrahepatic or extrahepatic. Normal serum bilirubin is 0.2 to 0.8 mg%, and scleral icterus becomes visible above 2.5 mg%.SRB 12 · p.646, p.647 SRB adds that in obstruction the bilirubin will not exceed about 40 mg%, because the rising intrabiliary pressure itself blocks further secretion.SRB 14 · p.699

State Courvoisier's law and give its exceptions.

In a jaundiced patient a palpably distended gallbladder is unlikely to be due to a stone, because a stone-bearing gallbladder is chronically inflamed, fibrotic and cannot distend; so the obstruction is usually below the cystic duct in a previously normal gallbladder, most often a periampullary or pancreatic head carcinoma.SRB 12 · p.646Das 34 · p.492B&L 71 · p.1244 The exception both Indian books agree on is double impaction, a stone in the cystic duct and another in the CBD, giving a mucocele with jaundice. SRB adds a large stone in Hartmann's pouch and empyema of the gallbladder with a CBD stone;SRB 12 · p.646 S Das adds Oriental cholangiohepatitis and a pancreatic calculus obstructing the ampulla of Vater.Das 34 · p.491 Bailey and Love gives no exceptions.

Did Courvoisier mention cancer?

Bailey and Love 28e answers this twice and differently. Chapter 71's eponym footnote says he made his observation in 1890 and that no mention was made of either gallbladder tenderness or malignancy.B&L 71 · p.1244 Chapter 72 says he first drew attention to the association of an enlarged gallbladder with a pancreatic tumour in 1890, and quotes him saying that when the duct is obstructed in some other way, such as a neoplasm, distension of the normal gallbladder is common.B&L 72 · p.1283 SRB prints the original words, which contrast stone with obstruction from other causes and do not name cancer.SRB 12 · p.631 The safe answer is that his observation contrasted stone with non-stone obstruction, and the association with malignancy is the inference the profession drew.

Your patient has a hilar cholangiocarcinoma. Will Courvoisier's sign be positive?

No. The gallbladder is palpable only if the obstruction is in the distal common bile duct, that is below the cystic duct; a hilar or Klatskin tumour obstructs above it, so the gallbladder is collapsed and the sign is negative.B&L 71 · p.1256 It is the one situation where a negative Courvoisier actively helps you localise the lesion.

Why does this patient bleed, and what do you do about it?

Absence of bile in the gut impairs absorption of fat and therefore of vitamin K, so synthesis of the vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X falls and the prothrombin time and INR rise; PT reflects the extrinsic pathway.SRB 12 · p.647 Check the prothrombin time and correct it with vitamin K or fresh-frozen plasma, and do so before ERCP as well as before surgery.B&L 72 · p.1283 Note that cholestyramine given for pruritus binds bile salts and can worsen the deficiency.

Charcot's triad and Reynolds' pentad?

Charcot's triad is pain, jaundice and fever, and it makes the diagnosis of cholangitis.B&L 71 · p.1253 Reynolds' pentad adds hypotension and altered mental state, and it marks acute obstructive or suppurative cholangitis, which SRB puts at about 5%; it is an indication for urgent biliary drainage, not for more imaging.SRB 12 · p.643, p.648 Note that Reynolds' pentad does not appear in Bailey and Love 28e's biliary chapter at all, so attribute it to SRB.

Explain how cholangitis becomes septic shock.

Obstruction causes bile stasis; stasis allows colonisation and multiplication of organisms, bile normally being sterile; intraductal pressure rises; biliary contents reflux into the circulation; and bacteraemia, septicaemia and septic shock follow.SRB 12 · p.647 The commonest organisms in symptomatic gallstone disease are E. coli and Klebsiella.SRB 12 · p.625 It is most often seen with a stricture, usually at the level of the CBD. The therapeutic consequence is that antibiotics alone are insufficient: the pressure has to come off the duct.

Classify the causes of obstructive jaundice.

By pathology: congenital, biliary atresia and choledochal cyst; inflammatory, ascending and sclerosing cholangitis; obstructive, CBD stones, stricture and parasitic infestation; neoplastic, carcinoma of the head or periampullary pancreas, cholangiocarcinoma, Klatskin tumour; and extrinsic compression by nodes or tumour.SRB 12 · p.647 Or anatomically, which is better in a viva: extrahepatic causes are luminal, including stones and ascariasis or clonorchiasis; mural, including growth, stricture and sclerosing cholangitis; or external compression. And intrahepatic cholestasis from hepatocellular disease or drugs.SRB 12 · p.647

Give Benjamin's classification.

Type 1, complete obstruction: pancreatic tumours, cholangiocarcinoma, iatrogenic CBD ligation, liver tumours. Type 2, intermittent: choledocholithiasis, periampullary tumour, choledochal cyst, bile duct papilloma, haemobilia, duodenal diverticula. Type 3, chronic incomplete: stricture, congenital, traumatic, post-radiotherapy, chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis. Type 4, segmental: traumatic, sclerosing cholangitis, cholangiocarcinoma, hepatolithiasis.SRB 12 · p.648 It is SRB's, not Bailey and Love's. Its practical use is that it tells you to ask whether the jaundice has ever remitted: intermittent jaundice means a stone or a periampullary tumour, not a pancreatic head cancer.

What is the first imaging test, and what does each subsequent one add?

Ultrasound first: it shows intra- and extrahepatic biliary dilatation, the level of obstruction, and extrahepatic stones. MRCP adds anatomy and stones non-invasively, with results comparable to ERCP or PTC. CT adds the tumour and its resectability, and liver and pancreatic disease. Endoscopic ultrasound is for when CT shows no tumour, when tissue is needed, when vascular invasion must be confirmed, or to separate a cystic tumour from a pseudocyst.B&L 71 · p.1235, p.1236, p.1238B&L 72 · p.1283

Would you do an ERCP to diagnose this?

No. Bailey and Love 28e says ERCP is now used only as a therapeutic modality in obstructive jaundice, ultrasound and MRCP having taken over the diagnostic aspect.B&L 71 · p.1237 I would use it when I intend to intervene at the same sitting, and specifically for cholangitis, diagnostic doubt after non-invasive imaging, or a deeply jaundiced patient with distressing pruritus facing a delay before surgery.B&L 72 · p.1283 It also provides brush cytology or biopsy. Correct the prothrombin time and give antibiotics first.

What is the double duct sign?

Concurrent narrowing of both the common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct, which is the cholangiographic signature of a tumour at the head of the pancreas.B&L 72 · p.1265

Is absence of urinary urobilinogen a useful test?

It signifies obstructive jaundice, because bilirubin is not reaching the gut to be converted by bacteria into urobilinogen, so none is reabsorbed and none is excreted.SRB 12 · p.624, p.625 Two caveats: it is only valid in complete obstruction, since in a partial or intermittent obstruction urobilinogen will still appear; and it is SRB's teaching, appearing in neither Bailey and Love's biliary chapter nor S Das's chapter, so attribute it.

How long can you leave an obstructed biliary tree?

Not long. SRB: severe biliary obstruction causes cell damage usually within one month and, if unrelieved, may lead to secondary biliary cirrhosis.SRB 12 · p.647 Add the other consequences: cholangitis, altered coagulation, hepatorenal syndrome and renal failure, and sepsis. So relief of obstruction is not an elective matter even when the cause is benign.

What proportion of pancreatic head cancers can be resected?

Few. More than 85% are unsuitable for resection at presentation because of advanced disease.B&L 72 · p.1284 SRB's breakdown agrees: only 10 to 15% operable, 40 to 50% locally advanced, and 40 to 50% with distant spread to liver or lungs.SRB 14 · p.699

What makes a pancreatic head tumour unresectable, and what does not?

Contraindications: hepatic or peritoneal metastases; nodal metastases distant from the pancreatic head; encasement of the superior mesenteric, hepatic or coeliac artery; and complete encasement and occlusion of the portal or superior mesenteric vein, or any degree of arterial involvement. Not contraindications: tumour size; continuous invasion of duodenum, stomach or colon; nodal metastases within the operative field; and a tumour that abuts or minimally invades the portal or superior mesenteric vein, since part of the vein can be resected.B&L 72 · p.1283

Would you stent a resectable pancreatic head cancer before operating?

Not routinely: preoperative ERCP and stenting is associated with a higher incidence of infective complications after surgery. I would stent for cholangitis, for diagnostic doubt, or if there is a delay before surgery in a deeply jaundiced patient with distressing pruritus, and would use a plastic or covered metal stent so it can be removed at operation.B&L 72 · p.1283 The exception is a hilar tumour requiring major liver resection, where preoperative drainage is needed because a bilirubin above 6 mg/dL impairs hepatic regeneration.B&L 71 · p.1256

Do you need a biopsy before a pancreatoduodenectomy?

Desirable but not essential, particularly when imaging clearly shows a resectable tumour, and the lack of a tissue diagnosis should not delay appropriate surgery. Percutaneous transperitoneal biopsy of a potentially resectable tumour should be avoided because of seeding; EUS-guided transduodenal or transgastric biopsy avoids spillage into the peritoneal cavity. But in unresectable disease, obtain tissue before starting palliative therapy.B&L 72 · p.1283

Is the standard operation still a Whipple?

Strictly, no. Whipple's original pancreatoduodenectomy included resection of the gastric antrum; preserving the antrum and pylorus gives a more physiological outcome with no difference in survival or recurrence, so the pylorus-preserving operation is standard and the classical Whipple is reserved for cases where the entire duodenum must be removed.B&L 72 · p.1284

Classify pancreatic cancer patients for treatment.

Four groups. Resectable: surgery then adjuvant chemotherapy, with neoadjuvant chemotherapy suggested by some centres but the evidence not strong. Borderline resectable, usually from venous occlusion or arterial abutment: neoadjuvant chemotherapy with or without chemoradiotherapy, then resection if downstaged, then adjuvant therapy. Locally advanced and unresectable: systemic chemotherapy, with surgery possible in a small downstaged cohort. Metastatic: systemic chemotherapy.B&L 72 · p.1284 Every patient should ideally be discussed in a multidisciplinary forum, and biological rather than chronological age should decide fitness.

A patient with jaundice sits up all night with his arms folded. Why?

Pancreatic pain. S Das: the peculiar feature of pancreatic pain is that it passes through to the back, is aggravated on lying down and lessened on sitting up, so the patient often spends the night sitting with his arms folded across his chest.Das 34 · p.511 Bailey and Love's equivalent warning is that back pain raises the possibility of retroperitoneal infiltration.B&L 72 · p.1283 It suggests the body and tail, or a locally advanced head tumour, and it is a bad prognostic sign either way.

Which signs on examination would tell you not to operate for cure?

A palpable mass, ascites, supraclavicular nodes and tumour deposits in the pelvis: Bailey and Love says these should be looked for with care and, when present, indicate a grim prognosis.B&L 72 · p.1283 So palpate the left supraclavicular fossa and perform a rectal examination, both of which are free and both of which can change the plan from resection to palliation. Remember that a non-palpable tumour proves nothing: S Das says the tumour itself is seldom palpable.Das 34 · p.511

Name the risk factors for cholangiocarcinoma.

Chronic inflammatory conditions: primary sclerosing cholangitis, Oriental cholangiohepatitis, hepatitis C. Parasitic infections: Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis. Congenital: choledochal cysts and Caroli's disease. Chemical agents: Thorotrast, vinyl chloride, dioxin, asbestos. Post-surgical: a biliary-enteric anastomosis. And non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.B&L 71 · p.1256 Note how many of them are the same parasitic and inflammatory diseases that appear as causes of obstruction in SRB's list.

Why does the patient itch, and what do you give?

Honestly, the mechanism is not settled. SRB says the cause is not clear and may be accumulation of bile acids in the skin or release of endogenous opioids.SRB 12 · p.647 Treatment is relief of the obstruction, and meanwhile cholestyramine or colestipol, remembering that both bind bile salts and can worsen fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, so give vitamin K alongside.

How would you prepare this patient for surgery?

Correct the prothrombin time with vitamin K or fresh-frozen plasma.B&L 72 · p.1283 Rehydrate, since dehydration is common in obstructive jaundice, and replenish hepatic glycogen with preoperative glucose. Antibiotics from the day before, cephalosporins or aminoglycosides. Total parenteral nutrition if needed, continued postoperatively. Pulmonary function assessment and respiratory physiotherapy. SRB notes specifically that preoperative albumin therapy is usually not done.SRB 14 · p.698, p.699 And relieve the obstruction first only if there is cholangitis, diagnostic doubt, a delay with distressing pruritus, or a planned major liver resection.

What is sump syndrome?

A late complication of choledochoduodenostomy for CBD stones: debris and food collect in the blind distal segment of duct below the anastomosis, the sump, causing recurrent cholangitis.SRB 12 · p.646

A child or young adult with jaundice, right hypochondrial pain and a mass. Diagnosis?

Choledochal cyst. SRB: female to male 4:1, common in Japan, presenting in infants, children and adults, with obstructive jaundice in 80%, pain in the right hypochondrium, cholangitis and a mass.SRB 12 · p.629 Bailey and Love adds that after surgery bacterial cholangitis occurs in 40%, repeated attacks lead to hepatic fibrosis, and timely referral for surgery is therefore critical.B&L 71 · p.1241 It is also a risk factor for cholangiocarcinoma, which is why it is excised rather than merely drained.
