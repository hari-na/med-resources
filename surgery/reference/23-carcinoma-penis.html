Short case 23 · General Surgery

Carcinoma of the Penis

A painless indurated lump or ulcer on the glans or prepuce in an uncircumcised man over forty, with a foul discharge and big groin nodes. The diagnosis is easy. The marks are in three places: retracting or splitting the prepuce so you actually see the lesion, knowing that half the enlarged nodes are infective rather than malignant, and knowing how much penis you now have to remove, which is the thing the books disagree about.

S Das: clinical method Bailey & Love: mechanism and management SRB: exam framework

1Aetiology, and the five facts to open with

1. Circumcision
B&L: "Circumcision soon after birth confers immunity against carcinoma of the penis. Later circumcision does not seem to have the same benefit, with the assumption that smegma is in some way carcinogenic."B&L 85 · p.1551 SRB: "Circumcision during infancy confers total immunity against carcinoma penis."SRB 26 · p.1052 S Das makes the same observation from the epidemiology of communities that practise neonatal circumcision.Das 40 · p.631
The mechanism to giveSmegma, retained behind a non-retractile prepuce, is assumed to be the carcinogen. That single assumption links circumcision, phimosis, chronic balanoposthitis and poor hygiene into one causal story, which is why it is worth saying rather than just listing the risk factors. And a disagreement about the timingThe three books do not quite agree on whether circumcision later in infancy still protects. See the first amber box.
2. Human papillomavirus
B&L: "Infection with HPV types 16 and 18 is a risk factor."B&L 85 · p.1551 SRB names HPV-16 with HIV infection, and condyloma acuminata (by human papilloma virus).SRB 26 · p.1052
Do not confuse the two sets of types16 and 18 cause cancer; 6 and 11 cause genital warts and the Buschke-Löwenstein tumour. B&L gives both: condylomata acuminata are caused by "HPV types 6 and 11".B&L 85 · p.1546
3. The premalignant conditions
All three books give substantially the same list. B&L: "definite precancerous states including leukoplakia of the glans, which is similar to the condition seen on the tongue, and penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PeIN)", plus lichen sclerosus and smoking.B&L 85 · p.1551 SRB adds long-standing genital warts, Paget's disease of the penis, erythroplasia of Queyrat, which it defines as "persistent rawness of glans penis", and balanitis xerotica obliterans.SRB 26 · p.1052 S Das's list is chronic balanoposthitis, leukoplakia, papillomas, Paget's disease and erythroplasia of Queyrat.Das 40 · p.638
One term worth unpackingBalanitis xerotica obliterans is lichen sclerosus of the glans and prepuce. B&L uses "lichen sclerosus" and SRB uses "balanitis xerotica obliterans", and a candidate who recognises them as the same condition will not be caught out by either book.
4. Phimosis, and the warning sign in an adult
B&L: "phimosis and chronic balanoposthitis are known to be contributory factors."B&L 85 · p.1551 SRB lists among the problems of phimosis "carcinoma of penis later", alongside recurrent balanoposthitis, calculus formation from smegma and retention of urine.SRB 26 · p.1048
The sentence that wins a vivaSRB: "in an adult, recent onset of phimosis should raise the suspicion of carcinoma penis."SRB 26 · p.1053 A prepuce that has always been tight is phimosis; a prepuce that has recently become tight in a man of fifty is a carcinoma growing underneath it.
5. Where it is common
SRB: "it is common in Asia and Africa."SRB 26 · p.1052 That is relevant: this is a case you will see in India and may never see in a British hospital.
Does circumcision in infancy protect, or only circumcision in the newborn? And how many patients are under forty? Two live disagreements in the same paragraph. S Das 40 p.638 against SRB 26 p.1052 on the timing, and against B&L 85 p.1552 on the age figure
On the timing of circumcision
S Das: "Firstly circumcision, if correctly performed soon after birth, confers almost total immunity against this disease. But circumcision done in early infancy does not provide the same degree of immunity."Das 40 · p.638
SRB: "Circumcision during infancy confers total immunity against carcinoma penis."SRB 26 · p.1052
B&L: "Circumcision soon after birth confers immunity against carcinoma of the penis. Later circumcision does not seem to have the same benefit."B&L 85 · p.1551
So S Das draws the line between the newborn and early infancy, and SRB says infancy as a whole confers total immunity. B&L agrees with S Das's direction but says only "later" without defining it. S Das's sentence, as printed, distinguishes "soon after birth" from "early infancy", which are so close together that the distinction is hard to credit; it reads like a printing error for "later in life", which is what B&L and the usual teaching say.
On the proportion under forty
S Das: "Though majority of the patients are above 40 years of age yet 30% of the patients are under 40 years of age."Das 40 · p.638
B&L: "About 10% of patients are under 40 years of age."B&L 85 · p.1552
A threefold difference, and neither book qualifies its figure. Both are compatible with the same clinical rule, that this is a disease of men over forty and that youth does not exclude it.
How to answer: on circumcision, give the direction and not a boundary you cannot defend. "Neonatal circumcision confers near-total immunity against carcinoma of the penis. Circumcision performed later in life does not confer the same protection, which supports the view that retained smegma under a non-retractile prepuce is the carcinogenic stimulus." Do not volunteer "early infancy does not protect", because SRB says the opposite and an examiner working from SRB will mark it wrong.

On the age: say "the majority are over forty, but a substantial minority are younger, quoted between about 10 and 30% depending on the series", or give whichever book's figure the examination is set from. The clinically useful point is the one both books imply: a penile lesion in a young man still needs a biopsy.

2Pathology

The cell type, and the one exception
B&L: "carcinoma of the penis is most typically a squamous cell carcinoma arising in the skin of the glans penis or the prepuce."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das adds the rarity: "majority of the carcinomas are of squamous variety, only rarely columnar variety may develop arising from Tyson's glands (situated on either side of the frenum)."Das 40 · p.638 That columnar variant from Tyson's glands appears in neither of the other two books, and it is the kind of detail an examiner who learned from S Das may ask for.
The three macroscopic types
SRB: infiltrating, which "occurs in a preexisting leukoplakia. It often presents as indurated area"; papilliferous, which "eventually attains a large size forming a fungating foul smelling lesion which often gets infected"; and ulcerative.SRB 26 · p.1053 B&L gives two and ties each to its precursor: "it may be flat and infiltrating or warty in appearance. The former often starts as leukoplakia or PeIN; the latter results from an existing papilloma."B&L 85 · p.1552
The point of the two-type divisionFlat and infiltrating comes from leukoplakia or carcinoma in situ; warty comes from a papilloma. Saying which precursor goes with which appearance turns a list into an explanation.
Site and grade
SRB: "glans penis is the most common site (coronal sulcus for basal cell carcinoma)", and "80% are of low grade tumours."SRB 26 · p.1053
Penile intraepithelial neoplasia, and its two eponyms
B&L's definition settles the naming, which is a favourite question: "PeIN is typically seen as a red cutaneous patch on the penis. When it occurs on the glans penis, it is known as erythroplasia of Queyrat; when it occurs on the shaft of the penis, it is called Bowen's disease."B&L 85 · p.1552
So the eponym is decided by the site, not the histology Glans: Queyrat. Shaft: Bowen. Same lesion, two names. And B&L adds the practical caution: "there are several other benign causes of red patches on the penis; when there is clinical doubt as to the underlying diagnosis a biopsy is indicated."B&L 85 · p.1552
How it spreads: local
Slowly, and with one anatomical barrier worth naming. B&L: "local growth continues for months or years."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das explains why: "direct spread to the body of the penis is prevented by a fascial sheath for many months. Lastly this is a slow growing tumour."Das 40 · p.638
Why that fascial sheath mattersBuck's fascia holds the tumour on the glans for a long time, which is exactly what makes conservative, glans-preserving surgery possible in early disease. The anatomy is the justification for the modern operation.
How it spreads: lymphatic, which is the whole prognosis
B&L: "the earliest lymphatic spread is to the inguinal nodes (N1 and N2 disease) and then to the iliac nodes (N3 disease). Distant metastatic deposits are infrequent."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das: "the earliest spread is to the lymph nodes (first to inguinal group and then to iliac group)."Das 40 · p.638 SRB: through lymphatics "to the horizontal group of inguinal lymph nodes which become nodular and hard. Lymph nodes on both sides can get involved."SRB 26 · p.1053
The three points about lymphatic spread that earn marks 1. It is bilateral, because the lymphatics of the penis cross the midline. So a block dissection is a bilateral operation.
2. The glans drains further than you think. SRB: "from glans, it also spreads to Cloquet lymph node which is located in femoral canal."SRB 26 · p.1053
3. The shaft can skip the groin. SRB: "carcinoma from shaft of penis can spread directly to the external iliac lymph nodes."SRB 26 · p.1053 So impalpable groins do not mean no nodal disease in a shaft tumour.
And SRB's phrase for the whole disease"It is a locoregional malignant disease", with "blood spread is rare".SRB 26 · p.1053 That is why surgery to the primary and the nodes still cures, even in advanced local disease.
How patients die of it
All three books give the same mechanism and it is worth saying. B&L: "the inguinal nodes can erode the skin of the groin and, in rare cases, death of the patient can result from erosion of the femoral or external iliac vessels."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das: "death may occur due to bleeding from external iliac or femoral vessel from erosion by the metastatic lymph nodes."Das 40 · p.638 SRB: once the nodes are fixed, "it may erode into the femoral vessels causing torrential haemorrhage and death."SRB 26 · p.1053
This is also the reason for palliative treatmentSRB gives palliative external radiotherapy to fixed inguinal nodes specifically "to palliate pain, fungation and anticipated erosion into femoral vessels."SRB 26 · p.1055 Pre-empting a fatal bleed is a legitimate reason to irradiate an incurable patient.

3Clinical features and examination

1. How did it present, and how long has he had it? Because late presentation is the rule and the reason is not medical. B&L: "many patients present late as a fungating or ulcerative growth, either because of embarrassment or because of misdiagnosis."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das describes exactly the misdiagnosis: "in an uncircumcised individual mild irritation and purulent discharge from under the prepuce are often the first symptoms. Gradually blood stained foul discharge may be noticed. At this stage the condition may be misdiagnosed as balanoposthitis."Das 40 · p.638 So S Das gives the instruction that follows"One must be clever enough to suspect this to be a case of carcinoma and a dorsal slit must be performed for proper inspection."Das 40 · p.638 B&L says the same: "in many, the prepuce is non-retractile and must be split to view the lesion."B&L 85 · p.1552 You cannot diagnose what you have not seen. A purulent preputial discharge treated as balanoposthitis without retracting or splitting the prepuce is how these cancers are missed.
2. Is it painful? Characteristically not, which is itself part of why it presents late. B&L: "there is typically little or no pain."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das: "it is a painless condition."Das 40 · p.638 SRB explains when it does hurt: "lesion is painless initially but later becomes painful due to secondary infection, often accompanied by discharge which is foul smelling, purulent and irritating."SRB 26 · p.1053 So pain means one of two thingsEither secondary infection of the primary, or fixed inguinal nodes, which SRB says cause "severe excruciating pain and lymphoedema".SRB 26 · p.1053 Pain in this disease is a sign of a complication, not of the tumour.
3. Is the urinary stream altered? Because it tells you the meatus or urethra is involved, which changes the T stage. SRB lists "altered urinary stream" among the clinical features and notes "urethral meatus may get involved causing alteration in urinary stream."SRB 26 · p.1053 B&L's T3 is urethral invasion.B&L 85 · p.1552
4. Describe the lesion properly S Das gives the two forms and the words to use: "majority of the patients present with a lump or an ulcer. The lump is a sessile cauliflower growth with an indurated base whereas an ulcer has the same indurated base with rolled out and everted margin, the floor is formed by necrosed tissues."Das 40 · p.638 Three words to use, in this orderIndurated base. Everted margin. Necrotic floor. An everted or rolled-out edge on an indurated base is a squamous carcinoma anywhere in the body, and saying so connects this case to the ulcer case. Then palpate proximally along the shaft for induration, because SRB notes "it spreads proximally to the body of penis causing induration", and that is what decides partial against total amputation.SRB 26 · p.1053
5. Palpate both groins, and then the iliac nodes Because half of what you feel is not cancer. B&L: "around 50% have inguinal lymph node enlargement at presentation but the nodal enlargement often reflects infection."B&L 85 · p.1552 S Das: "the inguinal lymph nodes are often enlarged, but about half of these cases the enlargement is due to sepsis and the other half due to secondary deposits."Das 40 · p.638 SRB: "only 50% of palpable inguinal nodes are involved by metastatic spread."SRB 26 · p.1054 All three books say the same thing, and it is the single most important fact in the management. Palpate the nodes you were not taught to palpateS Das illustrates both manoeuvres: palpating the inguinal nodes, and palpating the external iliac nodes with the thigh flexed to relax the abdominal muscles.Das 40 · pp.637, 638 He is explicit: "one must palpate the iliac group of lymph nodes to exclude secondaries in these nodes."Das 40 · p.638 SRB says external iliac nodes are felt "above and on medial aspect of the inguinal ligament".SRB 26 · p.1053 Flexing the hip to relax the abdominal wall, and saying why you are doing it, is a free mark. And say what the nodes feel likeMetastatic nodes become "nodular and hard", and later fixed; SRB: "fixed lymph node status indicates the advancement of the disease."SRB 26 · p.1053 Tender, softer, mobile nodes in the presence of a foul infected primary are more likely septic.
Dreslers quadrangle, which no other book gives and which an examiner may want SRB defines the area a groin dissection must clear: "upper border is formed by line joining anterosuperior iliac spine and pubic tubercle; laterally line joining anterosuperior iliac spine and a point 20 cm below it; medially pubic tubercle and a point 15 cm below it. Nodal block dissection for carcinoma penis should cover this area adequately."SRB 26 · p.1055

Learn it as a quadrangle hung from the line between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic tubercle, 20 cm down laterally and 15 cm down medially. It appears nowhere in Bailey & Love 28e.

4Investigations

Biopsy of the primary, which is not optional
B&L says it twice: "a biopsy should be performed", and "a biopsy should be performed to make the diagnosis."B&L 85 · p.1552 SRB specifies the kind and what it shows: "wedge biopsy from the lesion shows squamous cell carcinoma with epithelial pearls."SRB 26 · p.1053
SRB's grading by epithelial pearls, which is unique to it Very well differentiated: 75% epithelial pearls. Well differentiated: 50 to 75%. Moderately differentiated: 25 to 50%. Undifferentiated: under 25%.SRB 26 · p.1053 Grade matters here because SRB uses it to decide both the extent of amputation and whether to do a prophylactic groin dissection.
The nodes: aspirate, never open
SRB is emphatic: "FNAC of lymph nodes (no open biopsy for lymph nodes)."SRB 26 · p.1053 B&L agrees and adds the guidance: "for palpable nodes, ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration will confirm the diagnosis."B&L 85 · p.1553
Why no open biopsyAn incision in the groin before a block dissection contaminates the field, compromises the skin flaps on which the dissection depends, and risks seeding a wound that is already at high risk of necrosis. A needle gives the same answer without any of that.
Sentinel lymph node biopsy
SRB gives the anatomy of the node and names it: "SLNB, Cabana sentinel node is located above and medial to the junction of saphenous and femoral vein. It is the first node to get involved in carcinoma penis. So this sentinel lymph node biopsy after isosulphan blue dye injection into the primary is done to decide for the necessity for ilioinguinal block dissection."SRB 26 · p.1053 B&L gives it the same role, for the impalpable groin: "the management of patients where the nodes are not palpable involves the use of sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) followed by inguinal node dissection if the SLNB is positive."B&L 85 · p.1553
So the two books agree here, and the agreement is useful Palpable nodes: FNAC, then block dissection. Impalpable nodes: sentinel node biopsy, then block dissection only if positive. That is a clean, defensible answer drawn from both books.
Imaging
B&L: "MRI is performed for local staging. Assessment of locoregional lymph node status is essential."B&L 85 · p.1552 SRB: "ultrasound abdomen, to look for involvement of external iliac lymph nodes."SRB 26 · p.1053 B&L also uses CT for the pelvic nodes when deciding on pelvic lymphadenectomy.B&L 85 · p.1553
Why MRI for the primaryBecause the T stage is about which erectile body the tumour has entered, and MRI shows corpus spongiosum and corpus cavernosum. That is the measurement that decides glansectomy against partial penectomy.

5Staging

The T stages, which both books give in the same terms
B&L, compactly: "T1 tumours are confined to the skin, with T2 tumours invading the corpus spongiosum or the corpus cavernosum. T3 tumours invade the urethra and T4 tumours invade adjacent structures."B&L 85 · p.1552
SRB's version, from AJCC 8th edition, 2018, separates the two erectile bodies: T2 invades corpus spongiosum, T3 invades corpora cavernosa, each with or without urethral invasion, and T4 invades adjacent structures such as prostate, scrotum or pubic bone.SRB 26 · p.1054 SRB also subdivides T1a, without lymphovascular or perineural invasion and not high grade, from T1b, with invasion or high grade.SRB 26 · p.1054
Note the differenceB&L puts both erectile bodies in T2 and makes urethral invasion T3. The AJCC 8th edition that SRB reproduces splits spongiosum (T2) from cavernosa (T3) and treats urethral invasion as a modifier rather than a stage. Use SRB's version, because it is the current AJCC and because an Indian paper will be marked against it.
The N stages
B&L: inguinal nodes are N1 and N2, iliac nodes are N3.B&L 85 · p.1552 SRB's clinical N: cN1 one palpable mobile unilateral inguinal node; cN2 two or more palpable mobile unilateral nodes; cN3 a palpable fixed inguinal nodal mass, or pelvic lymphadenopathy, unilateral or bilateral.SRB 26 · p.1054
The useful shorthandN1 one node, N2 several, N3 fixed or pelvic. Fixity and pelvic disease are both N3, which is why a fixed groin is a different prognosis from a mobile one.
Jackson's staging, which SRB gives with survival figures and B&L does not have
Stage I: tumour involving only glans, prepuce or both, 90% five-year survival. Stage II: tumour extending into the body of the penis, 70%. Stage III: tumour with mobile inguinal nodes, 50%. Stage IV: tumour spreading to adjacent structures or fixed nodes, 5%.SRB 26 · p.1053
Why Jackson's is still worth knowingBecause it is clinical. You can stage a patient with your hands at the bedside and quote a prognosis, which is exactly what a clinical examination asks you to do. And the four survival figures, 90, 70, 50, 5, carry the whole message of the disease: mobile nodes still carry a 50% five-year survival, fixed nodes 5%. See the amber box for how B&L's figures compare.
Jackson's staging, or TNM? And which survival figures do you quote? Part live disagreement, part framework change. SRB 26 p.1053 gives Jackson's four stages with survival; the name Jackson does not appear in B&L 85 at all, which gives its own two figures
SRB
Gives both systems, Jackson's first with survival attached to each stage, then the full AJCC 8th edition TNM with stage groupings to stage IV.SRB 26 · pp.1053, 1054 Jackson's four stages: glans or prepuce only 90%; body of penis 70%; mobile inguinal nodes 50%; adjacent structures or fixed nodes 5%.
Bailey & Love 28e
Jackson appears nowhere in the chapter. B&L gives TNM descriptors in prose and then two survival figures grouped by nodal status rather than by stage: "the prognosis for tumours confined to the penis is good with 5-year survival rates in excess of 80%. With nodal involvement the 5-year survival rate falls to around 40%."B&L 85 · p.1553 Its summary box reduces the prognosis to one line: "nodal involvement indicates a poor prognosis."B&L 85 · p.1553
Do the figures actually conflict?
Less than they look. B&L's over 80% for disease confined to the penis is an average of Jackson's stage I (90%) and stage II (70%), which is about right. B&L's around 40% with nodal involvement is an average of Jackson's stage III (50%, mobile nodes) and stage IV (5%, fixed nodes), which is also about right. So the two books are consistent; SRB simply resolves the nodal group into mobile and fixed, which is the distinction that matters clinically.
How to answer: give Jackson's, because it is clinical and because an Indian examiner expects it, and then show you can convert. "On Jackson's clinical staging: stage I confined to glans or prepuce, stage II extending into the body of the penis, stage III with mobile inguinal nodes, stage IV with fixed nodes or invasion of adjacent structures, with five-year survivals of about 90, 70, 50 and 5%. Current practice uses the AJCC TNM eighth edition, in which T2 is invasion of the corpus spongiosum, T3 of the corpora cavernosa, and N3 is fixed inguinal nodes or pelvic nodal disease. Bailey and Love quotes over 80% five-year survival for disease confined to the penis, falling to around 40% once there is nodal involvement." The point to make out loud is the one both systems contain: nodal status, and specifically whether the nodes are mobile or fixed, is the dominant prognostic factor.

6Treatment of the primary

Carcinoma in situ, where the books fully agree
B&L: "when the diagnosis of carcinoma in situ is confirmed, treatment is by means of topical 5-fluorouracil cream, CO2 laser ablation or surgical excision."B&L 85 · p.1552 SRB: "topical 5 FU cream or imiquimab or Mohs surgery or limited wide excision or Nd:YAG laser photoirradiation is useful in carcinoma in situ."SRB 26 · p.1055 So: topical 5-FU, laser, or limited excision, with imiquimab and Mohs as SRB's additions.
Small, low-grade, low-stage tumours: organ preservation
B&L: "patients with small lesions of low grade and low stage are treated with organ-preserving surgery, such as limited excision, Mohs' surgery or laser ablation. Mohs' micrographic surgery is based on sequential tissue excision under repeat microscopic control. This helps in accurately identifying the tumour margin and maximally preserves the uninvolved tissues."B&L 85 · p.1553
Glansectomy, partial and total penectomy
B&L's ladder: "tumours affecting the glans penis require glansectomy, with more advanced tumours requiring partial penectomy. In advanced cases, total penectomy is required with the formation of a perineal urethrostomy. These techniques are indicated even in advanced metastatic disease for reasons of local control."B&L 85 · p.1553
Note that last sentenceLocal control is a reason to operate even when the disease is incurable. This is a locoregional cancer that kills by fungation and vascular erosion, so removing the primary is worth doing even with metastases.
SRB's version of the same ladder is more specific about the measurements, and that is where the books part company: "if growth involves the glans without extending into the proximal part of shaft of the penis, then partial amputation of the penis is done. A length of 2.5 cm stump is retained. Clearance of 2 cm from the proximal extended part of the tumour is needed. Advantage is proper streaming of the urine is possible."SRB 26 · p.1054
And for the more advanced: "if tumour involves the proximal part of the body of penis or if it is anaplastic or poorly differentiated tumour, total amputation of penis is done with perineal urethrostomy."SRB 26 · p.1054
Read the amber box before you quote 2 cmB&L explicitly describes the 2 cm margin as superseded.
Perineal urethrostomy, and its two problems
SRB: "problems with perineal urethrostomy are scrotal ammoniacal dermatitis and stricture at urethrostomy site. Dermatitis is prevented by asking the patient to urinate in sitting position lifting the scrotum upwards. Stricture needs dilatation."SRB 26 · p.1054
The advice is practical and memorableSit to void and lift the scrotum. That one instruction prevents the commoner of the two complications, and it is the sort of thing examiners reward because it shows you have thought about the patient after the operation.
Radiotherapy to the primary
SRB offers it as a curative option in a defined group: "in case of carcinoma in situ, T1 lesion of glans penis or well differentiated tumour in young individual, circumcision and curative radiotherapy to the penis can be given using radioactive tantalum wire implantation (6000 cGy in 7 days) or by wearing radium penile mould continuously or intermittently (6000 cGy in 7 days) or by linear accelerator external beam radiotherapy (6000 cGy in 5 weeks). Involvement of nodes in these patients is less than 10%."SRB 26 · p.1054 Its indications list for radiotherapy is: carcinoma in situ; small lesion under 2 cm; lesion confined to the glans; small lesion in a young individual; advanced inoperable disease; palliation of inoperable groin nodes; and post-operative radiotherapy.SRB 26 · p.1055
B&L offers none of this for the primarySee the amber box.
Chemotherapy
B&L: "chemotherapy is relatively ineffective and currently is reserved for palliation in those with metastatic disease."B&L 85 · p.1553 SRB gives the agents: "5 FU; methotrexate, bleomycin, cisplatin and vincristine", and adds a useful pharmacological point: "bleomycin is a radiosensitiser and so beneficial if RT is planned later."SRB 26 · p.1055
So the honest answerChemotherapy is the weakest arm in this disease. Surgery is the mainstay, which is what B&L's summary box says: "surgery is the mainstay of treatment."B&L 85 · p.1553
How much penis do you take? The 2 cm margin, or as little as possible? A live disagreement, and B&L states the change explicitly. SRB 26 p.1054 requires 2 cm clearance and a 2.5 cm stump; B&L 85 p.1553 calls the 2 cm margin superseded
SRB
Gives the classical measurements, and a functional reason for the stump: "a length of 2.5 cm stump is retained. Clearance of 2 cm from the proximal extended part of the tumour is needed. Advantage is proper streaming of the urine is possible."SRB 26 · p.1054 So the stump length is chosen to let the man stand to void, and the clearance is a fixed 2 cm.
Bailey & Love 28e
Names the old rule in order to retire it: "for most primary tumours surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment, with the traditional view that a 2-cm margin of normal tissue be removed being superseded by a more recent, more conservative view, such that penis-preserving surgery with excision of much lower margins of normal tissue is now accepted."B&L 85 · p.1553 And the operations it offers reflect that: limited excision, Mohs' micrographic surgery, laser ablation for small low-grade tumours, and glansectomy for tumours of the glans, with partial penectomy reserved for more advanced disease.B&L 85 · p.1553
What changed, and why
Two things, and both are worth saying. Pathology: studies of resection specimens showed that penile squamous carcinoma does not spread far beyond its visible edge, which S Das explains anatomically, since "direct spread to the body of the penis is prevented by a fascial sheath for many months" and "this is a slow growing tumour".Das 40 · p.638 So the 2 cm was always more than necessary. Consequence: the organ is not expendable. A 2 cm margin on a glans tumour costs the whole glans and much of the shaft, with devastating sexual and psychological results in a man who will often be cured; Mohs' surgery, which B&L describes as "sequential tissue excision under repeat microscopic control" that "maximally preserves the uninvolved tissues", exists precisely to resolve that tension.B&L 85 · p.1553 Note that the disagreement is about the margin, not the principle; both books agree that excision is the mainstay and that the proximal extent of induration decides partial against total amputation.
How to answer: state the principle, give SRB's measurements, then the modern qualification in a single sentence. "The operation is decided by how far proximally the tumour extends. If the growth is confined to the glans without involving the proximal shaft, a partial amputation is done; SRB teaches a 2 cm clearance from the proximal extent of the tumour, retaining a stump of about 2.5 cm so that the patient can direct his urinary stream. If the proximal body of the penis is involved, or the tumour is poorly differentiated, a total amputation with perineal urethrostomy is required. I would add that the traditional 2 cm margin has been superseded by more conservative penis-preserving surgery, with glansectomy for tumours of the glans and Mohs' micrographic surgery or laser ablation for small low-grade lesions, since the tumour grows slowly and is held up by Buck's fascia, so much smaller margins are now accepted."

For an Indian written paper, write SRB's 2 cm and 2.5 cm, because that is the marked answer, and add the modern sentence at the end. Never omit the proximal-extent rule: it is the actual decision, and the margin is only a number attached to it.
Is there a curative role for radiotherapy to the primary? A live disagreement. SRB 26 pp.1054, 1055 gives curative radiotherapy with doses and techniques; no radiotherapy to the primary appears anywhere in B&L 85
SRB
Offers it as a genuine alternative to amputation in a defined group, with three techniques and a dose for each: "in case of carcinoma in situ, T1 lesion of glans penis or well differentiated tumour in young individual, circumcision and curative radiotherapy to the penis can be given using radioactive tantalum wire implantation (6000 cGy in 7 days) or by wearing radium penile mould continuously or intermittently (6000 cGy in 7 days) or by linear accelerator external beam radiotherapy (6000 cGy in 5 weeks). Involvement of nodes in these patients is less than 10%."SRB 26 · p.1054 SRB also gives post-operative radiotherapy to the groin and palliative radiotherapy for fixed nodes.SRB 26 · p.1055
Bailey & Love 28e
Mentions radiotherapy exactly once in the whole section, and not for the primary: among the options for pelvic nodes, "when the iliac nodes are not enlarged in the presence of N2 disease, the options are observation, pelvic lymphadenectomy or radiotherapy."B&L 85 · p.1553 Neither tantalum nor a radium mould appears anywhere in the chapter. Where SRB offers radiotherapy, B&L offers surgery: limited excision, Mohs', laser ablation, glansectomy.B&L 85 · p.1553 Its summary box is unambiguous: "surgery is the mainstay of treatment."B&L 85 · p.1553
Why the two books diverge
They are competing for the same patient. The reason to irradiate a small glans tumour was always organ preservation, because the surgical alternative used to be a 2 cm margin and an amputation. Once conservative surgery with small margins, glansectomy and Mohs' surgery became available, radiotherapy lost its advantage and kept its disadvantages: urethral stricture, necrosis of the glans, oedema, and the difficulty of following a previously irradiated penis for recurrence. So B&L's silence is not an oversight; it is a consequence of the margin question in the previous box. The one place the books still agree on radiotherapy is palliation of fixed inguinal nodes.
How to answer: give both, and link them to the margin question, which is what makes it a thought-out answer rather than two lists. "Radiotherapy has a curative role in a narrow group: carcinoma in situ, a T1 lesion confined to the glans, or a well-differentiated tumour in a young man, where SRB gives 6000 cGy by tantalum wire implant, radium mould or external beam, with circumcision first so the prepuce does not take the dose. The purpose is organ preservation. Bailey and Love 28e offers no radiotherapy to the primary at all, because the same purpose is now served surgically, by glansectomy, Mohs' micrographic surgery and laser ablation with small margins, which avoids the late effects of irradiating the penis. Both books use radiotherapy for palliation of fixed inguinal nodes, where it relieves pain and fungation and may pre-empt erosion of the femoral vessels." Add SRB's pharmacological detail if there is room: bleomycin is a radiosensitiser, so it is useful if radiotherapy is planned.

7Treatment of the nodes

The principle, which both books state the same way
Treat the primary first, give antibiotics, wait, then reassess the nodes. The reason is the 50% figure: half of palpable nodes are reactive to the infected primary, and once the primary is removed and the sepsis treated, those nodes shrink. Operating on all of them means doing a morbid dissection on patients who do not need it.
B&L's sequence
"Treatment of any associated enlarged inguinal lymph nodes should be delayed until at least 3 weeks after local treatment of the primary lesion. Enlargement caused by infection will usually show signs of subsiding with antibiotic treatment. For palpable nodes, ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration will confirm the diagnosis and a block dissection of both groins should be undertaken."B&L 85 · p.1553
Note: both groinsNot the side of the lesion. The penile lymphatics cross the midline, so a positive node on one side mandates a bilateral dissection.
SRB's sequence, with its own vocabulary
"When lymph nodes are involved and are mobile, bilateral ilioinguinal nodal dissection is done." And the terms an examiner may ask for: "primary inguinal block is doing block dissection within 4 weeks of surgery for primary tumour. Secondary inguinal block is doing block dissection after 4 weeks of surgery for primary disease. Only 50% of palpable inguinal nodes are involved by metastatic spread. So often a course of antibiotic is given and waited for 4 to 6 weeks."SRB 26 · p.1054
When to dissect an impalpable groin: prophylactic dissection
SRB gives the risk threshold: "if primary tumour is poorly differentiated, and if tumour is T2 or above, chances of inguinal nodal spread is more than 50% and so a prophylactic inguinal nodal dissection is done."SRB 26 · p.1055 Its high-risk group is "invasive carcinoma; T2 and T3 tumours; with vascular invasion".SRB 26 · p.1055 And the terms: "therapeutic inguinal block is done whenever FNAC of node shows positive tumour."SRB 26 · p.1055
SRB's staged alternative, which is elegant"Superficial lymph node block is dissection superficial to fascia lata in N0 disease. It should undergo frozen section biopsy. If nodes are positive, then formal block dissection should be done."SRB 26 · p.1055 A limited dissection above the fascia lata, read on the table, deciding whether to go deeper. It is the same logic as sentinel node biopsy with a wider net.
The named dissections
Standard inguinal lymphadenectomy is "classical block dissection". Modified inguinal block dissection (Catalona) uses a "small incision, limited dissection, preservation of saphenous vein".SRB 26 · p.1055 SRB illustrates an S-shaped incision for ilioinguinal block dissection.SRB 26 · p.1055
Why preserve the saphenous veinBecause taking it worsens the lower-limb lymphoedema that follows a groin dissection. Catalona's modification exists to reduce exactly the complications listed next.
Complications of ilioinguinal block dissection, which you must be able to list
SRB, twice: "flap necrosis, lymphoedema of lower limb, femoral blow out, infection, lymphorrhoea and haemorrhage", with flap necrosis marked as common.SRB 26 · pp.1054, 1055
Say "flap necrosis" firstIt is the commonest, and naming it first shows you know which complication actually happens. Femoral blow-out is the one that kills; it follows skin loss over an exposed, irradiated or dissected femoral vessel.
The pelvic nodes
SRB: "removal of iliac nodes does not alter the outcome. It is done to confirm the spread, so that further therapy can be planned and prognosis can be predicted."SRB 26 · p.1055 B&L: "management of the pelvic nodes is controversial. When they are involved on CT scanning, surgery probably has little role; however, when the iliac nodes are not enlarged in the presence of N2 disease, the options are observation, pelvic lymphadenectomy or radiotherapy."B&L 85 · p.1553
The books agree herePelvic dissection is staging, not therapy. Say that and you have said what both books say.
How long do you wait before operating on the groin: three weeks or six? A live disagreement about timing, with no disagreement about the reason. B&L 85 p.1553 says at least 3 weeks; SRB 26 p.1054 says 4 to 6
Bailey & Love 28e
"Treatment of any associated enlarged inguinal lymph nodes should be delayed until at least 3 weeks after local treatment of the primary lesion. Enlargement caused by infection will usually show signs of subsiding with antibiotic treatment."B&L 85 · p.1553 Note the wording: at least three weeks, and after local treatment of the primary.
SRB
"Often a course of antibiotic is given and waited for 4 to 6 weeks",SRB 26 · p.1054 and again "a trial of antibiotic therapy is given for 4 to 6 weeks to reduce the size of the inguinal node."SRB 26 · p.1055 SRB also gives names to the two timings: a primary inguinal block is done within 4 weeks of the primary surgery, a secondary inguinal block after 4 weeks.SRB 26 · p.1054
They agree completely on why
Both give the same reason and the same figure: only about half of palpable inguinal nodes are metastatic, so an interval of antibiotics and healing lets the reactive nodes settle and identifies the ones that are truly involved. B&L's "at least 3 weeks" and SRB's "4 to 6 weeks" are both within the same clinical window, and B&L's phrasing admits longer. There is no real conflict about management, only about the number a candidate is expected to say.
How to answer: give the reason first and the number as a range, which is true in both books. "I would treat the primary, give a course of antibiotics, and reassess the groins after an interval of about four to six weeks, at least three weeks at a minimum. The reason is that only about half of palpable inguinal nodes are metastatic; the rest are reactive to the infected primary and will subside. Nodes that persist are then assessed by ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration, and if positive I would do a bilateral ilioinguinal block dissection, because the penile lymphatics cross the midline. Where the groins are impalpable but the primary is high risk, T2 or above, poorly differentiated or with lymphovascular invasion, I would do a sentinel node biopsy or a superficial dissection above the fascia lata with frozen section, and proceed to formal block dissection if positive." If the examiner wants one number, say four to six weeks with SRB, since that is the Indian teaching, and know primary and secondary inguinal block as the terms for before and after four weeks.
Young's operation and the Sir Piersey Gold operation: eponyms SRB teaches and B&L does not have, one of them with a rationale worth questioning An asymmetry rather than a contradiction. SRB 26 p.1054 names both; neither appears anywhere in B&L 85
SRB
Young's operation: "partial amputation of penis with bilateral ilioinguinal lymph node block dissection is called as Young's operation."SRB 26 · p.1054
Sir Piersey Gold operation: "total scrotectomy with orchidectomy is done along with total amputation of the penis, Sir Piersey Gold operation. It prevents frequent dermatitis of the scrotal skin because of the perineal urethrostomy and also reduces the sexual desire."SRB 26 · p.1054
Bailey & Love 28e
Neither eponym appears. B&L describes the same anatomy in plain terms: total penectomy with perineal urethrostomy for advanced disease,B&L 85 · p.1553 and block dissection of both groins for proven nodal disease.B&L 85 · p.1553 It describes no scrotectomy or orchidectomy as part of the operation for penile cancer.
The part worth thinking about
The first of SRB's two stated reasons for scrotectomy with orchidectomy is a real surgical problem: ammoniacal dermatitis of the scrotal skin from a perineal urethrostomy. But SRB itself gives a simpler remedy for that two sentences earlier, namely that the patient voids sitting with the scrotum lifted.SRB 26 · p.1054 The second stated reason, that the operation "reduces the sexual desire", is not a cancer indication at all; removing both testes from a man with a locoregional squamous carcinoma does nothing to his tumour. That is why no corresponding operation appears in B&L 28e, which treats total penectomy and perineal urethrostomy as the extent of what is needed.
How to answer: know the eponyms, because an Indian examiner will ask for them by name, and be careful how you present the second one. If asked what Young's operation is, answer directly: "partial amputation of the penis with bilateral ilioinguinal block dissection." If asked about the Sir Piersey Gold operation, describe it accurately, "total amputation of the penis with total scrotectomy and orchidectomy, done to avoid recurrent ammoniacal dermatitis of the scrotal skin from the perineal urethrostomy", and then, if you are asked whether you would do it, say that the dermatitis is usually managed by teaching the patient to void sitting with the scrotum lifted, and that current practice is total penectomy with perineal urethrostomy without routine scrotectomy or orchidectomy. Do not offer reduction of sexual desire as a reason for an operation.

8Buschke-Löwenstein tumour

What it is, and the one thing to remember about it
B&L: "the Buschke-Löwenstein tumour is uncommon. It has the histological pattern of a verrucous carcinoma. It is locally destructive and invasive but appears not to spread to lymph nodes or to metastasise. Treatment is by surgical excision."B&L 85 · p.1553 SRB: "it is verrucous carcinoma of penis (5 to 15% common). It is a curable malignancy. It is locally destructive, locally invasive. It is often large, exophytic, dry, verrucae-like growth. Neither spreads through lymphatics nor blood."SRB 26 · p.1055
The teaching point in one lineA huge, destructive, frightening-looking penile tumour that does not metastasise. So it needs local excision, not a groin dissection, and the prognosis is good.
Aetiology and treatment
SRB: "virus aetiology is proposed, HPV-6 and 11", which is the wart pair rather than the cancer pair. "After biopsy and confirmation, surgical excision or partial amputation is the treatment of choice. Systemic interferon alpha therapy and Nd:YAG laser therapy is successful."SRB 26 · p.1055
And SRB's prohibition, which B&L does not state "Radiotherapy should not be given."SRB 26 · p.1055 Verrucous carcinoma is classically held to undergo anaplastic transformation after irradiation. Volunteering that rule is worth a mark, and the two books do not conflict on it: B&L simply says excision and does not mention radiotherapy at all.
And one other penile malignancy worth a sentence
Malignant melanoma of the penis. B&L: "this is an uncommon tumour with the principles of management being the same as for squamous cell carcinoma. Blood-borne metastatic disease is, however, more common."B&L 85 · p.1553

9Rapid-fire viva

What is the commonest histological type, and where does it arise?

Squamous cell carcinoma, arising in the skin of the glans penis or the prepuce; the glans is the commonest site.B&L 85 · p.1552SRB 26 · p.1053 S Das adds that rarely a columnar carcinoma arises from Tyson's glands, which lie on either side of the frenum.Das 40 · p.638

What is the single most important aetiological factor?

Lack of neonatal circumcision. Circumcision soon after birth confers near-total immunity, and circumcision later does not confer the same benefit, which supports the view that retained smegma under a non-retractile prepuce is the carcinogenic stimulus.B&L 85 · p.1551SRB 26 · p.1052

Which HPV types?

Types 16 and 18 are the risk factors for carcinoma.B&L 85 · p.1551 Types 6 and 11 cause genital wartsB&L 85 · p.1546 and are the proposed cause of the Buschke-Löwenstein tumour.SRB 26 · p.1055

Name the premalignant conditions.

Leukoplakia of the glans; penile intraepithelial neoplasia, which is carcinoma in situ; lichen sclerosus, also called balanitis xerotica obliterans; long-standing genital warts or condylomata acuminata; Paget's disease of the penis; erythroplasia of Queyrat; and chronic balanoposthitis with phimosis.B&L 85 · p.1551SRB 26 · p.1052Das 40 · p.638

Erythroplasia of Queyrat or Bowen's disease, which is which?

Both are penile intraepithelial neoplasia, carcinoma in situ, and the name is decided by the site: on the glans it is erythroplasia of Queyrat, on the shaft it is Bowen's disease.B&L 85 · p.1552 Both look like a red cutaneous patch, and since there are benign causes of red patches on the penis, a biopsy is indicated when there is doubt.

An adult presents with phimosis of recent onset. What do you think?

Carcinoma of the penis growing under the prepuce.SRB 26 · p.1053 A long-standing tight prepuce is phimosis; a recently tight one in a man over forty is a tumour. He needs the prepuce retracted or, if that is impossible, a dorsal slit, so the lesion can be seen and biopsied.Das 40 · p.638B&L 85 · p.1552

Why is this cancer so often diagnosed late?

Because it is painless, because patients are embarrassed, and because the purulent preputial discharge is misdiagnosed and treated as balanoposthitis.B&L 85 · p.1552Das 40 · p.638 The lesson is that you cannot diagnose what you have not seen: the prepuce must be retracted or split.

A man with a penile carcinoma has large inguinal nodes. Are they malignant?

About half are. All three books give the same figure: roughly 50% of palpable inguinal nodes in this disease are reactive to infection of the primary rather than metastatic. So you treat the primary, give antibiotics, wait four to six weeks, and reassess; persistent nodes are sampled by ultrasound-guided FNAC.B&L 85 · p.1552, 1553Das 40 · p.638SRB 26 · p.1054

If you dissect the groin, which side?

Both. The lymphatics of the penis cross the midline, so a block dissection is bilateral and the nodes on either side may be involved.B&L 85 · p.1553SRB 26 · p.1053

What is the sentinel node in carcinoma of the penis?

Cabana's node, which lies above and medial to the junction of the saphenous and femoral veins. It is the first node involved, and sentinel node biopsy after isosulphan blue dye injection into the primary is used to decide whether a block dissection is needed.SRB 26 · p.1053 Bailey and Love uses sentinel node biopsy for exactly this, in the patient whose nodes are not palpable.B&L 85 · p.1553

Would you do an open biopsy of an inguinal node?

No. SRB states it as a rule: FNAC of lymph nodes, no open biopsy.SRB 26 · p.1053 An incision contaminates the field and compromises the skin flaps on which a later block dissection depends, in a groin where flap necrosis is already the commonest complication.

Stage this tumour on Jackson's system, and give the survival.

Stage I, glans or prepuce only, 90% five-year survival. Stage II, extending into the body of the penis, 70%. Stage III, with mobile inguinal nodes, 50%. Stage IV, invading adjacent structures or with fixed nodes, 5%.SRB 26 · p.1053 The message is that nodal status, and whether the nodes are mobile or fixed, dominates the prognosis.

What are the T stages?

On AJCC 8: T1 invades lamina propria or dartos, subdivided into T1a without lymphovascular or perineural invasion and not high grade, and T1b with invasion or high grade; T2 invades corpus spongiosum; T3 invades corpora cavernosa; T4 invades adjacent structures such as prostate, scrotum or pubic bone.SRB 26 · p.1054 Bailey and Love's prose version puts both erectile bodies in T2 and makes urethral invasion T3.B&L 85 · p.1552

Treatment of carcinoma in situ of the penis.

Topical 5-fluorouracil cream, laser ablation with CO2 or Nd:YAG, or limited surgical excision; SRB adds imiquimab and Mohs' surgery.B&L 85 · p.1552SRB 26 · p.1055 Radiotherapy is also an option in SRB.

How much penis do you remove?

It is decided by the proximal extent of the tumour, not by a fixed measurement. If the growth is confined to the glans without involving the proximal shaft, a partial amputation; SRB teaches 2 cm clearance from the proximal extent with a 2.5 cm stump retained so the patient can direct his stream. If the proximal body is involved or the tumour is poorly differentiated, total amputation with perineal urethrostomy.SRB 26 · p.1054 Bailey and Love notes the traditional 2 cm margin has been superseded by penis-preserving surgery with much smaller margins, with glansectomy for glans tumours and Mohs' surgery or laser for small low-grade lesions.B&L 85 · p.1553

What is Mohs' surgery?

Sequential tissue excision under repeat microscopic control, which identifies the tumour margin accurately and maximally preserves uninvolved tissue. It is used for small, low-grade, low-stage penile tumours and for carcinoma in situ.B&L 85 · p.1553

What is Young's operation?

Partial amputation of the penis with bilateral ilioinguinal lymph node block dissection.SRB 26 · p.1054

Complications of a perineal urethrostomy, and how do you prevent the commoner one?

Scrotal ammoniacal dermatitis, and stricture at the urethrostomy site. The dermatitis is prevented by teaching the patient to void sitting down with the scrotum lifted upwards; the stricture needs dilatation.SRB 26 · p.1054

Complications of ilioinguinal block dissection.

Flap necrosis, which is the commonest; lymphoedema of the lower limb; lymphorrhoea; infection; haemorrhage; and femoral blow-out, which is the one that kills.SRB 26 · pp.1054, 1055 Catalona's modified dissection uses a smaller incision, a limited field and preservation of the saphenous vein specifically to reduce these.

Is there any point in removing the iliac nodes?

For staging rather than cure. SRB: removal of iliac nodes does not alter the outcome and is done to confirm spread so that further therapy and prognosis can be planned.SRB 26 · p.1055 Bailey and Love calls the management of pelvic nodes controversial: where they are involved on CT, surgery probably has little role; where they are not enlarged in the presence of N2 disease, the options are observation, pelvic lymphadenectomy or radiotherapy.B&L 85 · p.1553

How does a patient with this disease die?

From erosion of the femoral or external iliac vessels by metastatic inguinal nodes that have ulcerated through the skin of the groin, causing torrential haemorrhage.B&L 85 · p.1552Das 40 · p.638SRB 26 · p.1053 That is also the reason palliative radiotherapy is given to fixed inguinal nodes, to pre-empt the bleed as well as to relieve pain and fungation.SRB 26 · p.1055

Is there a role for chemotherapy?

A limited one. Bailey and Love: chemotherapy is relatively ineffective and currently reserved for palliation in metastatic disease.B&L 85 · p.1553 SRB gives 5-FU, methotrexate, bleomycin, cisplatin and vincristine in combination, and notes bleomycin is a radiosensitiser so it helps if radiotherapy is planned.SRB 26 · p.1055 Surgery is the mainstay.

A huge warty destructive penile growth with no palpable nodes. What might it be?

A Buschke-Löwenstein tumour, which is a verrucous carcinoma. It is locally destructive and invasive but does not spread to lymph nodes or metastasise.B&L 85 · p.1553SRB 26 · p.1055 Treatment is surgical excision or partial amputation after biopsy, with interferon alpha and Nd:YAG laser also successful, and SRB adds that radiotherapy should not be given.SRB 26 · p.1055

What is Dreslers quadrangle?

The area a groin block dissection for carcinoma of the penis must clear. Its upper border is the line joining the anterior superior iliac spine to the pubic tubercle; laterally, a line from the anterior superior iliac spine to a point 20 cm below it; medially, from the pubic tubercle to a point 15 cm below it.SRB 26 · p.1055
